Subtopic 3.1

Sequence Limits and Monotone Convergence

Does getting endlessly closer mean meeting every requested error tolerance?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • A sequence is a function assigning a value to each integer index. Here n=1,2,… and a_n∈ℝ, with a_1 as the first term.
  • |a_n−L| is the distance from the real number L. The number ε>0 is a requested error tolerance; N is an index beyond which every term must be controlled.
  • Nondecreasing means a_{n+1}≥a_n; nonincreasing reverses the inequality. An upper bound M satisfies a_n≤M for every relevant term and need not itself be a term.

Learning goals

  • Explain convergence in the order ∀ε∃N∀n and choose an actual N.
  • Check monotonicity and boundedness in the appropriate direction before concluding convergence.

A limit controls all sufficiently large indices

a_n→L∈ℝ means that for every ε>0 there is an integer N≥1 such that |a_n−L|<ε for every n≥N. Different ε may require different N, but for a fixed ε every term beyond the chosen index must meet the condition. Observing a finite table is different from proving this statement.

The index n does not vary continuously like a real variable. If f(x)→L and a_n=f(n), the sequence also tends to L, but the converse generally fails. Although sin(2πn)=0 at integer indices, sin(2πx) has no limit as the real variable x tends to infinity.

Find N from an error expression

Since a_n=(3n+1)/(n+2)=3−5/(n+2), its distance from 3 is 5/(n+2). Choosing N=max(1,⌊5/ε⌋+1) makes n>5/ε for n≥N, so the distance is less than ε. The floor ⌊z⌋ is the greatest integer at most z.

Changing finitely many initial values does not change a limit if the error expression for all sufficiently large indices stays the same. Adding the same constant to every term, however, shifts the limit by that constant. Distinguish a few initial terms from the whole sequence.

Monotone convergence also needs a bound

A nondecreasing real sequence that is bounded above converges to a finite limit. Likewise, a nonincreasing sequence bounded below converges. It is enough for a tail to be monotone, because finitely many initial terms do not affect convergence. Monotonicity alone is insufficient: the sequence n grows without bound and has no finite limit.

Boundedness alone is also insufficient. The sequence (−1)^n stays between −1 and 1 but alternates and does not converge. In contrast, (−1)^n/n is not monotone but tends to 0 because its absolute value is 1/n→0. Monotone boundedness is sufficient, not necessary for every convergent sequence.

A worked example, step by step

For a_n=(3n+1)/(n+2), n≥1, establish monotonicity, an upper bound, and the limit. Find a starting index N that guarantees error <1/100.

  1. Rewrite a_n=3−5/(n+2), giving a_n<3 and a_1=4/3.
  2. a_{n+1}−a_n=5/[(n+2)(n+3)]>0. Thus the sequence is nondecreasing, with upper bound 3.
  3. Monotone boundedness gives convergence. The direct error 5/(n+2)→0, verified by the ε–N definition, identifies the limit as 3.
  4. For ε=1/100, the inequality 5/(n+2)<1/100 is equivalent to n>498. Thus N=499 works; at n=499 the error is 5/501<1/100.

The sequence increases, is bounded above, and tends to 3. Error <1/100 is guaranteed from N=499 onward, rather than inferred from a finite decimal table.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Give an ε–N choice proving b_n=(−1)^n/n→0. Write the first three terms of c_1=0, c_{n+1}=(c_n+3)/2, and verify c_n=3(1−2^{−(n−1)}).

Show hint

For b, the distance is 1/n. For c, substitute n=1 into the proposed expression and then check the recurrence.

Show solution

For b, N=max(1,⌊1/ε⌋+1) gives 1/n<ε for every n≥N. The first three c terms are 0,3/2,9/4. The expression gives c_1=0, and [3(1−2^{−(n−1)})+3]/2=3(1−2^{−n}), so it also satisfies the recurrence. The error 3·2^{−(n−1)} tends to 0, hence c_n→3.

Misconception and counterexample

Every bounded sequence converges, and its limit is the last term.

The bounded sequence (−1)^n does not converge because its two subsequences have different values ±1. An infinite sequence has no last integer index, and a limit need not be attained by a term.

Check your understanding

May N depend on ε in the statement a_n→L?

Show answer and reasoning

Yes.

The quantifier ∃N follows ∀ε>0. After choosing N, every subsequent term must be controlled.

Does increasing monotonicity alone imply a finite limit?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

a_n=n increases but is not bounded above.

Can a nonmonotone sequence converge?

Show answer and reasoning

Yes.

For (−1)^n/n, the distance from 0 is 1/n→0.

Explore further

Extension: The term set A of a nondecreasing real sequence bounded above is nonempty and has an upper bound. Its supremum sup A is the least of all upper bounds. Completeness of ℝ states that every nonempty real set bounded above has such a least upper bound; therefore L=sup A exists. For any ε>0, L−ε cannot be an upper bound, so some index N satisfies a_N>L−ε. For n≥N, monotonicity gives L−ε<a_N≤a_n≤L and hence |a_n−L|<ε. This proves monotone convergence completely. Apply it to −a_n for the nonincreasing case. The same completeness cannot be assumed for the rational numbers alone: an increasing sequence of rational approximations to √2 from below has an irrational real limit. Specify the number system in which convergence is asserted.

Subtopic 3.2

Series and Partial Sums

If each term is almost zero, must an endless sum be finite?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Here a_n is a real term for n≥1. S_N=∑_{n=1}^N a_n is the finite sum of N terms, and S_0=0.
  • Convergence of ∑a_n means that the partial-sum sequence S_N has a finite real limit. This is a different sequence from the terms a_n.
  • Infinity is not a finite real sum. If S_N→+∞, the series diverges; this differs from divergence through oscillating partial sums.

Learning goals

  • Define a series sum through explicit partial sums.
  • Explain why terms tending to zero are necessary but insufficient, and prove divergence of the harmonic series.

Define endless addition by a partial-sum limit

∑_{n=1}^∞a_n=S means S_N→S∈ℝ. It does not mean that continued calculator additions eventually stop, or that we directly substitute an infinity-th term. The precise condition is ∀ε>0∃N₀∀N≥N₀:|S_N−S|<ε.

For a_n=2^{−n}, the finite sum is S_N=1−2^{−N}. At N=1,2,3 it gives 1/2,3/4,7/8. The limit 1 is proved from the general partial-sum formula and the error 2^{−N}→0, not merely observed in these three values.

Small terms are necessary, not sufficient

If a series converges, then a_N=S_N−S_{N−1}→S−S=0. Thus a series diverges when its terms do not tend to zero. When a_n→0, however, this test alone gives no conclusion. Do not reverse a necessary condition into a sufficient one.

The terms a_n=1/n tend to 0, but the harmonic series diverges. The block k=2^{j−1}+1,…,2^j contains 2^{j−1} terms, each at least 1/2^j, so its sum is at least 1/2. Each new block adds at least this amount, leaving the partial sums without an upper bound.

Preserve the order and the initial terms

Adding, removing, or changing finitely many terms does not change convergence, but it changes a convergent sum by the corresponding finite amount. A sum starting at n=2 differs from one starting at n=1 by the first term; ignoring the starting index changes the answer.

The partial-sum definition uses the specified term order. The partial sums of 1−1+1−1+… are 1,0,1,0,… and do not converge. Grouping consecutive pairs and writing every sum as 0 retains only even-index partial sums and misses the original convergence condition.

A worked example, step by step

Find the partial sums and sum of ∑_{n=1}^∞2^{−n}. Give the partial sum and remainder at N=4.

  1. S_N=1/2+1/4+…+2^{−N}. Subtracting (1/2)S_N gives (1/2)S_N=1/2−2^{−(N+1)}.
  2. Hence S_N=1−2^{−N}. This calculation is performed for finite N.
  3. Since 2^{−N}→0, the partial-sum limit is 1. The actual remainder is R_N=1−S_N=2^{−N}.
  4. At N=4, S_4=15/16 and R_4=1/16. The term a_4=1/16 happens to equal the remainder in this example, but their definitions differ.

The sum is 1; the N=4 partial sum is 15/16 and the remainder is 1/16.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Find the first four partial sums for a_n=2(−1)^{n−1} and decide convergence. Use blocks to explain why the harmonic partial sums satisfy S_{2^k}≥1+k/2.

Show hint

Write the terms and the partial sums on separate lines. After the first harmonic term 1, there are k blocks with lengths given by powers of two.

Show solution

The terms are 2,−2,2,−2 and the partial sums are 2,0,2,0. Their odd and even subsequences differ, so the series diverges. For the harmonic series, the first term is 1 and each block j=1,…,k contributes at least 1/2; thus S_{2^k}≥1+k/2. As k grows without bound, the partial sums cannot be bounded above.

Misconception and counterexample

Checking a_n→0 proves convergence of the series.

The harmonic terms 1/n tend to 0, but the block lower bound above proves unbounded growth of the partial sums. A term limit cannot replace a sum limit.

Check your understanding

Is the sum of a series the limit of a_N?

Show answer and reasoning

No. It is the limit of S_N.

Terms and accumulated sums are different sequences.

What happens if a_n does not tend to 0?

Show answer and reasoning

The series diverges.

Convergence would require a_N=S_N−S_{N−1}→0.

Does removing the first term preserve both convergence and the sum?

Show answer and reasoning

Only convergence is preserved.

When the series converges, the sum decreases by the removed term. A divergent series has no finite real sum of the kind discussed here.

Explore further

Extension: If ∑a_n converges, every sufficiently late finite tail sum is uniformly small. Given S_N→S and ε>0, choose K so that |S_j−S|<ε/2 beyond K. For m≥n≥K+1, |∑_{k=n}^m a_k|=|S_m−S_{n−1}|≤|S_m−S|+|S_{n−1}−S|<ε. Conversely, if this tail condition holds for every ε, the partial sums form a Cauchy sequence: for every ε>0 there is an integer K such that |S_p−S_q|<ε for all p,q≥K. Indeed, for p>q≥K, use tail start n=q+1 and end m=p; for p=q the difference is 0, and for p<q exchange the indices. Completeness of ℝ then gives convergence. This condition controls all sufficiently late tail sums and is stronger than requiring each individual term to be small. The converse uses the theorem of Cauchy completeness of ℝ; a finite table does not prove that theorem.

Subtopic 3.3

Geometric and Telescoping Series

When middle terms cancel, do both ends of an infinite sum disappear too?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • For real c,r∈ℝ, the geometric term c r^{n−1} has first term c, with indices starting at n=1. At r=0 interpret the term sequence as c,0,0,… .
  • Simplify a finite sum first, then take N→∞. Subtracting two separately divergent infinite sums is not an available operation.
  • A telescoping sum adds adjacent differences such as b_n−b_{n+1}, so middle terms cancel in finite partial sums. Investigate the limit of the final term that remains.

Learning goals

  • Apply geometric convergence conditions, including the starting index and the exception c=0.
  • Expand finite cancellations while retaining final terms and exact remainders.

The geometric formula comes from a finite identity

For S_N=c+cr+…+cr^{N−1}, subtracting rS_N gives (1−r)S_N=c(1−r^N). If r≠1, S_N=c(1−r^N)/(1−r); if r=1, S_N=Nc. This is algebra of finite sums and does not assume convergence of an infinite sum in advance.

If c≠0 and |r|<1, then r^N→0 and the sum is c/(1−r). If |r|≥1, the terms do not tend to 0 and the series diverges. However, when c=0 all terms vanish, so the series converges regardless of r. Keep this exception explicit.

Write the actual first and final terms

The first term of ∑_{n=3}^∞4/3^n is 4/27 and its ratio is 1/3. Mistaking it for the n=1 first term 4/3 makes the sum nine times too large. An index shift is a finite algebraic change that must preserve the actual term sequence.

Summing b_n−b_{n+1} from n=1 to N gives b_1−b_{N+1}. If b_{N+1}→B∈ℝ, the sum is b_1−B; B is not necessarily 0. For example, b_n=2+1/n retains the limit B=2.

A two-step gap leaves two terms at each end

Since 2/[n(n+2)]=1/n−1/(n+2), summing from n=1 to N gives 1+1/2−1/(N+1)−1/(N+2). Even at N=1, the expression is 3/2−1/2−1/3=2/3, agreeing with the first term.

A telescoping shape does not by itself imply convergence. With b_n=n, each difference b_n−b_{n+1}=−1, so the partial sum is −N→−∞. Compute finite end terms and their limits instead of saying that infinitely many terms all disappear.

A worked example, step by step

Find the sums and Nth partial sums of A=∑_{n=1}^∞3(−1/2)^{n−1} and B=∑_{n=1}^∞2/[n(n+2)].

  1. For A, c=3 and r=−1/2. The finite partial sum is A_N=3(1−(−1/2)^N)/(1+1/2)=2(1−(−1/2)^N).
  2. Since |r|<1, A=2. Its signed remainder is A−A_N=2(−1/2)^N, with absolute value 2^{1−N}.
  3. For B, write each term as 1/n−1/(n+2), obtaining B_N=3/2−1/(N+1)−1/(N+2).
  4. The final two reciprocals tend to 0, hence B=3/2. Preserve the positive remainder 1/(N+1)+1/(N+2), rather than deleting it.

A=2 and B=3/2. Their partial sums and remainders are given by the finite formulas above.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Find ∑_{n=3}^∞4/3^n. Also find the smallest integer N≥1 guaranteeing that the absolute remainder of A is <1/16.

Show hint

Apply the geometric formula to first term 4/27. Check the strict inequality 2^{1−N}<2^{−4}.

Show solution

The sum is (4/27)/(1−1/3)=2/9. The inequality 2^{1−N}<1/16 requires N>5, so the smallest index is N=6, with error 1/32. At N=5 the error is exactly 1/16 and fails the strict condition.

Misconception and counterexample

Every series with middle-term cancellation converges and has zero tail error.

For adjacent differences with b_n=n, the partial sum −N diverges. Even the convergent series B retains a positive remainder consisting of two reciprocals at finite N.

Check your understanding

Does the geometric series with c=0,r=2 diverge?

Show answer and reasoning

No. It converges to 0.

All terms are zero, which is an explicit exception.

Do the partial sums converge for r=−1,c=3?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

They alternate 3,0,3,0,…, and the terms do not tend to 0.

May we simply replace the last term in b_1−b_{N+1} by 0?

Show answer and reasoning

Its actual limit must be checked.

If b_n→2, subtract 2; if b_n=n, the partial sums diverge.

Explore further

Extension: Prove directly that q^N→0 for 0<q<1. Write q^{−1}=1+d with d>0. For integers N≥1, (1+d)^N≥1+Nd: equality holds at N=1, and the induction step multiplies by 1+d and adds the nonnegative term Nd². Hence 0≤q^N≤1/(1+Nd). Given ε>0, choose N large enough to make the right side less than ε, proving the limit 0. If r=0, the terms vanish from the second onward; if −1<r<0, apply the same argument to |r^N|=|r|^N. This justifies geometric partial-sum limits regardless of sign. Beginning with a finite identity prevents invalid subtraction of divergent infinite sums.

Subtopic 3.4

Integral and Comparison Tests

In which direction does comparing a smaller series with a larger one support a conclusion?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Tail partial sums of a positive or nonnegative series are nondecreasing. Nonnegative terms mean each a_n≥0, unlike an alternating-sign series.
  • The improper integral ∫_K^∞f is the finite real limit of ∫_K^T f as T→∞. Do not assume that an area equals a term sum. Here ln denotes the natural logarithm and ln x is defined for x>0.
  • A function f is nonincreasing if x≤y implies f(x)≥f(y). In differentiable examples, f′≤0 can verify this condition.

Learning goals

  • Specify the inequality direction and nonnegative-term assumptions in direct comparison.
  • Explain the conditions for the integral test, p-series, remainder bounds, and limit comparison with a finite positive limit.

Compare the sizes of nonnegative partial sums

If 0≤a_n≤b_n beyond an integer K, convergence of ∑b_n implies convergence of ∑a_n. Conversely, divergence of ∑a_n implies divergence of the larger ∑b_n. The useful direction is to compare with a divergent lower bound or a convergent upper bound.

Divergence of the larger-term series does not force divergence of the smaller one. Although 0<1/n²≤1/n, the first series converges and the second diverges. Even if finitely many initial terms are negative, a nonnegative-tail comparison can determine convergence, but every term must be a defined finite real number.

The integral test needs a continuous, positive, nonincreasing tail

If f is continuous, positive, and nonincreasing on [K,∞) for an integer K≥1 and f(n)=a_n, then ∑_{n=K}^∞a_n and ∫_K^∞f converge or diverge together. This does not assert equality of their values. On each interval of width 1, compare f with its endpoint values.

For f=x^{−p},x≥1, the conditions hold when p>0. The integral is (T^{1−p}−1)/(1−p) for p≠1, and ln T for p=1. Its limit as T→∞ is finite exactly when p>1, so ∑1/n^p converges then. For p≤0, the terms do not tend to 0, giving divergence separately.

Preserve conditions in limit comparison and remainder estimates

If a_n,b_n>0 on a tail and a_n/b_n→L with 0<L<∞, then eventually (L/2)b_n≤a_n≤(3L/2)b_n, so both series have the same convergence behavior. If L=0, divergence of the b-series alone does not determine the a-series; likewise, at L=∞, convergence of the b-series alone does not determine the a-series.

Under the convergent integral-test conditions, for an integer N≥K the remainder R_N=∑_{n=N+1}^∞a_n satisfies ∫_{N+1}^∞f≤R_N≤∫_N^∞f. Use weak inequalities under the nonincreasing assumption. For f=1/x², this gives 1/(N+1)≤R_N≤1/N, bounding partial-sum error rather than determining the exact total sum.

A worked example, step by step

For n≥1, determine convergence of ∑1/(n²+n) and ∑1/(3n+2) by direct comparison.

  1. Both terms are positive and finite for n≥1. Comparing denominators gives 1/(n²+n)≤1/n².
  2. The p=2 series ∑1/n² converges by the integral test since ∫_1^∞x^{−2}=1. Therefore the smaller first series also converges.
  3. Since 3n+2≤5n, taking reciprocals reverses the inequality: 1/(3n+2)≥1/(5n)>0.
  4. The series ∑1/(5n) is a positive constant multiple of the harmonic series and diverges. Therefore the larger second series diverges.

The first series converges and the second diverges. Direct comparison does not compute the exact values of these sums.

Misconception and counterexample

Smaller terms allow us to conclude divergence by comparing with any divergent series.

The example 1/n²≤1/n has a smaller convergent series and a larger divergent one. To prove convergence use a convergent upper bound; to prove divergence use a divergent lower bound.

Show answer and reasoning

No.

The first term is already 1 and further positive terms are added. The test compares convergence, not equal values.

If a_n/b_n→0 and ∑b_n diverges, must ∑a_n diverge?

Show answer and reasoning

The conclusion is undetermined.

With b_n=1/n, the choice a_n=1/n² gives convergence. But for n≥2, a_n=1/(n ln n) has ratio 1/ln n→0 and still diverges. The function f(x)=1/(x ln x) is continuous and positive on [2,∞), with f′(x)=−(ln x+1)/(x²(ln x)²)<0. Its integral ∫_2^T f(x)dx=ln ln T−ln ln 2→∞, so all integral-test conditions hold and give divergence.

Explore further

Extension: Prove both directions of the integral test. Nonincrease gives ∫_n^{n+1}f≤f(n) for n≥K, and f(n)≤∫_{n−1}^n f for n≥K+1. Therefore ∫_K^{N+1}f≤∑_{n=K}^N f(n)≤f(K)+∫_K^N f. If the integral is finite, the right side bounds the nondecreasing partial sums above, so real monotone convergence gives convergence. If the series converges, the integer-endpoint integrals on the left are bounded. Every real endpoint T lies below some integer endpoint, so the whole positive integral is bounded and nondecreasing and has a finite limit. Conversely, if the integral diverges, the left lower bound forces unbounded partial-sum growth. Apply the same comparison to the tail after N and send its endpoint to infinity to obtain both remainder bounds. Continuity is a sufficient condition ensuring integrability on each finite interval; finitely many initial terms are added separately.
