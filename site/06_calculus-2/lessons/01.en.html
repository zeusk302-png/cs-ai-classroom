Subtopic 1.1

Integration by parts and repeated application

If we integrate x and eˣ separately and multiply the results, do we obtain an integral of x eˣ?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Function review: an input x determines an output f(x). The derivative f′(x) is the limit of the ratio of output change to a small input change.
  • Antiderivatives: if F′=f, then F is an antiderivative of f. On a connected interval, every antiderivative has the form F+C.
  • Basic rules: (x²)′=2x, (eˣ)′=eˣ, and (uv)′=u′v+uv′. For continuous f, a definite integral measures signed accumulation, with ∫ₐᵇf=F(b)−F(a).

Learning goals

  • Derive integration by parts from the product rule.
  • Choose u and dv by explaining why the new integral becomes easier.
  • Compute repeated integration by parts and definite-integral boundary terms, then check by differentiation.

Derive a rule for integrating a product

The product rule is (uv)′=u′v+uv′. Integrate both sides on the same interval and rearrange to obtain ∫uv′dx=uv−∫u′vdx+C. Writing du=u′dx and dv=v′dx gives the shorter form ∫u dv=uv−∫v du. The whole expression dv includes dx, and v is an antiderivative of v′.

This unit uses the sufficient condition that u and v have continuous derivatives on the interval. For definite integrals, ∫ₐᵇuv′dx=[uv]ₐᵇ−∫ₐᵇu′vdx. An indefinite-integral answer needs the constant C; a definite-integral answer needs the difference of boundary values.

What the choice achieves matters

For x eˣ, choosing u=x and dv=eˣdx gives du=dx and v=eˣ, making the new integral ∫eˣdx easier. Choosing u=eˣ and dv=x dx instead introduces x²eˣ into the new integral, which is usually harder. Both choices obey the formula, but they are not equally useful strategies.

Differentiation reduces a polynomial’s degree. In products with exponential or trigonometric functions, choosing the polynomial as u is therefore often helpful. This is a useful strategy, not a universal rule solving every integral. For example, this strategy alone does not produce an elementary antiderivative of ∫e^(x²)dx.

Repeat the method and check independently

For ∫x²eˣdx, the first integration by parts gives x²eˣ−2∫xeˣdx. A second application reduces the polynomial degree to zero and finishes the calculation. Write the remaining integral on each line until it is evaluated, to keep track of signs and coefficients.

Check the answer by differentiating it to recover the original integrand. This is stronger than an expression looking plausible or agreeing at a few points. For an expression with singularities, also state the connected interval on which differentiation and integration are possible.

A worked example, step by step

For real x, find ∫x²eˣdx and ∫₀¹x²eˣdx.

  1. Set u=x²,dv=eˣdx to obtain I=x²eˣ−2∫xeˣdx.
  2. For J=∫xeˣdx, set u=x,dv=eˣdx, giving J=xeˣ−eˣ+C.
  3. Therefore I=eˣ(x²−2x+2)+C.
  4. Differentiating gives eˣ(x²−2x+2)+eˣ(2x−2)=x²eˣ.
  5. Since F(1)=e and F(0)=2, the definite integral is e−2.

Indefinite integral: eˣ(x²−2x+2)+C. Definite integral: e−2. Its positivity agrees with x²eˣ≥0 on the interval.

Try it with paper and the lesson

On paper, compute ∫x e^(2x)dx and differentiate your answer.

Show hint

v=e^(2x)/2. The inner coefficient 2 in the exponent requires the factor 1/2.

Show solution

Choose u=x,dv=e^(2x)dx to get (x/2)e^(2x)−(1/2)∫e^(2x)dx=e^(2x)(x/2−1/4)+C. Its derivative is e^(2x)(x−1/2)+e^(2x)/2=x e^(2x).

Misconception and counterexample

∫fgdx=(∫fdx)(∫gdx).

Take f=g=1. Choosing x as each antiderivative makes the proposed product x·x=x², whose derivative is 2x, not the original integrand 1. The product rule has two terms, so simply multiplying antiderivatives is not an integration rule.

Check your understanding

Where does the negative term in integration by parts come from?

Show answer and reasoning

Move ∫u′v to the other side of the integrated product-rule equation.

It follows by integrating (uv)′=u′v+uv′.

Give one antiderivative of xeˣ.

Show answer and reasoning

eˣ(x−1).

Its derivative is eˣ(x−1)+eˣ=xeˣ.

For a definite integral, is evaluating uv at only one endpoint enough?

Show answer and reasoning

No. [uv]ₐᵇ=(uv)(b)−(uv)(a).

Accumulation requires the difference between the two boundary values.

Explore further

Go further: for Iₙ=∫xⁿeˣdx and integers n≥1, derive Iₙ=xⁿeˣ−nIₙ₋₁. Combine integration constants into one constant at the end. Explain why degree reduction reaches I₀=eˣ after n steps, with the final constant understood.

Subtopic 1.2

Integrals of trigonometric powers

How does viewing sin³x as a product of three sin x factors help us integrate it?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Radians: one full turn is 2π. The derivative formulas (sin x)′=cos x and (cos x)′=−sin x use radian inputs.
  • sin²x means (sin x)², a different function from sin(x²).
  • Substitution review: u=g(x) gives du=g′(x)dx. The formula ∫f(g(x))g′(x)dx=F(g(x))+C reverses the chain rule.

Learning goals

  • For an odd power, reserve one factor that matches the substitution derivative.
  • Use half-angle identities for even powers.
  • Check substitution signs and the units of trigonometric inputs.

Identities turn the problem into polynomial integration

The identity sin²x+cos²x=1 comes from the squared coordinates of a point on the unit circle summing to 1. Rewrite sin³x=(1−cos²x)sin x, then use u=cos x,du=−sin xdx. The reserved sin x is exactly the derivative factor.

For ∫sinᵐx cosⁿx dx with nonnegative integers m,n, an odd m lets you reserve sin x and replace the remaining sin² factors by 1−cos². An odd n lets you reserve cos x and replace the remaining cos² factors by 1−sin². If both are odd, either choice is possible.

When both powers are even

With only even powers, forcing a single reserved factor is not a good fit. From cos 2x=1−2sin²x=2cos²x−1, obtain sin²x=(1−cos 2x)/2 and cos²x=(1+cos 2x)/2. The powers decrease while the angle doubles.

Thus ∫sin²x dx=x/2−sin 2x/4+C. An antiderivative of cos 2x is sin 2x/2; combining that factor with the outer 1/2 gives 1/4. Recording both product coefficients and doubled-angle factors prevents a common factor-of-two error.

Boundaries and checks for definite integrals

If you convert the whole definite integral to u, convert its boundaries to u(a),u(b). In particular, u=cos x decreases from 1 to 0 as x goes from 0 to π/2. Handle both the negative sign in du and the reversed boundary order. Alternatively, find an antiderivative and substitute the original x boundaries.

The odd/even strategy for sin and cos does not carry over unchanged to every power of tan and sec. Those use other identity-and-derivative pairs such as 1+tan²x=sec²x and (tan x)′=sec²x. This section first gives a complete treatment of nonnegative integer powers of sin and cos.

A worked example, step by step

Find ∫sin³x cos²x dx and ∫₀^(π/2)sin³x cos²x dx.

  1. Write sin³x cos²x=(1−cos²x)cos²x sin x.
  2. For u=cos x,du=−sin xdx, obtain −∫(u²−u⁴)du=−u³/3+u⁵/5+C.
  3. An antiderivative is F=−cos³x/3+cos⁵x/5. Differentiating gives cos²x sin x−cos⁴x sin x=sin³x cos²x.
  4. F(π/2)=0 and F(0)=−1/3+1/5=−2/15. The difference is 2/15.

Antiderivative: −cos³x/3+cos⁵x/5+C. Definite integral: 2/15.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Use a half-angle identity to find ∫cos²x dx, then compute the definite integral from 0 to π.

Show hint

cos²x=(1+cos 2x)/2, and sin 0=sin 2π=0.

Show solution

An antiderivative is x/2+sin 2x/4+C. Its derivative is 1/2+cos 2x/2=cos²x. The boundary difference is π/2. The average value is (π/2)/π=1/2.

Misconception and counterexample

An integral of sin²x is sin³x/3.

The derivative of sin³x/3 is sin²x cos x, which has an extra cos x. You cannot directly apply the power rule for x to the inner function sin x without checking the chain-rule factor.

Check your understanding

Which substitution is useful for sin⁵x cos²x?

Show answer and reasoning

u=cos x.

Reserve sin x and rewrite sin⁴x=(1−cos²x)².

What is ∫sin²x dx?

Show answer and reasoning

x/2−sin 2x/4+C.

Use the half-angle identity and integrate cos 2x.

Under u=cos x, what boundaries replace x:0→π/2?

Show answer and reasoning

1→0.

cos 0=1 and cos(π/2)=0, so the original boundaries cannot be left unchanged.

Explore further

Go further: for ∫sin²x cos²x dx, use sin x cos x=sin 2x/2 to rewrite the integrand as (1−cos 4x)/8. Check x/8−sin 4x/32+C by differentiation. Explain why different identity-based routes give antiderivatives differing only by a constant on a connected interval.

Subtopic 1.3

Trigonometric substitution

Can viewing √(a²−x²) through circle coordinates remove the radical?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Square roots: √z is nonnegative for z≥0, and √(z²)=|z|.
  • Unit circle: sin²θ+cos²θ=1 and tan θ=sin θ/cos θ. Angles θ are in radians.
  • Substitution: x=g(θ) gives dx=g′(θ)dθ. Specify the interval and inverse transformation together.

Learning goals

  • Derive substitutions for the three standard radical forms using identities.
  • Choose angle ranges to determine absolute-value signs and inverse branches.
  • Return to the original variable and check derivatives and definite integrals.

Match each radical form to an identity

For a>0, use x=a sin θ for √(a²−x²). Then a²−x²=a²cos²θ, so the radical is a|cos θ|. Choosing θ∈[−π/2,π/2] makes cos θ≥0 and gives a one-to-one correspondence with x∈[−a,a]. You can then write the radical as a cos θ.

For √(a²+x²), use x=a tan θ with −π/2<θ<π/2 to get a sec θ; sec θ>0 on this interval. For √(x²−a²) on the branch x>a, use x=a sec θ with 0<θ<π/2 to get a tan θ. The branch x<−a needs its own range and sign choices; do not copy the positive-branch calculation unchanged.

Transform dx as well

If x=a sin θ, then dx=a cos θdθ. Substituting the radical but leaving dx unchanged means integrating a different expression. For the inverse, sin θ=x/a and the chosen angle range give θ=arcsin(x/a). The same range also gives cos θ=√(a²−x²)/a.

A triangle drawing with positive side lengths is convenient for x≥0. To handle negative x, use coordinate and angle signs, or work directly with identities. A triangle metaphor with negative side lengths cannot replace branch conditions.

Choose the tool and understand the interval

A radical does not always make trigonometric substitution the shortest method. For ∫x/√(a²+x²)dx, u=a²+x² is much simpler. First look for the derivative of the inner function already present; otherwise consider simplifying the radical with an identity.

Check an indefinite integral by differentiation on an open interval where the original integrand is defined. A radical’s derivative may be singular at endpoints, while a definite integral of a continuous integrand can still use endpoint values. Distinguish the applicable interval from the differentiation conditions of the theorem.

A worked example, step by step

For −2<x<2, find ∫√(4−x²)dx. Also compute ∫₀²√(4−x²)dx.

  1. Set x=2sin θ with −π/2<θ<π/2. Then dx=2cos θdθ and the radical is 2cos θ.
  2. The integral becomes 4∫cos²θdθ=2θ+sin 2θ+C.
  3. Use θ=arcsin(x/2) and sin 2θ=2sin θcos θ=x√(4−x²)/2.
  4. For F=2arcsin(x/2)+(x/2)√(4−x²), differentiation gives 2/√(4−x²)+√(4−x²)/2−x²/(2√(4−x²))=√(4−x²).
  5. The interval 0→2 corresponds to θ:0→π/2. F(2)−F(0)=π, agreeing with the quarter-circle area for radius 2.

F=2arcsin(x/2)+(x/2)√(4−x²)+C. The definite integral is π. Check the derivative on the open interval; evaluate endpoints using the continuous extension of F.

Try it with paper and the lesson

With x=3tan θ, −π/2<θ<π/2, express √(9+x²) and dx in θ, then check at x=3.

Show hint

Use 1+tan²θ=sec²θ and (tan θ)′=sec²θ.

Show solution

The radical is 3sec θ, and dx=3sec²θdθ. At x=3, tan θ=1,θ=π/4, giving the radical 3√2. This agrees with directly computing √(9+9)=3√2.

Misconception and counterexample

√(a²cos²θ)=a cos θ for every θ.

For a>0, the correct expression is a|cos θ|. At θ=π, the left side is a and the proposed right side is −a. Remove the absolute value only after choosing a range with cos θ≥0.

Check your understanding

Which substitution and real range suit a²−x², for a>0?

Show answer and reasoning

x=a sin θ, |x|≤a, θ∈[−π/2,π/2].

The circle identity and a one-to-one sine range determine the radical’s sign.

If x=2sin θ, what is dx?

Show answer and reasoning

2cos θdθ.

Multiply by the derivative of the substitution function.

Is trigonometric substitution essential for ∫x/√(1+x²)dx?

Show answer and reasoning

No. u=1+x² gives √(1+x²)+C.

du=2x dx, and differentiating this antiderivative gives x/√(1+x²).

Explore further

Go further: derive ∫dx/√(a²−x²)=arcsin(x/a)+C on −a<x<a, with a>0. Under x=a sin θ, the a cos θ factors in dx and the radical cancel. The original integrand is undefined at x=±a, so distinguish the open antiderivative interval from endpoint questions for improper integrals.

Subtopic 1.4

Partial-fraction decomposition

Will splitting a complicated fraction into a sum of simpler ones make integration easier?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Polynomials: x²−1=(x−1)(x+1); expansion and factorization reverse each other.
  • Identity: equality must hold for every allowed x, not merely one particular value.
  • Antiderivatives: (ln|x−a|)′=1/(x−a) for x≠a. Constants on separate connected intervals may be independent.

Learning goals

  • Use polynomial division to obtain a proper rational fraction.
  • Choose numerator and denominator structures matching each factor type.
  • Combine fractions and compare coefficients, then integrate and state intervals separated by singularities.

Proper fractions and what is being decomposed

For P/Q, first check cancellation and inputs excluded by the original expression. If deg P≥deg Q, use polynomial division to write P/Q=H+R/Q with deg R<deg Q. Integrate the polynomial H directly and decompose the proper fraction R/Q.

Factor a real-coefficient polynomial denominator into linear factors and quadratic factors irreducible over the reals. The decomposition is an identity where the denominator is nonzero. Determine unknown coefficients from the polynomial identity obtained by clearing denominators.

Include every term required by the denominator

Distinct linear factors (x−a)(x−b) require A/(x−a)+B/(x−b). A repeated factor (x−a)ᵐ requires every power, A₁/(x−a)+⋯+Aₘ/(x−a)ᵐ. Keeping only the highest power cannot represent a general numerator.

For an irreducible quadratic x²+px+q, use the general lower-degree numerator (Ax+B)/(x²+px+q). A repeated quadratic needs such a numerator for each power. Solve coefficients by expansion or suitable substitutions, then combine fractions again to verify the result.

Integrate the pieces and state the intervals

Simple linear fractions give logarithms; higher powers of linear factors use power-rule integration. Irreducible quadratic fractions can be split into a logarithmic part matching the denominator’s derivative and an arctangent part after completing the square. Differentiate the result to verify it.

Writing only ln(x−a) loses the interval x<a. The expression ln|x−a| is appropriate on each interval with x≠a. If a definite integral crosses a denominator root, do not automatically subtract antiderivative endpoint values: separately examine convergence of the improper integral.

A worked example, step by step

For x≠1,−2, find ∫(2x+1)/((x−1)(x+2))dx.

  1. Set up A/(x−1)+B/(x+2). Clearing denominators gives 2x+1=A(x+2)+B(x−1).
  2. At x=1, 3=3A, so A=1. At x=−2, −3=−3B, so B=1. These substitutions are into the polynomial identity after clearing denominators.
  3. Check the numerator: (x+2)+(x−1)=2x+1.
  4. Integrating gives ln|x−1|+ln|x+2|+C.
  5. Differentiation gives 1/(x−1)+1/(x+2)=(2x+1)/((x−1)(x+2)).

On each of (−∞,−2), (−2,1), and (1,∞), an antiderivative is ln|x−1|+ln|x+2|+C. Choose constants separately for the different intervals.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Decompose 1/[x(x+1)] into partial fractions and integrate. Check the decomposition value at x=1.

Show hint

Compare the constant and x coefficients in 1=A(x+1)+Bx.

Show solution

A=1 and A+B=0, giving B=−1. The integral is ln|x|−ln|x+1|+C on intervals excluding x=0,−1. At x=1, the original value is 1/2 and the decomposed value is 1−1/2=1/2. Differentiation also recovers the identity.

Misconception and counterexample

For a repeated factor (x−1)², the single term A/(x−1)² is always enough.

Since (x+1)/(x−1)²=1/(x−1)+2/(x−1)², a single constant numerator cannot produce the x term of the original numerator. Allow both A/(x−1) and B/(x−1)².

Check your understanding

What should you do first if the numerator degree is at least the denominator degree?

Show answer and reasoning

Polynomial division.

Separate a polynomial part from a proper fraction with lower numerator degree.

What are A,B for 1/[x(x+1)]?

Show answer and reasoning

A=1,B=−1.

Compare coefficients in 1=A(x+1)+Bx.

Can ∫₋₁¹1/x dx be called 0 simply by subtracting the endpoint values of ln|x|?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

There is a singularity at x=0, and the two one-sided improper integrals diverge separately. The symmetric principal value differs from the ordinary improper integral.

Explore further

Go further: derive x²/(x²−1)=1+1/(x²−1)=1+(1/2)/(x−1)−(1/2)/(x+1). Differentiate x+(1/2)ln|x−1|−(1/2)ln|x+1|+C for x≠±1. Explain why beginning without division can omit the required polynomial term.
