Subtopic 3.1

Conditions for Euler Trails

Is a walk that uses every road once the same problem as a walk that visits every place once?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • In a finite undirected graph, vertices represent places and edges represent connections. Degree counts incident edge ends; a loop contributes 2. Parallel edges are distinguished as separate edges.
  • A walk follows consecutive edges; a trail is a walk without repeated edges; a path is a walk without repeated vertices. Length is the number of edges used.
  • An isolated vertex has degree 0. Requiring all vertices incident to edges to lie in one connected component does not require isolated vertices to be connected as well. Here, the edge-support subgraph consists of the positive-degree vertices and all original edges.

Learning goals

  • Explain the exact conditions for Euler trails and circuits.
  • Check both degrees and connectivity of the part containing edges.

Traversing every edge

An Euler trail uses every edge exactly once. Vertices may be revisited. If its start and end agree, it is an Euler circuit. Here, a circuit is a closed trail, not a simple cycle whose internal vertices are also visited only once.

This unit uses finite undirected graphs with a nonempty vertex set. If there are no edges, we allow a length-zero traversal staying at any vertex as an Euler circuit. If edges do exist, the start must be incident to an edge to reach all edges.

Pairing arrivals with departures

In a closed traversal, incoming and outgoing edges are paired at each vertex, so every degree must be even. In an open Euler trail, only the distinct start and end have one unpaired edge end and hence odd degree; the others have even degree. A loop supplies two ends for arrival and departure as well.

A single traversal using every edge visits all incident vertices within one component. Degree conditions alone are insufficient. Two disjoint triangles have degree 2 everywhere, yet one traversal cannot use all six edges.

From necessary to sufficient conditions

When the positive-degree vertices are connected, an Euler circuit exists if every degree is even. If exactly two vertices have odd degree, an open Euler trail exists with those two as endpoints. Thus, allowing the closed case, an Euler trail requires 0 or 2 odd-degree vertices. The next topic gives a construction by splicing.

For example, attach edge cd to triangle abc. Degrees are a=2,b=2,c=3,d=1. The open traversal c→a→b→c→d uses all four edges once, but no closed traversal can do so. Adding an isolated vertex z preserves this edge traversal; visiting z is not required.

A worked example, step by step

For V={a,b,c,d,z}, E={ab,bc,ca,cd}, determine whether an Euler trail and circuit exist, and write a possible trail.

  1. Count the incident edges: a and b have degree 2, c has 3, d has 1, and isolated z has 0.
  2. The edge-incident vertices a,b,c,d lie in one component. Isolated z does not obstruct the requirement to traverse every edge.
  3. The two odd-degree vertices are c,d, so an open Euler trail exists but an Euler circuit does not.
  4. The traversal c→a→b→c→d uses ca,ab,bc,cd, each of the four original edges exactly once.

An open Euler trail exists from c to d. No Euler circuit exists, and z need not be visited.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Check degrees and endpoints separately after removing cd, and after adding a new vertex e and edge de to the original graph. Also compare two disjoint triangles.

Show hint

Adding or deleting an edge changes parity at its two endpoints. First check connectivity of the incident vertices.

Show solution

Removing cd leaves triangle abc, with d,z isolated. Then a→b→c→a is an Euler circuit. Adding de gives c=3,d=2,e=1, and c→a→b→c→d→e is an open trail. Two disjoint triangles have even degrees everywhere but no Euler trail because the edge-support subgraph is disconnected.

Misconception and counterexample

If every degree is even, connectivity is unnecessary.

All vertices of two disjoint triangles have degree 2. With no edge connecting the components, a single trail cannot use the edges of both.

Check your understanding

May an Euler trail never repeat a vertex either?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

The ban is on repeated edges. The trail c→a→b→c→d revisits c.

Does adding one isolated vertex always destroy an Euler circuit?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Connectivity of the edge-containing part and the degree conditions remain unchanged. Visiting isolated vertices is not part of the Euler requirement.

Can an undirected graph with four odd-degree vertices have an Euler trail?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

At every vertex other than the start and end, arrivals and departures must be paired.

Explore further

Extension: if a trail starts at v and ends at w, the number of used edge ends at a vertex x contributes 2 for every internal visit, with one extra end for each start or end visit. When v≠w, only v,w have odd counts. When v=w, start and end also pair, so every count is even. If every edge is used, these counts are the original degrees. Any two incident vertices appearing in the same trail are connected by part of the trail, so edge-support connectivity is necessary too. This argument counts the two ends of loops and distinct parallel edges without alteration. Directed graphs need separate in-degree and out-degree conditions; this undirected theorem cannot be applied unchanged.

Subtopic 3.2

Constructing Circuits

Can two small closed walks be joined into one traversal of all roads?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • In an even-degree graph, the edge ends at each vertex can be paired. Used edges are deleted from the remaining graph.
  • A closed trail returns to its start and has no repeated edges, but may repeat vertices.
  • Edges need distinct names so that parallel edges can be used separately. The example below is a simple graph without loops or parallel edges.

Learning goals

  • Construct closed trails and splice in remaining trails.
  • Prove preservation conditions and termination of the construction.

Getting stuck only at the start

Suppose a finite graph has even degree at every vertex and connected edge support. Start at an incident vertex s and follow unused edges. If you arrive at a vertex other than s and cannot leave, its used arrivals and departures differ by 1. It has used an odd number of ends despite having exhausted an originally even degree, a contradiction.

Therefore, when no further edge can be taken, you have returned to the start. Since there are finitely many edges and each is used only once, the process terminates. The resulting closed trail may use all edges or only some.

Starting where remaining edges meet the trail

Deleting the edges of a closed trail subtracts an even number from each degree, so remaining degrees are still even. If any edges remain, original connectivity ensures that some vertex of the current trail is incident to a remaining edge. Start there and construct another closed trail using only remaining edges.

Replace one visit to the shared vertex with the new trail to splice them. For example, a→b→c→a and a→d→e→a become a→b→c→a→d→e→a. Their edge sets are disjoint, so no edge is repeated.

Reducing the open case to the closed case

If u,v are the two odd-degree vertices, add a temporary edge e* between them. Even if an original edge already joins them, allow e* as a separately named parallel edge. Both odd degrees become even, so construct an Euler circuit in the enlarged graph.

Cut that circuit at e* and read the remaining sequence to obtain a u–v trail using every original edge once. The temporary edge is not included in the actual solution. This reduction does not remove the assumption that the original edge support is connected.

A worked example, step by step

Triangles abc and ade share only vertex a. For E={ab,bc,ca,ad,de,ea}, construct two closed trails and splice them.

  1. Vertex a has degree 4 and b,c,d,e have degree 2. The edge-support subgraph is connected.
  2. First construct a→b→c→a, using ab,bc,ca.
  3. Remaining edges ad,de,ea form a→d→e→a starting at a. They share no edges with the first trail.
  4. Insert the new trail at a visit to a, obtaining a→b→c→a→d→e→a. Each of the six edges is used once.

An Euler circuit is a→b→c→a→d→e→a, of length 6.

Try it with paper and the lesson

If ea is removed from the same graph, which vertices become odd? Use a temporary edge to construct an open trail and check its five actual edges.

Show hint

Vertex a has degree 3 and e has 1. Add temporary a–e, then cut that edge out of the circuit.

Show solution

The odd vertices are a,e. The trail a→b→c→a→d→e uses ab,bc,ca,ad,de once each. It results from adding temporary ea to obtain a→b→c→a→d→e→a and then removing that final temporary edge.

Misconception and counterexample

Any closed trail that you find is automatically a complete Euler circuit.

The trail a→b→c→a leaves ad,de,ea unused. To prove complete coverage, check that the remaining-edge list is empty.

Check your understanding

After deleting a closed trail, are remaining degrees even?

Show answer and reasoning

Yes.

An even number of edge ends was used at each vertex.

If two trails share a vertex, can edge repetitions be ignored when joining them?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Build the second trail from unused edges to keep their edge sets disjoint.

Is the temporary edge retained in the open solution?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Cut it out to obtain a trail using only original edges.

Explore further

Extension: suppose some edges remain but none is incident to any vertex of the current closed trail W. Original connectivity gives a path from a vertex of W to an endpoint of a remaining edge. The first edge leaving the vertex set of W cannot have been used by W: both endpoints of any used edge belong to W. Thus this edge remains and is incident to W, contradicting the assumption. Constructing a new closed trail in the remaining even-degree graph and splicing it into W increases the number of used edges by at least 1. This number is bounded by the finite total, so repetition terminates with all edges used. This proves sufficiency for an Euler circuit; the temporary-edge argument also proves sufficiency with two odd vertices.

Subtopic 3.3

Hamilton Properties and Counterexamples

Can conditions for using every edge decide whether every vertex can be visited once?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • The Hamilton examples below use finite undirected simple graphs with n≥3 vertices. A simple cycle repeats only its starting vertex at the end; no other vertex repeats.
  • A Hamilton path contains every vertex once, and a Hamilton cycle is a simple cycle containing every vertex.
  • Connected components group vertices joined by paths. Deleting a vertex removes that vertex and all incident edges.

Learning goals

  • Separate Euler and Hamilton conditions using counterexamples.
  • Prove Hamilton impossibility by counting components after vertex deletion.

The objects to visit are vertices

A Hamilton path visits every vertex exactly once. A Hamilton cycle does so and then returns to the start. It need not use every original edge. With n≥3, each vertex on the cycle requires two distinct incident edges, so minimum degree 2 is necessary.

An Euler circuit uses every edge and may revisit vertices. The graph of two triangles sharing a from the previous topic has an Euler circuit passing through a twice. But deleting a leaves components {b,c},{d,e}, so no Hamilton cycle exists.

Necessary does not mean sufficient

The shared-triangle graph has even degrees everywhere and minimum degree 2, yet no Hamilton cycle. Deleting one vertex from a Hamilton cycle leaves a path connecting all remaining vertices, but deleting a from this graph leaves them disconnected.

Conversely, K₄ has degree 3 at every vertex and no Euler circuit, but a→b→c→d→a is a Hamilton cycle. Odd degrees by themselves do not prevent a Hamilton cycle.

Sufficient conditions and the reach of evidence

Dirac’s theorem states that an undirected simple graph with n≥3 has a Hamilton cycle if every degree is at least n/2. This is sufficient: failure of the condition does not imply absence. A cycle of length 5 has minimum degree 2<5/2 but is already a Hamilton cycle. This unit cites that sufficient-condition theorem; what it proves is the necessary vertex-deletion component condition. Do not treat the two conditions as converses or as the same theorem.

Evidence of existence is an actual cycle whose vertex order and every consecutive edge have been checked. Evidence of impossibility must be a structural argument ruling out every candidate. A few failed attempts cannot establish global absence or a general complexity theorem.

A worked example, step by step

Compare the shared-triangle graph G and K₄, deciding existence of an Euler circuit and a Hamilton cycle for each.

  1. G has degrees 4,2,2,2,2 and is connected, so it has Euler circuit a→b→c→a→d→e→a.
  2. Deleting a from G separates bc and de. The remaining path after deleting a from any Hamilton cycle would connect all remaining vertices, giving a contradiction.
  3. All four degrees in K₄ are 3. With four odd vertices it has neither an Euler trail nor an Euler circuit.
  4. In K₄, a→b→c→d→a contains all four vertices and every traversed edge exists. Hence a Hamilton cycle exists.

G has an Euler circuit but no Hamilton cycle. K₄ has a Hamilton cycle but no Euler trail.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Does the star K₁,₃ with center s and leaves a,b,c have a Hamilton path? Find a Hamilton path in G and compare the cases.

Show hint

An internal path vertex uses two neighbors. Leaves of the star can occur only at the path endpoints.

Show solution

The star has three leaves but a path has only two endpoints, so no Hamilton path exists. In G, b→c→a→d→e visits all five vertices once. Absence of a Hamilton cycle is different from absence of a Hamilton path.

Misconception and counterexample

Minimum degree 2, or even degree everywhere, guarantees a Hamilton cycle.

Two triangles sharing one vertex meet both conditions, but deleting that vertex creates two components, ruling out a Hamilton cycle.

Check your understanding

Must a Hamilton cycle also use every edge?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Its target is vertices; the cycle in K₄ uses only four of the six edges.

Does failure of Dirac’s condition imply no Hamilton cycle?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

The 5-cycle has minimum degree 2, below the threshold, but is itself a Hamilton cycle.

Does absence of a Hamilton cycle imply absence of a Hamilton path?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

The path b→c→a→d→e in the shared-triangle graph G is a counterexample.

Explore further

Extension: delete a nonempty vertex set S from a simple graph with n≥3 and a Hamilton cycle. Removing S from the cycle breaks the remaining vertices into at most |S| consecutive path pieces. The remaining graph contains the edges of these pieces, so its component count cannot exceed the piece count. Thus c(G−S)≤|S| is necessary. If S is the full vertex set, it still holds with 0 remaining components. A Hamilton path gives at most |S|+1 pieces, so c(G−S)≤|S|+1 is necessary. For the star, S={s} gives 3>2, ruling out even a path; for shared triangles, 2>1 rules out only a cycle. These component conditions are used only as necessary conditions here; no general sufficiency is claimed.

Subtopic 3.4

Applying Traversal Models

Do inspecting every road and visiting every house once use the same graph conditions?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • First specify what vertices and edges represent. Using each edge once and visiting each vertex once are different requirements.
  • Separate roads joining the same two places remain parallel edges. Undirected means travel is allowed both ways; one-way roads require another model.
  • Traversal existence, minimum total distance, deadlines, safety and capacity constraints are different questions.

Learning goals

  • Translate requirements into edge- or vertex-visiting problems.
  • Distinguish feasibility with repetitions from feasibility with an exactly-once requirement.

Deciding what must not be missed

If road inspection requires traversing every road exactly once and returning to base, an Euler circuit is appropriate. If delivery requires visiting every house exactly once and returning, a Hamilton cycle may be considered. Restricting an actual delivery task to Hamilton conditions when revisiting junctions is allowed would change its requirements too strongly.

The paper roads in this unit are undirected, traversable and static; the traveler moves between edge endpoints. Visiting an isolated house requires a connecting route, whereas an isolated vertex with no road to inspect may have no effect on an Euler traversal.

Allowing repetitions changes the problem

Shared triangles permit closed road inspection but not a closed traversal visiting each junction once. Changing whether vertices may repeat changes the answer for the same drawing. Without lengths or costs, only existence is being decided; do not call the result shortest.

Allowing undirected edge repetitions can address odd vertices by traversing some edges additional times. A shortest closed inspection with lengths is a separate optimization problem. Here we prove only a length lower bound for a small graph and an example attaining it.

Even a small cost proof has conditions

Give each edge of triangle abc and edge cd length 1, and inspect starting and ending at d. Edge cd is the only edge connecting d to the other roads, so a closed walk must use it at least twice. The other three roads each need at least one traversal, giving length lower bound 5.

The walk d→c→a→b→c→d has length 5 and attains the bound, so it is shortest in this paper model. Real road times, turns, traffic, vehicles and work deadlines were not measured, and the sum of unit lengths is not identified with physical time.

A worked example, step by step

Let E={ab,bc,ca,cd}, with each edge of length 1. Find a shortest walk starting at d, traversing every edge at least once and returning to d; compare an exactly-once requirement.

  1. Degrees c=3,d=1 rule out a closed Euler circuit using every edge exactly once.
  2. Deleting cd separates d. To leave d and return, cd must be used at least twice.
  3. The other edges ab,bc,ca must each be used at least once, giving total length at least 2+3=5.
  4. The walk d→c→a→b→c→d uses cd,ca,ab,bc,cd and has length 5. It attains the lower bound and is shortest.

No exactly-once closed traversal exists, but with repetitions the shortest closed inspection has length 5.

Try it with paper and the lesson

In the same model, change the start to d and the end to c and inspect each edge exactly once. Also require delivery to isolated vertex z, and compare the results.

Show hint

Use the two odd vertices as endpoints of an open trail. Separately check whether any edge makes isolated z reachable.

Show solution

The trail d→c→a→b→c uses the four edges once each and has length 4. But z has no incident connecting edge, so no delivery walk also visiting z exists. Road inspection may ignore z, whereas delivery requiring its visit may not.

Misconception and counterexample

Meeting Euler conditions also satisfies all real timing, safety and shortest-route requirements.

Euler’s theorem guarantees existence of an exactly-once edge traversal in the specified graph. If edge times, one-way restrictions, interruptions and deadlines were omitted, it does not guarantee those real conditions.

Check your understanding

Is a house-visiting requirement automatically inspection of every edge?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

A walk can visit every vertex while leaving some edges unused.

Does a paper walk of unit length 5 mean five seconds of actual travel?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Physical time, speed and delay were neither defined nor observed.

Does finding one candidate prove the example’s shortest length 5?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Both a length-5 candidate and a lower bound of 5 for every allowed walk were checked.

Explore further

Extension: deleting a bridge e separates a graph into two parts A,B. If a closed walk starts in A, visits edges in B and returns, the numbers of transitions A→B and B→A are equal. Its total uses of the unique connecting edge e are a positive even number, at least 2. This lower bound applies to cd in the example. General graphs may contain several bridges and cycles; with negative edge weights, repeated traversal may drive cost down without bound. Existence of a shortest walk and minimization conditions therefore need fresh analysis. This example assumes a finite undirected graph with positive unit weights and specified start and end.
