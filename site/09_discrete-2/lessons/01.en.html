Subtopic 1.1

Simple graphs, directed graphs, and multigraphs

If two roads join the same cities, or a road is one-way, can the same map symbol capture everything?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Sets: {A,B} and {B,A} are the same set; elements are not repeated.
  • Ordered pairs: (A,B) and (B,A) differ when A≠B.
  • Self-check: friendship may be symmetric, whereas following someone may be one-way.

Learning goals

  • Define vertices and edges as data describing a relation.
  • State whether directions, parallel edges, and loops are allowed.
  • Distinguish drawing positions from the meaning of weights.

A relation, beyond dots and lines

This unit concerns finite graphs. An undirected simple graph G=(V,E) consists of a vertex set V and an edge set E whose elements are two-element sets of distinct vertices. {A,B} and {B,A} are the same edge. Since {A,A} is not a two-element set, loops are not allowed. The set E does not count the same edge twice.

Vertices represent objects, and edges represent the relation we choose to model. The positions of dots and lengths of drawn lines are not part of this definition. Isolated vertices must also appear in V. Defining V as the union of endpoints in an edge list can lose isolated vertices.

Direction and multiplicity are separate choices

A directed edge is recorded as an ordered pair (source,destination). Having (A,B) does not imply having (B,A). State separately whether loops are allowed. In this lesson, a simple directed graph has neither parallel edges nor loops. Other sources may use different naming conventions, so the structural conditions take priority.

In a multigraph, give edges identifiers such as e₁,e₂ and specify the endpoints of each edge. This distinguishes multiple edges with the same endpoints. Some books call a graph that also permits loops a pseudograph. Here we state conditions explicitly, for example “undirected multigraph with loops allowed,” rather than infer every rule from one name.

What information belongs to an edge?

A weighted graph adds a function such as w:E→ℝ to record distance, time, or cost. Drawing an edge longer does not automatically mean that it has greater cost. Direction and weight are independent choices; whether negative weights are permitted also depends on the purpose.

Combining two actual roads into one simple edge can express connectivity but loses road counts and individual costs. First decide what the model is for, then check whether it preserves the necessary information. This section addresses the meaning of representation rather than shortest-path algorithms.

A worked example, step by step

Let V={A,B,C,D,E}. There are two roads between A and B, one one-way road B→C, one two-way road between C and D, and isolated vertex E. Build a representation preserving individual roads and allowed travel directions.

  1. Record every vertex, including isolated E.
  2. Represent all travel by directed edges. Distinguish the two A↔B roads with e₁:A→B,e₂:B→A,e₃:A→B,e₄:B→A.
  3. Record just e₅ for B→C, and e₆:C→D,e₇:D→C for C↔D.
  4. The result is a directed multigraph without loops. Distinguish reverse-direction edges from same-direction parallel edges.

Five vertices and seven directed edges. Under this convention, each two-way road is represented by two directional edges. Four actual roads and seven directed edges are different counts.

Try it with paper and the lesson

On paper, draw V={1,2,3}, E={{1,2},{2,3}} with two different vertex layouts. Then draw a directed version allowing only (1,2),(2,3), and compare whether travel from 3 to 1 is possible.

Show hint

Follow consecutive edges; in the directed version, follow the arrows.

Show solution

The undirected version permits 3→2→1. The directed version has no outgoing edge from 3, so travel from 3 to 1 is impossible. The two undirected drawings have the same endpoint relation and hence represent the same graph.

Misconception and counterexample

Whenever two drawn lines cross, their crossing automatically becomes a vertex.

Drawing edges {A,B},{C,D} across each other does not connect them unless the crossing is declared a vertex. Record whether a real crossing is an intersection or an overpass in V and the endpoint specifications.

Check your understanding

Are undirected edges {A,B} and {B,A} different?

Show answer and reasoning

They are the same.

The endpoint set has no order.

If only A→B is present, is B→A automatically present?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

A directed edge is an ordered pair; the reverse is a separate edge.

Does a simple-graph representation preserve every detail of two parallel roads?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Connectivity may remain, while edge multiplicity and individual attributes can be lost.

Explore further

Go further: a mixed graph can store undirected and directed edges separately. Replacing every undirected edge by two opposite directed edges preserves possible travel but changes the identity of the undirected edge and the degree convention. Explain for which purposes this transformation is appropriate.

Subtopic 1.2

Degrees and the handshaking lemma

If we add the numbers of edge endpoints touching all vertices, how many times is each edge counted?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • The size |V| of a finite set is its number of elements.
  • Parity: a sum of even numbers is even; a sum of k odd numbers has the same parity as k.
  • Graphs: first identify the two endpoints and the direction of each edge.

Learning goals

  • Apply degree conventions for loops and directions.
  • Prove the handshaking lemma by counting endpoint occurrences in two ways.
  • Derive that the number of odd-degree vertices is even.

Degree counts endpoint occurrences

In an undirected simple graph, deg(v) counts the edges incident to v and equals its number of neighbors. In an undirected multigraph, count parallel edges separately. If loops are allowed, the two endpoints of a loop both touch the same vertex, so each loop adds 2 to its degree. Neighbor count and degree may therefore differ.

In a directed graph, distinguish indeg(v) from outdeg(v). Edges ending at v contribute to indegree; edges starting at v contribute to outdegree. A directed loop adds 1 to each. If using total degree, explicitly state the convention indeg+outdeg.

Count endpoints in two ways

Counting endpoint occurrences by vertex gives Σᵥdeg(v). Counting by edge gives 2|E|, since every edge has two endpoints. Both count the same occurrences, so Σdeg(v)=2|E|. Counting a loop twice also maintains this equality.

For directed graphs, counting the one source of every edge gives Σoutdeg=|E|, and counting the one destination gives Σindeg=|E|. The sum of total degrees is 2|E|, but the sum of just indegrees or just outdegrees is |E|.

A parity consequence and its limits

Because Σdeg is even, the number of odd-degree vertices is even. The even-degree terms sum to an even number, so the parity of the whole sum equals the parity of the number of odd terms. A finite undirected graph cannot have exactly one odd-degree vertex.

An even degree sum is necessary, but not sufficient, for a sequence to be the degree sequence of a simple graph. With n vertices, a simple graph has maximum degree at most n−1. More detailed feasibility tests connect to later graph-construction problems.

A worked example, step by step

For the undirected simple graph V={A,B,C,D,E}, E={AB,AC,BC,CD,DE}, compare vertex degrees with the edge count.

  1. A touches AB,AC, so its degree is 2. B touches AB,BC, so its degree is 2.
  2. C touches AC,BC,CD, giving 3; D touches CD,DE, giving 2; E touches only DE, giving 1.
  3. The degree sum is 2+2+3+2+1=10. There are five edges, so 2|E|=10.
  4. The odd-degree vertices are C,E: two vertices, an even count.

Degrees (2,2,3,2,1), sum 10=2×5. Recording every undirected edge in both endpoint adjacency lists also gives total list length 10.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Draw an undirected multigraph with two parallel edges between U and V and one loop at U. Calculate the degrees.

Show hint

The loop adds 2 at U; each parallel edge adds 1 at both U and V.

Show solution

deg(U)=2+2=4, deg(V)=2. Three edges give degree sum 6=2×3. Whether a loop makes U its own neighbor affects distinct-neighbor conventions, but endpoint counting fixes the degree.

Misconception and counterexample

An even degree sum always guarantees a simple graph.

The three-vertex sequence (3,3,0) sums to 6, but every degree must be at most 2, making it impossible. The handshaking lemma supplies a necessary condition; simplicity, vertex count, and other conditions must also be checked.

Check your understanding

Can a finite undirected graph have exactly three odd-degree vertices?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

That contradicts the even degree sum.

What is the degree sum of an undirected graph with seven edges?

Show answer and reasoning

14.

Each edge contributes two endpoint occurrences.

With seven directed edges, is the outdegree sum 14?

Show answer and reasoning

It is 7.

Count one source per edge. The indegree sum is also 7.

Explore further

Go further: in a bipartite graph with V=L∪R, if every edge joins one vertex in L to one in R, then Σᵥ∈Ldeg(v)=|E|=Σᵥ∈Rdeg(v). With |L|=2, |R|=3, and six edges, calculate the average degrees 3 and 2. The averages differ while the sums agree.

Subtopic 1.3

Adjacency and incidence matrices

What changes when a network is represented by a vertex–vertex table rather than a vertex–edge table?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • A matrix is a table of numbers with ordered rows and columns. Aᵢⱼ is its entry in row i, column j.
  • Adjacency: two vertices share an edge. Incidence: a vertex occurs as an endpoint of an edge.
  • Quick review: transposition Aᵀ exchanges rows and columns. Even the same numeric table requires a declared ordering.

Learning goals

  • Declare vertex and edge orders before constructing both matrices.
  • Distinguish symmetry and row-sum meanings for directed and undirected adjacency matrices.
  • Check signed incidence conventions, matrix dimensions, and column sums.

Adjacency is vertex × vertex

Fix a vertex order v₁,…,vₙ for an undirected simple graph. Its adjacency matrix A has Aᵢⱼ=1 when {vᵢ,vⱼ} is an edge, and 0 otherwise. Without loops, its diagonal is zero. Being undirected gives Aᵢⱼ=Aⱼᵢ. Row sums equal degrees.

For directed graphs, use Aᵢⱼ=1 to mean vᵢ→vⱼ. Row sums are then outdegrees and column sums indegrees. A multigraph may instead store edge counts or sums of weights; distinguish these from a 0/1 table. If an adjacency matrix records an undirected loop as 1, its row sum may differ from degree, which counts that loop twice, so an additional convention is needed.

Incidence is vertex × edge

For a loopless undirected graph, the vertex × edge incidence matrix B has Bᵢₖ=1 when vertex vᵢ is an endpoint of edge eₖ. One column represents one edge and has exactly two 1s. Parallel edges produce repeated column patterns.

A signed directed incidence matrix uses −1 at the source, +1 at the destination, and 0 elsewhere. Each non-loop column therefore sums to zero. Some sources transpose the table to edge × vertex; check this lesson’s vertex × edge convention. A directed loop produces a zero column because source and destination cancel, so the signed matrix alone cannot reconstruct loops.

Information and storage costs

An adjacency matrix has n² entries; an incidence matrix has n|E| entries. A sparsely connected graph can also use adjacency lists recording just neighbors. Representations may preserve the same relation while requiring different storage and answering questions differently.

Changing the vertex order requires changing adjacency rows and columns together. For incidence, vertex and edge orders change separately. Different numeric tables do not by themselves prove different graphs. Independently check simple-graph row sums, diagonal entries and symmetry, and the two-endpoint patterns of incidence columns, to find recording errors.

A worked example, step by step

Use vertex order A,B,C,D,E and edge order AB,AC,BC,CD,DE to construct adjacency and unsigned incidence matrices for an undirected simple graph.

  1. Adjacency rows: A:[0,1,1,0,0], B:[1,0,1,0,0], C:[1,1,0,1,0], D:[0,0,1,0,1], E:[0,0,0,1,0].
  2. Incidence columns: AB:(1,1,0,0,0), AC:(1,0,1,0,0), BC:(0,1,1,0,0), CD:(0,0,1,1,0), DE:(0,0,0,1,1).
  3. Rewritten as incidence rows: A:[1,1,0,0,0], B:[1,0,1,0,0], C:[0,1,1,1,0], D:[0,0,0,1,1], E:[0,0,0,0,1].
  4. The adjacency matrix is symmetric with zero diagonal. Both tables have row sums (2,2,3,2,1); all incidence column sums are 2.

Both tables happen to be 5×5 because the numbers of vertices and edges coincide. Adjacency columns represent vertices; incidence columns represent edges.

Try it with paper and the lesson

With vertex order 1,2,3 and edge order e₁:1→2,e₂:3→2, write the directed adjacency and signed incidence matrices.

Show hint

Adjacency uses source rows and destination columns. Incidence uses source −1 and destination +1.

Show solution

A=[[0,1,0],[0,0,0],[0,1,0]]. B=[[-1,0],[1,1],[0,-1]]. A has row sums (1,0,1) and column sums (0,2,0). Both columns of B sum to zero, and B is 3×2.

Misconception and counterexample

Adjacency matrix and incidence matrix are two names for the same table.

With three vertices and two edges, adjacency is 3×3 and incidence is 3×2. The first records connections between vertex pairs; the second records endpoints for each edge, with different sign conventions.

Check your understanding

What is the diagonal of a loopless undirected adjacency matrix?

Show answer and reasoning

Zero.

Edges connecting a vertex to itself are not allowed.

In directed adjacency with source rows and destination columns, what does a column sum give?

Show answer and reasoning

Indegree.

It counts edges whose destination is the column’s vertex.

If all signed incidence columns sum to zero, must the graph be connected?

Show answer and reasoning

That alone does not tell us.

Source and destination signs cancel locally for each edge; connectivity is a separate property.

Explore further

Go further: for an undirected simple graph, (A²)ᵢⱼ=ΣₖAᵢₖAₖⱼ counts length-two walks vᵢ→vₖ→vⱼ. Calculate (A²)AA=2 and (A²)AC=1 in the example. Returning in two steps repeats a vertex, so these walks differ from simple paths.

Subtopic 1.4

Graph isomorphism and invariants

How can we prove that a network with changed names and positions has the same underlying structure?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Bijection: match every vertex exactly once, without duplication or omission.
  • Logical equivalence “iff”: an edge exists exactly when its corresponding edge exists; both directions hold.
  • Connectivity: travel between two vertices is possible along consecutive edges. Degree counts edges incident to a vertex.

Learning goals

  • Define isomorphism as a bijection preserving edges and nonedges.
  • Prove isomorphism with an explicit correspondence.
  • Prove nonisomorphism from differing invariants and explain the limits of matching invariants.

The same structure after renaming

An isomorphism between undirected simple graphs G,H is a bijection φ:V(G)→V(H) such that for all distinct u,v, {u,v}∈E(G) iff {φ(u),φ(v)}∈E(H). Checking only one direction can overlook extra edges in H.

Rotating, stretching, or crossing lines in a drawing does not change isomorphism. Extend the definition to preserve directions for directed graphs, edge multiplicities for multigraphs, and weights when required for weighted-graph comparison. First decide which structure is being compared.

Invariants preserved by isomorphism

Vertex count, edge count, sorted degree sequence, number of connected components, and existence of cycles of a specified length are preserved. Degree is preserved because the neighbors of v correspond bijectively to those of φ(v). Paths become corresponding paths, so connectivity is also preserved.

If even one invariant differs, the graphs are not isomorphic. Matching degree sequences alone do not prove isomorphism. The six-cycle C₆ and two disjoint triangles both have six vertices, six edges, and all degrees 2, but one is connected and the other has two components.

Narrow candidates, then check the whole condition

Vertices of different degrees cannot correspond, reducing the candidates. When many vertices have the same degree, consider neighbor degrees, cycle structure, and other information. Ultimately, write an actual bijection and verify the edge and nonedge conditions to obtain a proof.

Ordering vertices by the correspondence makes the adjacency matrices of isomorphic graphs match. Different tables in the original name order do not prove nonisomorphism. This section addresses proofs and counterexamples for small graphs; it makes no claim about current complexity results for general isomorphism algorithms.

A worked example, step by step

G has V={a,b,c,d}, E={ab,bc,cd}; H has V={1,2,3,4}, E={13,34,42}. Show that they are isomorphic.

  1. In G, a,d have degree 1 and b,c degree 2. In H, 1,2 have degree 1 and 3,4 degree 2.
  2. Choose φ(a)=1,φ(b)=3,φ(c)=4,φ(d)=2. Every vertex corresponds exactly once.
  3. Edges ab,bc,cd map respectively to 13,34,42, exactly all edges of H.
  4. Nonedges ac,ad,bd map to 14,12,32, which are absent from H.

φ is a bijection preserving edges and nonedges in both directions, so G≅H. The same path has been renamed.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Draw C₆ as a six-vertex cycle and two separate triangles. Compare vertex counts, edge counts, degree sequences, and component counts.

Show hint

Every vertex has two neighbors, but you cannot travel from one triangle to the other.

Show solution

Both have |V|=6, |E|=6, degree sequence (2,2,2,2,2,2). C₆ has one component; the two triangles have two, so they are not isomorphic. They also differ in whether a triangle exists.

Misconception and counterexample

Equal vertex counts, edge counts, and degree sequences guarantee isomorphism.

C₆ and two disjoint triangles are a counterexample. These invariants can rule out isomorphism when they differ, but matching them does not complete a structural correspondence.

Check your understanding

May corresponding vertices have different degrees under an isomorphism?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

A bijection between their neighbor sets preserves degrees.

In the example, if φ(b)=3, where do b’s neighbors a,c go?

Show answer and reasoning

To 1,4.

The neighbors of 3 in H are exactly 1,4.

Do differing drawn line lengths imply nonisomorphism?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

The definition of an unweighted graph does not include drawn line lengths.

Explore further

Go further: the inverse of an isomorphism is an isomorphism, and composing two isomorphisms gives another. Apply the inverse and composition of bijections, then the edge iff conditions, to prove that isomorphism is a reflexive, symmetric, and transitive equivalence relation.
