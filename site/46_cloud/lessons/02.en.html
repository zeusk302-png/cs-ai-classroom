Subtopic 2.1

Power, cooling and redundancy

If computers use 100 kW, does the whole building also use 100 kW? Does one spare device survive every kind of failure?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Power is the rate of energy use. 1 W=1 J/s and 1 kW=1000 W. Using constant power P kW for t hours consumes Pt kWh. Do not add kW and kWh as if they were the same unit.
  • A ratio divides two quantities with the same unit. 1000 kWh/800 kWh=1.25 is dimensionless. Capacity is an upper limit that can be handled under specified conditions, rather than actual usage.
  • Redundancy adds paths or devices that can provide the same function. Readiness check: two 60 kW devices total 120 kW, but taking one down for maintenance leaves 60 kW. Also check whether both devices share the same piping.

Learning goals

  • Calculate PUE, power and energy using the same measurement boundary and time window.
  • Distinguish power-conversion efficiency, cooling capacity and effective battery energy.
  • Explain the failure scope of N+1, including common failures and maintenance conditions.

Draw both the IT equipment and building boundaries

A data center includes computing, storage and communication equipment, together with its power supply, cooling, space and operation. Much of the electrical energy used by equipment becomes heat, so heat also needs a path out. Here we use only a hypothetical model that approximates the heat load to be removed by IT power, setting aside thermal balance, external heat and efficiency changes. The electrical power consumed by cooling equipment and the heat load it can remove are different quantities even when both are expressed in kW.

PUE divides total facility energy E_fac by IT equipment energy E_IT>0 over the same time window and measurement boundary. With constant powers over the same boundary, it can also be calculated as P_fac/P_IT. Define the numerator consistently, including IT, cooling and power losses within the chosen scope. Do not mix instantaneous and annual values measured under different temperatures, loads or periods. A PUE near 1 means a small relative infrastructure overhead; it does not prove that the same useful work uses less energy.

Backup power has an energy budget and a duration

Here a power converter’s efficiency η is output energy/input energy over the same period, with 0<η≤1. Output of 90 kWh at η=0.9 requires input of 100 kWh and loses 10 kWh. PUE compares the whole facility with IT, so it is not the same indicator as this converter’s η. Batteries limit stored energy, power converters limit deliverable power, and cooling limits heat-removal capacity.

Declare a hypothetical emergency path with nominal battery energy 5 kWh and a deliverable fraction 0.8 that already accounts for both allowable discharge and conversion. Effective energy is 4 kWh. If the load is constant, the battery supplies the entire 125 kW load, and the converter’s power limit also meets that load, duration is 4/125 hours. Real batteries depend on discharge rate, temperature, aging, switching delay and maintaining cooling; dividing nominal energy by power alone does not guarantee actual duration.

N+1 survives one specified loss

For a heat load of 100 kW and hypothetical guaranteed cooling capacity 60 kW per device, the minimum device count is N=ceil(100/60)=2. Three devices in parallel provide N+1, leaving two devices and 120 kW after one is unavailable. This addition assumes that each capacity is valid under the same conditions and that flow distribution, power and piping do not obstruct the remaining devices’ capacity. Installed device count alone does not verify those assumptions.

If one device is undergoing maintenance and another fails, only 60 kW remains, which cannot handle 100 kW. If their shared piping or power distribution stops, all three devices may be unusable. Record whether redundant paths fail independently, whether isolation and switching are possible, and which time windows count planned downtime as a service failure. More devices, lower failure probability and actual uninterrupted service are separate claims.

A worked example, step by step

A hypothetical facility uses constant IT power 100 kW and additional infrastructure power 25 kW for eight hours. A nominal 5 kWh battery with effective fraction 0.8 supplies the entire emergency load of 125 kW. Assume generator switching takes two minutes. Cooling uses the three 60 kW devices above. Calculate without actual measurements.

  1. IT energy is 100×8=800 kWh, and total energy is (100+25)×8=1000 kWh. The boundary and period match, so PUE=1000/800=1.25 and additional energy is 200 kWh.
  2. Hypothetical battery delivery is 5×0.8=4 kWh. Assuming the deliverable power limit is at least 125 kW, duration is 4/125=0.032 hours=1.92 minutes.
  3. Since 1.92 minutes<2 minutes, the battery alone fails the condition of bridging the entire switching period. The shortfall is 0.08 minutes=4.8 seconds. This is not an observation of actual generator or battery performance.
  4. After losing one cooling device, 2×60=120 kW≥100 kW satisfies the hypothetical capacity condition. Overlapping maintenance of one device and failure of another leaves 60<100 and fails it.

PUE is 1.25 and IT/facility energy is 800/1000 kWh. The battery lasts 1.92 minutes, too little for a two-minute switch. The cooling model survives only one device loss and does not guarantee operation with two simultaneously unavailable devices.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Over the same eight-hour boundary, candidate X has IT power 100 kW/total power 130 kW, and candidate Y has IT power 200 kW/total power 240 kW. Compare PUE and total energy. Useful throughput is not given. For a cooling load of 180 kW and 70 kW per device, also find the N+1 device count and capacity after losing one or two devices.

Show hint

Each denominator is that candidate’s own IT energy. A smaller ratio and a smaller total are different comparisons. Round load/per-device capacity upward to obtain N.

Show solution

X has PUE 1.3/total energy 1040 kWh; Y has 1.2/total energy 1920 kWh. Y’s lower PUE does not imply lower total energy or higher performance. N=ceil(180/70)=3, so N+1 means four devices. One loss leaves 210≥180; two losses leave 140<180 kW. Common-path failure is separate.

Misconception and counterexample

Low PUE and N+1 equipment are enough to reduce total energy and keep service uninterrupted at all times.

Although Y has lower PUE, its total energy exceeds X’s, and useful work is unknown. N+1 specifies capacity under one loss; overlapping maintenance and failure, or shared-piping failure, provides a counterexample.

Check your understanding

Does constant power of 50 kW for three hours consume 150 kW of energy?

Show answer and reasoning

It consumes 150 kWh.

Multiplying power by time gives energy, so time remains in the unit.

For one converter with output 90 kWh and efficiency 0.9, what are input energy and loss?

Show answer and reasoning

Input is 100 kWh and loss is 10 kWh.

Since η=output/input, 90/0.9=100; the difference between the two energies is the loss. This is not the same indicator as facility PUE.

If one N+1 device is under maintenance, is another device failure automatically covered?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Two unavailable devices require a separate condition. In the 100 kW load/three 60 kW device model, only one device remains and is insufficient.

Explore further

Proof: for load L>0 and guaranteed per-device capacity c>0, N=ceil(L/c) gives (N−1)c<L≤Nc. If at most one of N+1 installed devices is unavailable, at least N remain and total capacity Nc≥L. Two unavailable devices leave (N−1)c<L, so this model cannot guarantee sufficient capacity. The proof also fails to apply without the capacity premises that device capacities can be added and actual paths and common components are functioning. Also, if E_fac=E_IT+E_extra with E_extra≥0, then PUE=1+E_extra/E_IT≥1. At fixed E_extra, increasing only E_IT lowers PUE, showing algebraically that an improved relative measure is not sufficient for a decrease in absolute energy.

Subtopic 2.2

Servers, storage and networks

If we double the servers, do request throughput and storage both double?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • A server is a computer that processes requests. The CPU performs calculations, memory holds data for current work, storage holds data to preserve, and the network moves data between different devices. One request may use these resources together.
  • Capacity is measured in bytes, throughput in tasks per second, and response time from a request’s start to its completion. Here decimal MB=10⁶ bytes, GB=10⁹ bytes and TB=10¹² bytes are used, and the hypothetical model assumes one byte is eight bits.
  • Readiness check: at 100 MB/s, requests using 2 MB each have an upper bound of 50 requests/s. Actual waiting, failures and other traffic may lower that rate.

Learning goals

  • Distinguish capacity, bandwidth, throughput and latency units for different resources.
  • Use each request’s resource requirements to derive a throughput bound and identify a bottleneck.
  • Distinguish replicated logical capacity, data protection and backup.

A request needs a resource path

Servers, storage and switches are connected within racks, and higher-level links are shared by multiple racks. Different points are shared at different scales, so adding all network-interface speeds does not give simultaneous external throughput. Physical equipment and cluster/application management are different layers: software must combine results and manage copies, and hardware count alone does not establish those semantics.

Declare a hypothetical request that consumes 0.005 core-seconds of CPU, reads 0.5 MB from storage and transfers 0.25 MB externally. There are eight identical cores, total read throughput 400 MB/s, and a shared external link of 1 Gbit/s. CPU usage means the time used on one core per request. Exclude network overhead, compression, caching, failures, synchronization and other loads, and assume the given capacities are effective for this workload.

The smallest resource bound limits the rate

At λ requests/s, CPU requires 0.005λ≤8 core-seconds/s, giving λ≤1600. Storage requires 0.5λ≤400, giving 800. The 1 Gbit/s link is 125 MB/s; dividing by 0.25 gives 500. Since every identical request needs all these resources, long-run throughput is at most min(1600,800,500)=500 by conservation of additive resource use.

An upper bound is not an achieved rate: scheduling dependencies, imbalance and fixed overhead can lower it. Maximum throughput also does not mean no waiting. Doubling only the network to 2 Gbit/s makes its bound 1000 and the overall bound 800, now limited by storage. Response time is not 1/500 seconds. Under a separate hypothetical assumption of sequential CPU, read and transfer with no queuing, their durations sum to 0.005+0.5/400+0.25/125=0.00825 seconds. This is not an actual measurement.

Replication uses capacity and has a protection scope

In a separate capacity model, four 2 TB devices form two pairs, with each pair storing two copies of the same data. Excluding metadata and reserved space, physical capacity is 8 TB and distinct logical data is at most 4 TB. Read/write policy, paths and device conditions determine performance; two copies do not imply twice the performance. The earlier 400 MB/s is an independently given throughput, not a specification derived from this capacity example.

If one device in each pair fails and its surviving copy is correct and readable, data remains. Losing both devices in the same pair, or propagating a deletion to both copies, does not guarantee recovery. Availability concerns whether specified requests succeed; durability concerns retained data; backup needs separately preserved past states and a recovery procedure. Independence from common power, controllers, software and networks needs additional assumptions, beyond counting copies.

A worked example, step by step

Use eight cores, storage throughput 400 MB/s and an external link of 1 Gbit/s with the request requirements above. Compare doubling only the cores to 16 and doubling only the network to 2 Gbit/s. Also find logical capacity for four 2 TB devices storing two copies in two pairs.

  1. The CPU bound is 8/0.005=1600 requests/s, and the storage bound is 400/0.5=800 requests/s. Units cancel as required.
  2. 1 Gbit/s=10⁹/8 bytes/s=125 MB/s. The network bound is 125/0.25=500 requests/s, so the overall upper bound is min(1600,800,500)=500.
  3. Doubling only the cores gives bounds 3200/800/500 and leaves the minimum at 500. Doubling only the network gives 1600/800/1000 and a minimum of 800. Neither calculation establishes an achieved rate.
  4. Physical capacity is 4×2=8 TB, and two-copy logical capacity is at most 4 TB. A surviving correct replica is not a guarantee of restoring the state before a deletion.

The base bound is 500 requests/s; doubling only cores still gives 500, and doubling only the network gives 800. Physical/logical capacity is 8/4 TB. No actual performance or recovery trial has been performed.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Keep eight cores, 400 MB/s storage and a 1 Gbit/s link, but each request now uses 0.005 core-seconds, reads 1 MB and transfers 0.25 MB externally. Find the bounds and compare doubling only the network to 2 Gbit/s. If both copies propagate a deletion, do they provide backup?

Show hint

Only the storage requirement doubles: recompute the minimum. Two current copies and a preserved old state are different things.

Show solution

Bounds are 1600/400/500 requests/s, with minimum 400. Doubling the network gives a network bound of 1000, but the minimum remains 400. If deletion reaches both copies and there is no old version or recovery procedure, restoring the earlier state is not guaranteed.

Misconception and counterexample

More cores and physical storage increase every request rate and user storage by the same proportion.

In the example with network bound 500, doubling the cores leaves the overall bound at 500. With two copies, 8 TB physical storage provides 4 TB of logical data. First specify workload, shared paths and replication contracts.

Check your understanding

In these hypothetical units, can 1 Gbit/s be read as 1 GB/s?

Show answer and reasoning

No. It is 125 MB/s.

One byte is eight bits, so the rate is 10⁹/8 bytes/s. Confusing the units inflates the network bound by a factor of eight.

If the throughput bound is 500 requests/s, does every request respond within 2 ms?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Throughput counts completions per time; response time runs from one request’s start to its completion. Sequential resources and waiting times differ.

Do two replicas always restore the state before a user’s deletion?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Deletion can also be replicated. Check separately preserved versions, recovery procedures and access contracts.

Explore further

Derivation: fix effective resource rates c_j>0 and per-request requirements d_j>0, and suppose m identical requests are processed over a sufficiently long interval H. In an ideal steady-state model satisfying m d_j≤H c_j for their required work, m/H≤c_j/d_j. Applying this to every j gives the minimum upper bound. If work is in progress at the start or end, include residual work at the window boundaries, or require bounded residual/H to tend to zero in a long-run limit. Exclude a resource with d_j=0 from this request’s constraints rather than dividing by zero. For integer replication factor r≥1 and ideally balanced physical capacity S, keeping all r copies of the same data gives rD≤S, hence logical data D≤S/r. This conservation relation proves neither performance, failure independence nor the ability to restore a backup.

Subtopic 2.3

Availability zones and regions

With two servers in different availability zones, must login and data reads continue to work?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • A failure domain groups resources that a common cause can affect together. Buildings, power, networks and regions may define such scopes; shared programs and policies also need a stated contract.
  • Let event A mean that a specified path succeeds at a specified time or state. Probability needs a defined sample space and weights, rather than a device count. A∪B means at least one succeeds; A∩B means both succeed.
  • P(A|U) is conditional on common path U functioning. Independence under U means P(A∩B|U)=P(A|U)P(B|U). Readiness check: if copies share a login path that fails, counting copies does not save requests.

Learning goals

  • Explain availability zones and regions within the scope of the cited AWS and Azure definitions.
  • Calculate hypothetical success with a common path and conditionally independent copies.
  • Distinguish placement, replication, routing, maintenance and recovery from user success conditions.

An availability zone has a provider-specific definition

AWS describes a Region as a physical location with multiple availability zones; an availability zone has one or more discrete data centers, with separate facilities and redundant power, networking and connectivity. Azure describes one or more physically separated groups of data centers in a region with independent power, cooling and networking. These definitions are not a universal zone-count or product standard for every company.

Azure distinguishes zonal deployment, where you choose a zone, from zone-redundant service, which distributes across zones. Support depends on service, region, tier and configuration. A region’s zone count, a service’s deployed zone count and replica count need not match. Logical zone numbers across subscriptions need not map to the same physical zone. Choosing a zone does not design application synchronization or user failover.

Include common paths in the success event

In an original hypothetical point-in-time model, common path U functions with probability 0.98. Each readable copy A and B functions with conditional probability 0.99, and they are independent conditional on U. Define S=U∩(A∪B). Declare immediate detection of a failed copy, failover to a healthy copy, that copy holding the latest required data, authorization and sufficient capacity. Actual detection/recovery delays and consistency behavior are outside this formula.

Under U, both copies fail with probability 0.01²=0.0001, so P(S)=0.98×(1−0.0001)=0.979902. Omitting U gives 0.9999 and changes the user-success event. If a common configuration error makes A and B dependent, independent multiplication does not apply. Observed time availability is neither a future probability, an SLA nor a guarantee for every allowed failure. Request-success ratios, time ratios and conditional point probabilities have different denominators.

Placement does not replace operating conditions

During maintenance one copy is offline, changing the conditions for the remaining copy: the two-copy independent 0.99 calculation cannot simply be reused. Check surviving load, failover and the data path. Update order and rollback contracts are not proved by placement alone.

Azure explicitly states that availability zones do not protect against an entire region outage. Multiple regions also do not prove continuity if global authentication, DNS, deployment errors or replication create common dependencies. Define failures, data requirements and recovery windows, then connect placement to procedures. Distance and data location can affect latency and storage constraints; this is neither an actual latency measurement nor a finding of regulatory compliance.

A worked example, step by step

In the hypothetical point model, P(U)=0.98 and P(A|U)=P(B|U)=0.99, with independence conditional on U and S=U∩(A∪B). Calculate success. During maintenance, A is always unavailable; make the new assumption that U and B’s conditional probability remain unchanged.

  1. Under U, each copy fails with probability 0.01. Conditional independence makes joint failure 0.0001.
  2. At least one healthy copy under U has probability 1−0.0001=0.9999. This is still conditional, not whole-user success.
  3. Whole success is 0.98×0.9999=0.979902, or 97.9902%, under the immediate-failover, data and capacity conditions.
  4. During maintenance, S=U∩B, so 0.98×0.99=0.9702, or 97.02%. The two-copy formula no longer applies.

The two-copy model gives 97.9902%; the model with only one copy during maintenance gives 97.02%. These are not measurements of actual provider availability, user SLO or failure-recovery time.

Try it with paper and the lesson

In a new hypothetical example P(U)=0.95. Conditional on U, the four states have probabilities: both healthy 0.85, A only 0.05, B only 0.05, both failed 0.05. Find marginal and whole success probabilities, then compare independence with the same marginals. Does placement alone remove a shared login failure?

Show hint

Sum the table and compute each marginal. Compare actual joint failure with the product under independence, and multiply by P(U) last.

Show solution

The table sums to 1 and each healthy marginal is 0.90. At least one succeeds with conditional probability 0.95, giving whole success 0.95×0.95=0.9025, or 90.25%. Under independence, joint failure would be 0.1²=0.01 and whole success 0.95×0.99=0.9405, or 94.05%, a difference of 3.8 percentage points. The given table is not independent, so 0.9405 is not its answer. Changing zones alone cannot remove a common login path.

Misconception and counterexample

Different availability zones make every failure independent, and multiplying two availability figures gives whole-user success.

If only one of two copies is needed, multiply the probabilities of both failures rather than both successes, and only under the required independence condition. Common U failures or a correlated-state counterexample change the whole probability. Also check the actual service’s zone support, failover and data contracts.

Check your understanding

In the AWS/Azure descriptions, does an availability zone always contain exactly one data center?

Show answer and reasoning

No. These official descriptions allow one or more data centers.

The placement unit called an AZ and the count of individual buildings are not the same definition. Read each other provider’s own definition.

Is 0.9999 in the two-copy example the probability of whole-user success?

Show answer and reasoning

No. It is the probability of at least one functioning copy conditional on U.

Whole success also needs U, giving 0.979902 after multiplying by 0.98.

Do several zones in the same region automatically protect against an entire region outage?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Zones and regions have different failure scopes. Check service support, dependency paths and recovery strategy separately.

Explore further

Derivation: for P(U)>0 and S=U∩(A∪B), conditional probability and complementation give P(S)=P(U)[1−P(Aᶜ∩Bᶜ|U)]. Independence between U and A/B is unnecessary, but replacing joint failure in the brackets with the product of the two marginal failures requires independence under U. If both conditional healthy marginals are a, joint failure lies between 0 and 1−a, so the probability that at least one is healthy lies between a and 1. If the healthy events are exactly identical, A=B, it is a and an extra copy adds no benefit; under independence it is 1−(1−a)². This is an argument about a probability model, not proof that actual geographic separation guarantees independence. Removing the latest-data, authorization, routing or capacity assumptions for a healthy copy requires redefining S itself.
