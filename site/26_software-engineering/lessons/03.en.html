Subtopic 3.1

Stakeholders and interviews

Does quickly building a reservation button solve the problems of people at a library?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • A stakeholder is a person or group connected to using, operating, developing or approving a system, or affected by it. Stakeholders include more than users who directly press a button.
  • Elicitation finds and states needed outcomes and constraints. An interview statement, the author’s interpretation and an agreed requirement are different kinds of evidence.
  • Readiness check: write user, librarian and operations staff as three roles on paper, and imagine one outcome each needs. The following example is hypothetical; no actual person was interviewed.

Learning goals

  • Map stakeholders by role, impact and approval responsibility.
  • Analyze vague needs using neutral questions and specific events.
  • Trace the evidence from statement to interpretation, follow-up question and candidate requirement.

Who needs which outcome?

In a hypothetical small library, users want fewer wasted visits, librarians want to avoid duplicate reservations, and operations staff want to explain reservation status during failures. Approvers may set priorities and budgets but cannot speak for every user experience. A table of lifecycle roles, usage context, information access and impacts reveals requirements missed when only button users are considered.

Do not assume that one user’s statement represents every user’s actual behavior. All statements here are self-contained fictional examples; no recruitment, consent, recording or actual survey took place. Planning actual research requires conditions for participant consent and handling necessary records, but this lesson makes no finding about compliance with a particular law or institutional policy.

Ask about the problem behind a proposed solution

Do not immediately approve fictional statement U1, “Please make the notification button bigger,” as a big-button requirement. Ask when and what outcome was missed and how the last reservation was checked. If the exercise supplies fictional U2, “After the connection dropped, I did not know whether it was accepted, so I pressed again and made a duplicate reservation,” status checking and duplicate prevention become more direct candidate needs.

A neutral question such as “In what order did you check your recent reservation?” does not force an answer. “A bigger button would solve it, right?” predetermines the answer and mixes two problems. Preserve a statement’s original meaning and context, placing interpretation in a separate column. Do not present researchers’ guesses as quotations or actual observations.

Connect analysis to renewed confirmation

Create candidate N1, “I want to check whether a retry made the same reservation,” and consider concrete requirement R1, “Do not create duplicate results for the same user, book and active reservation.” Questions remain: is this per book or per individual copy, may a new reservation follow cancellation, and how do concurrent requests and failure recovery work? Keep candidates and unresolved statuses until those questions are answered.

Build trace links from original statement ID to need ID, requirement ID and acceptance-criterion ID. Confirm interpretations and exceptions with stakeholders again and record conflicts to determine whether agreement exists. Interview count alone does not prove requirements completeness, and majority preferences do not automatically remove constraints such as data preservation and access permissions.

A worked example, step by step

Given fictional U1 requesting a large notification button, U2 describing a retry after connection failure, and librarian S1 saying “The same person reserves the same book twice,” derive candidate requirements and confirmation questions.

  1. U1 proposes a solution. Record the big-button design idea separately from the inferred problem of missing an outcome.
  2. Connect U2 and S1 to a candidate problem of duplicate active reservations. This is information supplied by the exercise, not evidence from actual research.
  3. Write candidate N1 for status checking and R1 for duplicate prevention, leaving the definitions of user, book and active as confirmation questions.
  4. Add questions about reserving again after cancellation and concurrent failures, marking the requirements as not yet approved. U1 alone does not fix the button size.

Candidate problems are unclear acceptance status and duplicate active reservations. Status-checking and duplicate-prevention candidates and identity/cancellation/concurrency questions have been derived, but must not be marked as actual interviews or completed approval.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Given fictional administrator A1, “Give us lots of reports,” and employee E1, “I need to know why earlier reservations were canceled,” write two neutral follow-up questions, one candidate need and one unresolved item.

Show hint

Unpack “lots” into report purpose, reader and decision. Ask separately about creation and access to reason information.

Show solution

Examples: “What records did you recently look for, and for which decision?” “Who records cancellation reasons, and who needs to view them?” A candidate need is checking reasons needed for a decision. Record fields, access permissions and actual frequency of need remain unconfirmed. Increasing report count alone does not guarantee meeting the need.

Misconception and counterexample

Writing down what one customer who makes the most requests says completes the requirements.

Different operations, support, direct-user and approval roles can expose missed constraints. A button proposal versus a duplicate-retry problem is another counterexample. Statement count proves neither representativeness, consistency nor feasibility.

Check your understanding

May a fictional interview statement be recorded as an actual user observation?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

That changes how the information was produced and the scope of its evidence.

Is “A bigger button would be convenient, right?” a neutral question?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

It leads toward a solution and a positive answer. Ask separately about past events and purposes.

If the cancellation exception in candidate R1 is unresolved, may it be labeled an approved requirement?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Interpretation, exceptions and approval status must be checked and traced.

Explore further

Extension: let finite need set N and requirement set R have trace relation T⊆N×R. If every n has a linked r, formal need coverage is 100%. However, an r linked only by name may fail to meet n’s actual outcome, so totality of this relation is insufficient for semantic satisfaction. Review link rationale, conditions and outcomes. Conversely, an unlinked requirement may have a new rationale such as risk response or an external constraint; check the kind of rationale rather than deleting it automatically.

Subtopic 3.2

Functional and nonfunctional requirements

If a reservation can be made, are requirements met even when it is slow or duplicated?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • A functional requirement states what the system must do under which conditions. A quality requirement specifies degrees and constraints such as performance and reliability for that behavior. Content and verification conditions matter more than classification labels.
  • One millisecond, ms, is 1/1000 second. Timing needs start/end events, load and environment. Server processing and completion on a user’s screen are different intervals.
  • Readiness check: a successful reservation and finishing within 500 ms answer different questions. One can be true while the other is false.

Learning goals

  • Distinguish behavioral requirements, quality requirements and design constraints in context.
  • State premises, outcomes, time intervals and denominators measurably.
  • Construct counterexamples separating functional success from performance or permission conditions.

State both what happens and how well

Hypothetical R1 says that an authenticated user requesting an available book receives exactly one active reservation ID. Hypothetical Q1 says that, among 100 valid requests in a defined environment, at least 95 obtain a correct result within 500 ms. Functional requirements alone leave expectations about time, failure and duplication to guesswork; quality requirements alone may count fast but incorrect results as successes.

Functional/nonfunctional are project classifications. Rejecting unauthorized requests may also be a concrete function serving a security quality goal, so do not assume two mutually exclusive baskets. An item specifying an implementation choice such as PostgreSQL is a design constraint, distinct from an outcome-based requirement. Record a justified external constraint, but do not turn a user’s proposal alone into a mandatory implementation.

Fix conditions and observation boundaries

Q1’s original hypothetical test specification fixes the same server configuration, a dataset of 1000 books, one client making 100 serial valid requests, and distinct reservation conditions for each. Measure from the client sending a request to receiving its correct result. Do not retry; include errors and timeouts in the denominator as slow failures. Since the interval includes the network, server processing time alone cannot replace it. This requirement example has not been actually tested.

The ≤500 ms boundary includes 500, and 95/100 means an inclusive “at least 95%.” It is not the same as average time≤500 ms. With 94 requests at 100 ms and six at 1000 ms, the average is 154 ms but the 95% condition fails. With 95 at 500 ms and five at 10000 ms, the 95% condition passes but the average is 975 ms. Passing a finite sample does not guarantee a distribution under every future load.

Preconditions and failures are requirements too

State R1’s normal premises, such as availability, authentication and absence of duplicates, separately from the retry rule returning the existing ID when an active reservation already exists, and the no-state-change requirement for missing books, denied permissions or internal failure. Removing failures from the denominator and counting only successes changes Q1 favorably. Reservation identity scope and ordering/atomicity of concurrent events need separate agreement.

Check functional correctness through inputs, outputs and changed state; check timing quality through the observation time of that same correct result. Being measurable does not prove that a numeric threshold is needed. The 500 ms and 95% values are candidates for fictional stakeholder review; accessibility, recovery and load environments may need separate requirements. This applies NASA’s public checklist as an example, rather than judging compliance with a paid quality-classification standard.

A worked example, step by step

For Q1, a hypothetical test table of 100 correct results gives 94 at 400 ms, one at 500 ms and five at 900 ms. Assume every functional result is correct and calculate timing acceptance.

  1. The denominator is the specified 100 requests, assuming no missing or duplicate rows. Do not replace it with a subset of successful results.
  2. There are 94+1=95 results at ≤500 ms. Include the one exactly at 500 ms.
  3. 95/100=95%, so the at-least-95% condition passes. The average is (94×400+500+5×900)/100=426 ms.
  4. The 426 ms average is an additional calculation; 95% is the decision criterion. This fictional table is not an actual application or network performance measurement.

The hypothetical table passes Q1. Functional correctness is a separate premise supplied by the problem, not verification of future loads or every user experience.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Compare fictional table A: 99 requests at 100 ms plus one at 10000 ms, and B: 94 at 100 ms plus six at 1000 ms. Calculate Q1’s ratios and averages, and decide whether the two criteria are equivalent.

Show hint

Write the count of fast requests and the sum of all times in separate columns.

Show solution

A passes at 99%, with average 199 ms. B fails at 94%, with average 154 ms. Despite its smaller average, B fails the 95% condition. The criteria are not equivalent; do not turn a sample’s pass into a guarantee about the actual population.

Misconception and counterexample

Nonfunctional requirements can stay as “fast and safe,” and passing functional tests is enough.

The 94%/154 ms counterexample shows that the selected timing criterion changes the decision. Define interval, load, failures, denominator and boundaries for “fast,” and check correct reservation results and timing separately.

Check your understanding

Does Q1 count 500 ms as a fast request?

Show answer and reasoning

Yes.

The criterion was defined as ≤500 ms.

Must rejecting unauthorized requests always be classified as nonfunctional?

Show answer and reasoning

No. It can also be a function implementing a security goal.

Classification may overlap with context or level, but behavior and verification must remain clear.

Is Q1 unchanged if only successful results remain in the denominator and errors are removed?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

That changes the contract which also includes errors among the original 100 valid requests.

Explore further

Extension: if functional predicate F and pure timing predicate Q_time are both required on valid input set D, acceptance is F∧Q_time. A correct but slow implementation may have F true/Q_time false; an implementation quickly returning an incorrect ID may have F false/Q_time true. Checking only one is insufficient for the combined requirement. Q1 in the explanation also includes a correct-result condition, so fast errors must count as failures. Distinguish this combined Q1 from Q_time, which considers only time. Evaluating fictional-table predicates is neither fitting an actual timing distribution nor product certification.

Subtopic 3.3

Scope and acceptance criteria

If every test is green, have all promises for this version been kept?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Scope specifies which outcomes, functions and environments this work includes and excludes. Exclusion means the boundary of the current agreement, not permanent lack of need.
  • An acceptance criterion is an observation condition for deciding whether a requirement is satisfied. Link requirement IDs to test/analysis IDs without confusing test count with satisfaction.
  • Readiness check: draw a boundary that includes reservation creation and lookup now and excludes payment. Connecting creation with lookup is a contract inside the included scope.

Learning goals

  • State boundaries for inclusion/exclusion, assumptions, dependencies and changes.
  • Create acceptance criteria in terms of state, behavior and observed outcomes.
  • Distinguish trace coverage, passing cases, overall correctness and confirmation of needs.

Scope fixes the system’s promises

Hypothetical version V1 includes book-ID lookup, authenticated users’ reservation creation/cancellation, and lookup of their own reservation status. It excludes payment, delivery and integration with external libraries. Record dependency on an external authentication service, while separately assigning responsibility for reservation access checks. External dependence does not automatically exclude accepting incorrect authentication information from responsibility.

State the assumption that the target is an active reservation for one book ID and actual allocation of physical copies is excluded. Changing the meaning of book ID or active also changes creation, duplication and lookup criteria. Record the fixed requirement version together with change rationale, impact and approval status to avoid secretly loosening criteria later to obtain a pass.

Observe both state and results

Hypothetical AC1: if authenticated user u has no active reservation for book b and b is reservable, completing creation returns one reservation ID r and exactly one (r,u,b,active) entry appears in the user’s own list. AC2: if an active reservation for the same u,b already exists, return the same r without increasing the active count. Each statement includes input, prior state, action, result and state change.

Set criteria for failure and cancellation too. If an unauthorized user tries to view someone else’s reservation, return the specified rejection without changing state. A completed cancellation is no longer active, and the next creation obtains a new ID. These sequential cases do not guarantee concurrent requests or persistence; leave them as separate requirements/tests. Do not report checking every exception after testing only one normal case.

The scope of acceptance evidence

The original sequential paper model tracks active count from initial 0 to first creation 1, retry 1, cancellation 0 and new creation 1, showing the expected AC1/AC2 states. Checking only the return string success could pass an implementation that creates two duplicates. Observe both result IDs and state counts; do not alter IDs to make comparing retry returns difficult.

Passing a fixed finite set of acceptance cases gives evidence for those cases. It proves neither correctness over unlimited inputs, faults, concurrency or future environments, nor actual suitability for users’ needs. Separate verification against a requirement from confirming whether that requirement defines the needed outcome. Paper calculation checks, independent document review and actual user testing are different evidence.

A worked example, step by step

In a fictional state table, u,b have no active reservation. Creation returns new r1, a retry under the same conditions returns r1, cancellation makes r1 inactive, and creation again returns new r2. Trace the four sequential events.

  1. First creation returns r1, active(r1)=true and active count 1. AC1’s result and state match.
  2. Retry returns r1 without creating a new entry. Keeping count 1 is central to AC2.
  3. After cancellation, r1 is inactive and active count is 0. Remaining history differs from an active reservation.
  4. New creation returns r2 and active count 1. The new-ID contract requires r2≠r1. History has two entries r1,r2, but only one is active.

Returns are r1,r1,cancellation confirmation,r2; active counts are 1,1,0,1. This is an original paper acceptance model, not actual database/service/persistence/concurrency testing.

Try it with paper and the lesson

An incorrect implementation returns r1 on first creation and r2 on retry, keeping both active. Could checking only success in the result pass it? Name two observations for AC2. Is missing payment a V1 failure?

Show hint

State count and ID equality differ from a success string. Compare excluded scope with requirements.

Show solution

Checking only success could incorrectly pass it. Comparing the returned ID with existing r1 and checking that active count remains 1 exposes the violation. Payment is explicitly excluded from V1, so its absence alone does not violate V1. If a new requirement includes it, evaluate against the changed version.

Misconception and counterexample

Passing 100 tests verifies all requirements and the actual usage purpose.

If all 100 check only normal creation, they miss duplicate retries or permission failures. Check scope, trace links and observations and record the cases covered by passing tests. Actual user testing must be separate; it has not been performed here.

Check your understanding

Does success satisfy AC2 even if there are two active entries after retry?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Both the same ID and an unchanged count were required.

Does remaining cancellation history necessarily mean active count is still 1?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Existence of history and an active state are different conditions.

If a new requirement adds payment and old V1 tests pass, is the new scope verified too?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Review changed requirements, criteria and test links again.

Explore further

Extension: for finite requirement set R and case set T with link relation C⊆R×T, one linked case for each requirement gives 100% link coverage. If cases do not actually discriminate the requirement, or their inputs cover only part of it, overall satisfaction does not follow. An AC2 oracle checking only success is a counterexample. A stronger argument requires proving that input partitions cover every required case and that generalization within each partition holds. For the sequential reservation-state model, induction can show active count per pair is 0 or 1 and every defined transition preserves it; this does not automatically give atomicity to an actual concurrent database implementation.
