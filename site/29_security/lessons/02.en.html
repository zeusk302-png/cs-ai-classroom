Subtopic 2.1

Passwords, MFA and sessions

Does asking for a password twice make an authentication process use two factors?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • A principal is an entity, such as an account or service, to which permissions are granted. Authentication checks control of the authenticators associated with the claimed principal.
  • A secret is a value that must remain undisclosed. A hash function transforms an input into a fixed-length value; matching hashes are not a mechanism for recovering the original input.
  • A session is state linking successive interactions after authentication. Calculate time differences t−s using the same clock basis and units.

Learning goals

  • Distinguish the types of authentication factors from the number of questions asked.
  • Evaluate overall session lifetime and inactivity expiration separately.

The contract for checking a password

The server uses password-verification information bound to an account. Instead of storing the password in plaintext, it verifies it with a dedicated password-hashing scheme that includes a salt and cost parameter. A salt makes sharing precomputed guesses across accounts more difficult; it need not be secret in the way the password must be. A hash does not eliminate guessing candidates for a weak password.

A successful authentication gives evidence of control of that account’s authenticators. Permission to modify a particular document is a separate authorization question. Public resources may be accessible without authentication, so it does not follow that every resource requires a login.

Two factors and risks after authentication

Factors are different kinds of evidence, such as something known, something possessed and a physical characteristic. A password and a PIN are both knowledge factors: two questions do not give two different factors. A password combined with verification of a separate possession factor can provide two factors through an appropriate combined procedure. Biometrics must also be considered in terms of their integration with an actual authenticator and verification procedure.

The name MFA alone guarantees neither phishing resistance nor protection from session theft. A bearer session secret issued after authentication allows access to whoever presents it, so an attacker who steals it may also use it. Design protected channels, protection of session secrets, expiration and reauthentication alongside the initial authentication.

Two clocks in a paper session

Our fictional server uses monotonic time measured in whole minutes. On successful authentication, set created=last=t and valid=true. A session check succeeds only if valid is true, 0≤t−created<12 and 0≤t−last<5. Only a successful check updates last=t; a failed check sets valid=false. This is our own policy, not a set of NIST timeout values.

Activity does not extend the overall lifetime; it resets only inactivity time. Logout sets valid=false. Equality is excluded at the boundary, so exactly 5 or 12 minutes causes failure. This sequential model excludes clock reversal and simultaneous checks. Actual distributed servers need further contracts for clocks, atomicity and recovery.

A worked example, step by step

Create a session after two-factor authentication at t=0. Check it successively at t=4,9,10. Trace each result and last under the policy above.

  1. At t=0, set created=last=0 and valid=true. Successful authentication does not itself grant permission to modify every resource.
  2. At t=4, the overall age is 4<12 and inactivity is 4<5. The check succeeds and last becomes 4.
  3. At t=9, the overall age is 9<12, but inactivity is 9−4=5, which fails the strict bound. The check fails and valid becomes false.
  4. At t=10, presenting the secret again still fails because valid=false. The last successful time, last=4, remains unchanged.

The checks succeed, fail and fail; last=4. Restarting a session must be the result of a new authentication procedure.

Try it with paper and the lesson

For a new session with created=last=0, tabulate checks at t=4,8,12. Also explain whether a password plus a PIN constitutes two factors.

Show hint

Update only last after each success. At the final check, examine overall lifetime first.

Show solution

At t=4 the check succeeds and last=4; at t=8 it succeeds and last=8; at t=12 it fails because overall age is 12. A password plus a PIN provides two knowledge secrets, not two different factor types.

Misconception and counterexample

Once MFA has succeeded, leaking the session secret is safe.

With a bearer session, someone presenting a stolen secret may be allowed access. Strengthening initial authentication and protecting the subsequent session are separate boundaries.

Check your understanding

Are a password and a PIN two different factor types?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Both demonstrate knowledge of a value.

A valid session has created=0 and last=8. What happens at t=12?

Show answer and reasoning

The check fails.

Inactivity of 4 is allowed, but overall age 12 does not satisfy <12.

Does successful authentication alone allow modification of someone else’s document?

Show answer and reasoning

Not without separate permission.

Checking the account and authorizing a principal-object-action combination are different decisions.

Explore further

The session invariant is created≤last≤t after a successful check, and created does not change without new authentication. At creation, created=last=t, so it holds. Before a successful check, t≥last≥created; setting last to the current t preserves the inequalities. Failure and logout leave created and last unchanged and do not allow further successful checks. This induction applies only to our sequential model with monotonic time. Resetting the lifetime through reauthentication requires specifying the new authentication strength and the policy for invalidating the old session. The fictional timetable alone does not establish compliance with NIST’s actual scope or AAL-specific requirements.

Subtopic 2.2

Access control, roles and capabilities

Does the label “teacher” allow someone to change every student’s grade?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • An object is a protected resource such as a document or record. An action is an operation, such as read or update, on that resource.
  • R′⊆R means that every element of R′ also belongs to R. For example, {read}⊆{read,update} is true.
  • A role groups common permissions granted to multiple principals. An ACL is a list attached to an object identifying permissions for principals.

Learning goals

  • Specify an allow condition that includes principal, object, action and context.
  • Compare role-based permission with reducing a capability’s authority.

Who may do what?

Access control compares a request’s principal u, object o, action a and context with policy P. A default-deny model allows a request only when an explicit allow condition is true. Distinguish owner or role strings supplied in a request from trusted server information. An object identifier that is difficult to guess cannot replace permission checking.

In our fictional school, students may read their own grades but may not update them. Teachers may read or update grades in their assigned class. Thus the policy needs ownership or class relationships as well as roles. After authenticating the principal, the server retrieves the actual object’s class to decide.

Roles and per-object lists

RBAC manages common rules through user-to-role and role-to-action relationships. Without an object scope, users with the same role may obtain access across class boundaries. An ACL lists which principals may perform which actions on a particular object. These are policy representations, not names that automatically guarantee correct implementation.

In this model, student s may read when owner(o)=s, and teacher T may read or update when class(o)=assigned(T). Deny all other cases. Every entry point for a protected operation must apply this decision. Hiding a button in the interface does not enforce the server policy.

Capabilities and decreasing authority

A capability is an unforgeable token authorizing operations on a particular object. Here we assume an abstract token whose object identifier, permission set and expiration are verified as issued by a trusted issuer. Merely writing permissions in ordinary JSON does not make it unforgeable. In a bearer scheme, possession conveys authority, so transfer and storage are also policy concerns.

In an issuance procedure allowing attenuation, derive (o,R′,e′) from (o,R,e) only when R′⊆R and e′≤e. The verifier checks a valid issuance/delegation chain and the current time t<e′. Every action allowed by the attenuated token therefore belongs to the original permissions. The proof depends on disallowing arbitrary string edits that change the object or add rights.

A worked example, step by step

Student s owns grade record X. Teacher T is assigned class A. X belongs to class A and Y to class B. Evaluate s/read/X, s/update/X, T/update/Y and attenuation of capability C=(X,{read,update},10) to C′=(X,{read},8).

  1. Allow s/read/X because owner(X)=s.
  2. Deny s/update/X because the student rule does not allow action update.
  3. Deny T/update/Y because class(Y)=B≠A. The teacher role alone is insufficient.
  4. C′ has the same object, a subset of permissions and expiration 8≤10, so it is a valid attenuation candidate. After validating the trusted issuance chain, allow read at t=7 and deny it at t=8 due to expiration. Here t denotes time, distinct from teacher T.

Allow, deny and deny the first three requests. Reading through a capability also requires token verification and the time condition.

Try it with paper and the lesson

From C=(X,{read},10), which of (X,{read,update},9), (Y,{read},9) and (X,{read},11) is a valid attenuation candidate?

Show hint

Check all three conditions: permission inclusion, identical object and nonincreasing expiration.

Show solution

None is valid. The first adds update, the second changes the object and the third extends expiration. An unchanged token or (X,{read},9) satisfies these conditions as a candidate, but the issuance chain must also be verified.

Misconception and counterexample

Hiding the object ID or checking the teacher role completes access control.

A teacher who learns an object ID from another class passes the role check but violates the class relationship. Check the scope at the server point where the object is actually used.

Check your understanding

What if X changes owner to another student before s/read/X?

Show answer and reasoning

Deny the request.

The current condition owner(X)=s is false.

What property does R′⊆R prove about attenuation?

Show answer and reasoning

Actions allowed by the attenuated token are included in the original permissions.

If a∈R′, the definition of subset gives a∈R.

Is any file listing permissions a capability?

Show answer and reasoning

Not by itself.

It needs an enforcement basis such as unforgeable issuance and verification or a protected object reference.

Explore further

Repeated attenuation maintains R2⊆R1⊆R0 and e2≤e1≤e0. Indeed, a∈R2 implies a∈R1 and then a∈R0; t<e2 implies both t<e1 and t<e0. Assuming that each step preserves the same object and a valid issuance chain, repeat the argument to show that any finite delegation chain creates neither additional action rights nor a longer expiration. These inclusions do not by themselves solve theft of the original token, violation of delegation restrictions, changes in the object’s meaning or propagation of revocation; those need separate contracts.

Subtopic 2.3

Privilege escalation and auditing

Does a denial log alone identify the attacker with certainty?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Privilege escalation acquires access wider than the policy allows. Vertical escalation gains a higher role; horizontal escalation accesses another person’s resources at the same level.
  • An audit record supports later tracing of observed events. A recorded principal identifier is not complete proof of the actual human actor.
  • An atomic transition is one model step in which another transition cannot intervene. A policy version identifies the rules used for a decision.

Learning goals

  • Distinguish request-supplied fields from trusted role information.
  • Explain the check-use boundary and the scope of evidence in logs.

Escalation versus an authorized permission change

Assigning a teacher another class through an approved process is a legitimate permission change. In contrast, a server that trusts a client’s role=admin field to permit administrator actions enables vertical privilege escalation. Accessing another student’s grades rather than one’s own is horizontal escalation and violates the same principal-object-action policy.

Our defensive model uses the server principal obtained through authentication and the server’s current roles and relationships. Granting permissions itself needs a policy specifying who may grant which scope to whom. Confusing permission management with ordinary document editing undermines least privilege.

The gap between checking and using

Permission may be revoked after a check succeeds but before the write occurs. A policy requiring permission at check time differs from one requiring it at use time. This unit chooses use-time permission and combines checking the current version and modifying the object into one atomic transition. If revocation happens first, the later write is denied.

Reading a version v does not itself create atomicity. Actual implementations need synchronization, a transaction or conflict handling based on version comparison, together with a failure contract. Our sequential atomic table explains the contract’s meaning; it is not a performance or concurrency-safety test of a database or operating system.

What an audit can tell us

Specify a format recording request identifier, observed principal, object, action, allow/deny result, reason and policy version. Recording a raw password or bearer token turns the log into another resource for secret leakage. Prevent uncritically recorded newlines in input from appearing as fabricated events.

Restricted access to records, tamper detection and retention policies improve evidential reliability, but records alone neither prevent violations nor identify an offender conclusively. An account may have been stolen and events outside the observation point may be missing. Timestamps from different machines alone do not establish a complete causal order; the example’s sequence numbers describe observation order at a single server.

A worked example, step by step

Under policy v0, student s cannot modify X. Process the following requests and permission changes atomically in order: s requests modifying X with role=admin; a trusted administrator grants s permission to modify X (v1); s modifies X; the administrator revokes permission (v2), after which s again requests modifying X. Use only server information for actual roles.

  1. Do not adopt the client’s role field as authority. Deny the first request under v0 and leave X unchanged.
  2. A grant transition by an administrator with policy-management permission produces v1. This is an authorized permission change.
  3. Allow s’s next modification under current v1. Its successful audit event records principal s, object X, action update and version v1.
  4. Deny the modification after revocation has completed as v2. This server’s audit order and policy versions show the decision basis but do not prove that the person named s directly sent the request.

Deny, perform an authorized grant, allow, then deny after revocation. A failed modification leaves X unchanged in this model.

Try it with paper and the lesson

A request passes a check under v1, then waits while revocation to v2 completes first. Compare writing based on the cached check with an atomic recheck at use time. Explain whether the password belongs in a log.

Show hint

This unit uses a use-time permission policy. Separate a past check from the current allow condition.

Show solution

Trusting only the cached result may allow a write that violates the current policy. An atomic recheck under v2 denies it. Do not put the password directly in the log; use minimal information such as request ID and decision reason to meet the audit purpose.

Misconception and counterexample

An audit log prevents permission violations and conclusively identifies the actual actor.

A wrongly allowed request can still appear in a log, so recording differs from prevention. Even a recorded principal ID for a stolen account does not establish that the account owner was the actual actor; further evidence is needed.

Check your understanding

Is a request field role=admin evidence of administrator authority?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Check trusted server policy and principal relationships.

Does a use-time policy make a write safe if it relies only on a check performed before revocation?

Show answer and reasoning

That does not guarantee the contract.

It fails to account for policy changes between check and use.

Must a raw bearer token be recorded in the audit log?

Show answer and reasoning

Do not record it directly.

Someone reading the token may abuse the session, making the audit log a leakage path.

Explore further

The protected-write invariant is: “Each successful transition’s principal, object and action were allowed by the policy version used in that transition.” Initially no successful records exist, so the condition holds vacuously. If the next transition is a denial or policy change, it adds no successful write and does not alter earlier version-specific decisions. If it is a successful write, the same atomic transition permits it only after passing the current policy check; the new record therefore satisfies the condition too. Induction establishes the invariant provided every entry point uses the same decision and check-use is atomic. Later revocation does not retroactively invalidate an earlier write. Whether audit-storage failure denies a sensitive write or sends a record to a separate queue is a service contract. Without atomicity and durability for recording and writing, ordinary function calls alone do not establish that both succeeded together.
