Subtopic 4.1

Testing for Exactness

Can changes in two coordinate directions be combined into the change of a single function?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • For an unknown function y(x), the derivative y′ is its instantaneous rate of change with x. M(x,y)+N(x,y)y′=0 is a relation that y and y′ must satisfy. At N=0, one must not automatically divide to write y′=−M/N.
  • The partial derivative M_y differentiates M with respect to y while holding x fixed. C¹ means that the function and its first partial derivatives are continuous. For a C² function, mixed partial derivatives may be interchanged.
  • An open region contains a small disk around each of its points. Here a simply connected region is a connected open region in which every closed curve can be contracted to a point without leaving the region. An open rectangle is an example.
  • The total differential dΦ=Φ_x dx+Φ_y dy gives the first-order change in Φ(x,y) due to both coordinate changes. A line integral substitutes a curve x(t),y(t) into Mx′+Ny′ and integrates with respect to t.

Learning goals

  • Define an exact equation by the existence of a potential and check the assumptions of the partial-derivative test.
  • Distinguish local from global potentials and calculate a counterexample on a region with a hole.

The definition is the existence of a potential

Suppose M,N are given on an open region D. The form M dx+N dy is exact if there is a function Φ defined throughout D with Φ_x=M and Φ_y=N. We call Φ a potential function. To interpret M dx+N dy=0 as an equation for a function, a C¹ function y whose graph lies in D must satisfy M(x,y(x))+N(x,y(x))y′(x)=0.

For example, Φ=x²y+3x+2y² has Φ_x=2xy+3 and Φ_y=x²+4y. Thus the sum of the two terms is dΦ, and Φ stays constant along a solution. Starting from this definition shows why equality of partial derivatives at just one point and exactness throughout a region are different claims.

The test includes the region and continuity

If M,N∈C¹(D) and a potential exists, then Φ is C², so M_y=N_x holds at every point of D. This necessary condition remains valid when the region has a hole. Conversely, on an open rectangle, if M_y=N_x holds everywhere, a potential can be constructed. On a general open simply connected region, C¹ regularity and this identity are also sufficient for a global potential.

Around any point of an open region we can choose a small rectangle. Hence, if C¹ regularity and M_y=N_x hold in that neighborhood, we obtain a local potential there. Simple connectivity of the entire region is a sufficient condition for extending this local result to a global result. Do not reverse the statement and claim that every exact form requires a simply connected region.

A loop around a hole reveals the difference

On D=ℝ²∖{(0,0)}, take M=−y/(x²+y²) and N=x/(x²+y²). Direct differentiation gives M_y=N_x=(y²−x²)/(x²+y²)². Yet on the unit circle x=cos t, y=sin t, 0≤t≤2π, we have Mx′+Ny′=sin²t+cos²t=1, so the line integral is 2π. A global potential would make this closed-path integral the difference between the endpoint and starting values, namely 0, a contradiction.

On the half-plane x>0, differentiating Φ=arctan(y/x) gives the same M,N. This is a potential on a restricted region, illustrating the local distinction. Meanwhile, on the same punctured plane, (x/(x²+y²),y/(x²+y²)) is the gradient of Φ=ln√(x²+y²). A hole prevents the partial-derivative test alone from guaranteeing global exactness, but does not forbid every potential.

A worked example, step by step

On ℝ², test whether M=2xy+3, N=x²+4y is exact and find a potential to verify the result.

  1. M,N are polynomials, so they are C¹ on ℝ². The plane is an open simply connected region, so the global sufficient condition applies.
  2. Differentiating M with respect to y gives M_y=2x, and differentiating N with respect to x gives N_x=2x. The identity holds throughout the region.
  3. Integrating M with respect to x gives Φ=x²y+3x+g(y). Matching Φ_y=x²+g′(y) to N gives g′=4y, so we can choose g=2y².
  4. Check again that Φ_x=2xy+3=M and Φ_y=x²+4y=N. Thus Φ=x²y+3x+2y² is a global potential.

The form is exact. Adding a constant to the potential gives the same total differential. Choosing branches that are actual function solutions is addressed in the next subtopic.

Try it with paper and the lesson

On ℝ², let M=3x²+2y. Test N₁=2x+2y and N₂=3x+2y separately, and find a potential in the exact case.

Show hint

Compare M_y with each N_x. In the exact case, integrate M with respect to x and add a function of y alone.

Show solution

M_y=2 and (N₁)_x=2, so the first form is exact. Taking Φ=x³+2xy+y² gives Φ_x=3x²+2y and Φ_y=2x+2y. For the second form, (N₂)_x=3≠2, so it is not exact on ℝ²; this discrepancy persists on every nonempty open subregion.

Misconception and counterexample

Checking M_y=N_x guarantees a single global potential on every region.

The rotational field on the punctured plane has equal mixed partials and a closed-path integral of 2π. C¹ regularity and the identity give a local result; a global conclusion requires a sufficient condition such as simple connectivity or a separate check of path independence.

Check your understanding

If M_y=N_x holds at only one point, can we conclude exactness in a neighborhood of that point?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

The required identity must hold throughout an open neighborhood. For example, M=y, N=x² has equal partial derivatives only at x=1/2, not throughout a neighborhood.

For an exact form with C¹ coefficients, is M_y=N_x necessary even on a region with a hole?

Show answer and reasoning

Yes.

The first partial derivatives of the potential are C¹, so the potential is C² and its mixed partials can be interchanged.

Is Φ=ln√(x²+y²) a global function on D=ℝ²∖{0}?

Show answer and reasoning

Yes.

The radius is positive throughout D and the real logarithm is single-valued. A field having this gradient is exact even though the region has a hole.

Explore further

Prove sufficiency directly on a rectangle. Let D=I×J be an open rectangle, (x₀,y₀)∈D, M,N∈C¹(D), and M_y=N_x. Define Φ(x,y)=∫(x₀→x)M(s,y₀)ds+∫(y₀→y)N(x,t)dt. The integration paths remain in D, and continuous partial derivatives on the finite intervals allow differentiation under the second integral with respect to x. Then Φ_y=N(x,y), and Φ_x=M(x,y₀)+∫(y₀→y)N_x(x,t)dt=M(x,y₀)+∫(y₀→y)M_y(x,t)dt=M(x,y). This explicitly constructs the potential. Extension to general simply connected regions depends on a path-independence theorem; the MIT text explains this connection using Green's theorem. The punctured-plane counterexample in the main text was checked independently by parametrizing a circle.

Subtopic 4.2

Finding a Potential Function

Why can the constant left after integration be a function of y?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • When integrating with respect to x, hold y fixed. Adding any function g(y) of y alone to an antiderivative of ∂Φ/∂x=M does not change its x partial derivative.
  • A level set Φ(x,y)=C consists of all points where Φ has that value. A function solution y(x) is a C¹ graph in this set; an entire circle or vertical line need not be a single y(x).
  • The chain rule gives dΦ(x,y(x))/dx=Φ_x+Φ_y y′. By the mean value theorem, a function with zero derivative on an open connected interval is constant.
  • The sufficient condition from the implicit function theorem used here is Φ∈C¹ and Φ_y≠0 at the initial point. Near that point, the level set is then a unique local C¹ graph y(x).

Learning goals

  • Integrate in one coordinate and determine the remaining function using the other partial derivative.
  • Use an initial point to choose the level value and check the local branch and domain of an actual function solution.

A function of the other variable remains

On a rectangle where M,N satisfy the exactness condition, first integrate Φ_x=M. Write Φ=A(x,y)+g(y) and match Φ_y=A_y+g′(y)=N. If N−A_y is a function of y alone, integrate it to obtain g. Finally check that differentiating the resulting Φ actually recovers both M and N.

Alternatively, integrate N with respect to y and start with Φ=B(x,y)+h(x). The two potentials differ by a constant on a connected region. Difficulty in performing an integral does not prove nonexactness. Exactness concerns the existence of a potential, not our ability to express it with simple elementary functions.

Initial conditions and the potential constant

Once a potential is found, an initial point (x₀,y₀) chooses C=Φ(x₀,y₀). Adding K to Φ also shifts the level value to C+K, representing the same set. Distinguish the freedom to add a constant to a potential from the level value that selects different solution curves.

If Φ_y=N is nonzero at the initial point, the implicit function theorem gives a C¹ function solution near that point. On that branch, y′=−M/N. This is a sufficient condition for obtaining a local graph; N=0 does not mean the potential calculation is wrong. The geometry at the initial point and the equation M+Ny′=0 must be examined separately.

A level set contains solution branches but is not itself a single branch

The level set Φ=x²+y², C=1 is a circle. Its upper and lower branches are y=±√(1−x²), −1<x<1, but the entire circle cannot be expressed as a single-valued y(x). At (1,0), N=2y=0 and M=2x=2, so no y(x) solution with a finite derivative can include that point.

The level set Φ=x²+y², C=0 contains just one point. It is not a function solution on a nonempty open x interval. Parametrized curves can also handle vertical directions. Specify whether the original equation asks for y(x) or allows x(t),y(t), because the kind of solution differs.

A worked example, step by step

For (2xy+1)dx+(x²+2y)dy=0 on ℝ² with y(1)=1, find a potential and the slope of the function solution near the initial point.

  1. M_y=2x=N_x and the coefficients are C¹, so a global potential exists. Integrating Φ_x=2xy+1 gives Φ=x²y+x+g(y).
  2. Matching Φ_y=x²+g′(y) to x²+2y gives g′=2y. Choose g=y², obtaining Φ=x²y+x+y².
  3. At the initial point, C=Φ(1,1)=1+1+1=3. Thus the local solution branch satisfies x²y+x+y²=3.
  4. Since N(1,1)=3≠0, a local C¹ branch exists. Its slope is y′(1)=−(2·1·1+1)/(1²+2·1)=−1. Differentiate Φ again to check both original coefficients.

The potential is Φ=x²y+x+y², the initial level value is 3, and the initial slope is −1. This concerns a branch near the initial point, not a claim that the entire level set is one function.

Try it with paper and the lesson

For (2x+y)dx+(x+2y)dy=0, find the level value and local slope through (1,0). What changes if 7 is added to the potential?

Show hint

Start with Φ=x²+xy+g(y). Check whether N vanishes at the initial point.

Show solution

From Φ_y=x+g′(y)=x+2y, choose g=y². Then Φ=x²+xy+y², C=1, and N(1,0)=1≠0. The slope y′(1) is −2. Using Φ+7 changes the level value to 8, but Φ+7=8 is the same set as Φ=1, so the branch and slope are unchanged.

Misconception and counterexample

After obtaining Φ=C, every point of that set can be included in one function solution.

The upper and lower circle branches give different y values for the same x. At its vertical tangent (1,0), no finite y′ satisfies M+Ny′=0. Check the initial point, graph condition, differentiability, and domain as well.

Check your understanding

Why write the integration constant as g(y) when integrating Φ_x=M?

Show answer and reasoning

A function of y alone has zero x partial derivative.

We must also match Φ_y=N; restricting the addition to a numerical constant could lose the required y dependence.

What does Φ_y=N≠0 at the initial point guarantee?

Show answer and reasoning

The level set near that point can be represented as a C¹ graph y(x).

This is a sufficient condition from the implicit function theorem, not a guarantee of a global branch or existence for every x.

If Φ and Ψ have the same gradient on a connected open region, what kind of function is Φ−Ψ?

Show answer and reasoning

A constant function.

Both partial derivatives of their difference vanish, so there is no change along a piecewise smooth path inside the region. Any two points of a connected open region can be connected by such a path.

Explore further

Prove the equivalence between a function solution and a constant level value. Assume Φ∈C¹(D), Φ_x=M, Φ_y=N, and that the graph of y∈C¹(I) lies in D. If y solves the original equation, the chain rule gives dΦ(x,y(x))/dx=M+Ny′=0. Since I is a connected interval, the mean value theorem gives Φ(x,y(x))=C. Conversely, if this composite function is constant, the same chain rule gives M+Ny′=0, so y is a solution. The converse already assumes a C¹ graph; obtaining a graph from the entire level set is a separate job for implicit-function conditions. If Φ and Ψ have the same gradient, H=Φ−Ψ has zero gradient. Connect two points of the connected open region by a piecewise C¹ path. On each piece, dH/dt=0, and continuity at the joins gives equal H values at the two points. On a region with multiple connected components, different constants may be added on different components.

Subtopic 4.3

Bernoulli and Homogeneous Substitutions

Can an apparently nonlinear equation become linear or separable after changing variables?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • For z=y(x)^β, the chain rule gives z′=βy^(β−1)y′ on an interval where that real power is differentiable. For arbitrary real β, a safe definition on y>0 is y^β=exp(β ln y).
  • A first-order linear equation z′+P(x)z=Q(x) can be solved with an integrating factor on an open interval where P,Q are continuous. After the substitution, check that the solution z is in the allowed range of the inverse substitution.
  • Dividing by H(v)−v during separation can lose constant v values for which this expression is zero. Substitute the zero cases into the original equation before dividing.
  • When y=xv, the product rule gives y′=v+xv′. The ratio v=y/x is defined only for x≠0. Start on a positive interval for a positive initial x, or a negative interval for a negative initial x.

Learning goals

  • Derive the Bernoulli substitution and explain the real-power domain and lost zero solutions.
  • Check a substitution and its inverse for a ratio-homogeneous equation, preserving constant-ratio solutions.

Why the Bernoulli substitution makes the equation linear

Consider y′+p(x)y=q(x)y^α on an open interval I where p,q are continuous. For α=0, define the right-hand side to be the function q(x); the equation is linear. For α=1 it is y′+(p−q)y=0. When α≠0,1, first choose a branch with y>0 so arbitrary real powers and their derivatives have clear meanings. Set z=y^(1−α); then z′=(1−α)y^(−α)y′.

Multiplying the original equation by (1−α)y^(−α) gives z′+(1−α)pz=(1−α)q. Conversely, a positive solution z of this linear equation gives y=z^(1/(1−α)), which solves the original equation. On the positive range the transformation has an inverse and a nonzero derivative. Negative branches can also be possible for powers such as integer powers, but their domains and inverse branches must be chosen separately.

Solutions and intervals lost through division

For α=2, y=0 solves the original equation but cannot be represented by z=1/y. Finding all nonzero solutions differs from finding all solutions of the original equation. If α>0 and the original equation is extended continuously using 0^α=0, it has the zero solution. For α<0 this extension is generally undefined. Treat α=0 separately as the linear equation with right-hand side q(x), without evaluating 0^0.

After inverse substitution, exclude points where a denominator vanishes or a real power is not allowed. For instance, y=1/(1+Ceˣ) with C<0 cannot pass through a zero of its denominator. Also, if an equation with a noninteger exponent loses uniqueness at zero, waiting solutions may join the zero solution. A formula for nonzero branches alone therefore does not establish all solutions.

Ratio homogeneity has a different meaning

If the right-hand side depends only on a ratio, as in y′=H(y/x), use v=y/x and y=xv where x≠0. Substitution gives xv′=H(v)−v. On a branch with H(v)−v≠0, separation gives ∫dv/(H(v)−v)=ln|x|+C. A constant satisfying H(v₀)=v₀ gives a separate solution y=v₀x that would be lost through division.

Here homogeneous refers to a ratio structure unchanged by allowed simultaneous scaling (x,y)↦(λx,λy). In a linear homogeneous equation y′+p(x)y=0, homogeneous means that the forcing term is zero. The equation y′=(y/x)² is ratio-homogeneous but nonlinear. Preserve the domain of H for the ratio and an interval of fixed sign for x throughout the substitution.

A worked example, step by step

Solve y′+y=y², y(0)=1/2 on a positive branch, and also check constant solutions lost through substitution.

  1. Since α=2, set z=1/y. We have z′=−y′/y²; multiplying the original equation by −1/y² gives z′−z=−1. This step requires y≠0.
  2. The integrating factor is e^(−x). Thus (e^(−x)z)′=−e^(−x), giving e^(−x)z=e^(−x)+C and z=1+Ceˣ.
  3. The initial value z(0)=2 gives C=1. Since z=1+eˣ>0, the function y=1/(1+eˣ) is positive and defined for every real x.
  4. Substitute y′=−eˣ/(1+eˣ)² to obtain y′+y=1/(1+eˣ)²=y², with y(0)=1/2. The original equation also has y=0, lost through substitution. Its other constant solution y=1 is included by C=0.

The initial-value solution is y=1/(1+eˣ), x∈ℝ. The constant solutions 0 and 1 were checked separately. Near the initial value 1/2, local Lipschitz continuity of the polynomial right-hand side gives uniqueness; the displayed solution exists on the entire real interval.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Solve the ratio-homogeneous equation y′=1+y/x, y(1)=2 using v=y/x, and decide whether the original equation can be used at x=0.

Show hint

From v+xv′=1+v obtain xv′=1. Since the initial x is 1, use ln x on x>0.

Show solution

Since v′=1/x, v=ln x+C. From v(1)=2 get C=2 and y=x(ln x+2), x>0. Its derivative is y′=ln x+3=1+y/x, and the initial value is correct. Although y tends to 0 as x→0+, the original ratio y/x is undefined at x=0, so this is not a solution including that point.

Misconception and counterexample

Inverting every real solution of the transformed linear equation gives every solution of the original equation.

When arbitrary real powers are defined on a positive branch, linear solutions with z≤0 cannot be used in the inverse substitution. The substitution z=1/y does not represent the zero solution. A ratio substitution excludes x=0, and division by H(v)−v may lose straight-line solutions. Check each exclusion and each zero case in the original equation.

Check your understanding

For α=1 in y′+py=qy, is z=y^(1−α) a useful substitution?

Show answer and reasoning

No. Solve y′+(p−q)y=0 directly.

This substitution sends all y to z=1 and has no inverse.

Why does the z=1/y formula for y′+y=y² miss y=0?

Show answer and reasoning

Because 1/y is undefined at zero.

Check the zero solution by direct substitution; no finite z recovers it.

For y′=(y/x)² on x>0, which straight-line solutions have constant ratio v?

Show answer and reasoning

y=0 and y=x.

The equation xv′=v²−v requires v(v−1)=0 for constant v. Preserve both cases before division.

Explore further

Check both directions of the positive-branch equivalence. Let α≠1, let p,q be continuous on I, and let z∈C¹(I) be positive and satisfy z′+(1−α)pz=(1−α)q. Define y=z^(1/(1−α))>0. Then z=y^(1−α) and z′=(1−α)y^(−α)y′. Substitute into the linear equation and divide by the nonzero factor (1−α)y^(−α) to obtain y′+py=qy^α. The forward direction performs the same calculation by multiplication. On an interval where x≠0, v=y/x and y=xv are also inverse transformations; the product rule makes the original ratio equation equivalent to xv′=H(v)−v. However, the separated integral applies only on intervals where H(v)−v≠0. If H(v)=v identically, every constant v is allowed, giving y=Cx, and the integral that divides by zero cannot be used. For the original Bernoulli example, the C=−1 branch is y=1/(1−eˣ), defined only on (−∞,0) and (0,∞). One-sided limits of the denominator show that no finite continuous extension exists at x=0.

Subtopic 4.4

Choosing an Integrating Factor

What can be multiplied into a nonexact equation to make it exact while preserving its solutions?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • The function μ(x,y) multiplies the given coefficients M,N. It must be nonzero throughout the region under consideration so that we can divide back. C¹ means continuity of μ and its first partial derivatives.
  • The product rule gives (μM)_y=μ_y M+μM_y. If μ depends only on x, μ_y=0; if it depends only on y, μ_x=0.
  • An antiderivative A of a continuous function P satisfies A′=P. The function μ=exp A satisfies μ′=Pμ and never vanishes. Fixing an integration base point reduces unnecessary constant freedom.
  • The ratio (M_y−N_x)/N requires N≠0; (N_x−M_y)/M requires M≠0. Exclude zero-denominator points from the formula's range, then check them separately with the original product condition.

Learning goals

  • Derive one-variable integrating-factor formulas from the product rule and check their conditions.
  • Use counterexamples to explain why nonvanishing and domain restrictions matter for preserving solutions.

The multiplied equation must have the same solutions

Suppose M,N∈C¹(D) and μ∈C¹(D) never vanishes on D. If μM dx+μN dy is actually exact, μ is called an integrating factor. For function solutions, μ(M+Ny′)=0 and M+Ny′=0 are equivalent at the same points, so C¹ solutions in the same region are preserved. If μ introduces a singularity, this equivalence applies only on a region excluding that point.

Expanding the necessary mixed-partial condition gives μ_y M+μM_y=μ_x N+μN_x, or Nμ_x−Mμ_y=(M_y−N_x)μ. Satisfying it does not automatically give a global potential on an arbitrary region with holes. Use the formulas below on an open rectangle, or separately check a stronger global sufficient condition or an actual potential.

Sufficient conditions for an x-only or y-only factor

On an open rectangle I×J, if N≠0 and (M_y−N_x)/N=P(x) is a continuous function of x alone, choose μ(x)=exp(∫(x₀→x)P(s)ds). The relation μ′=Pμ satisfies the mixed-partial condition; μ is nonzero, so the multiplied equation is exact. To include a point where the denominator vanishes, do not rely only on cancellation in the ratio: recheck the actual derivative identity and regularity of μM,μN.

Similarly, if M≠0 and (N_x−M_y)/M=Q(y) is a continuous function of y alone, use μ(y)=exp(∫(y₀→y)Q(t)dt). The signs differ between these cases, so derive the formulas by the product rule rather than memorizing their order. Failure of a ratio to depend on one variable alone means this simple search fails, not that all integrating factors are absent.

More general factors and the connection to linear equations

Take M=N=e^(−xy). The two ratios are y−x and x−y, so neither meets its one-variable formula on a rectangle. Nevertheless, multiplying by μ=e^(xy) gives dx+dy and the global potential Φ=x+y. An integrating factor depending on both variables clearly exists, so one-variable failure is not a nonexistence test.

The first-order linear equation y′+p(x)y=q(x) can be written with M=p(x)y−q(x), N=1, giving (M_y−N_x)/N=p(x). Hence the familiar exp(∫p dx) is a special case of the x integrating factor derived here. Whichever method is used, check the multiplied coefficients' partial derivatives, the potential, and then whether the result satisfies the original equation.

A worked example, step by step

On ℝ², find an x integrating factor that makes y dx+dy=0 exact, and obtain its function solutions.

  1. For M=y, N=1, we have M_y=1, N_x=0. The original form is not exact, but N≠0 and (M_y−N_x)/N=1 depends on x alone.
  2. Choose μ=eˣ. Then μ′=μ and μ never vanishes. The multiplied coefficients are eˣy and eˣ, with (eˣy)_y=eˣ=(eˣ)_x.
  3. Integrating Φ_x=eˣy gives Φ=eˣy+g(y). From Φ_y=eˣ+g′(y)=eˣ get g′=0, so choose Φ=eˣy.
  4. From Φ=C obtain y=Ce^(−x). Its derivative y′=−Ce^(−x) gives y+y′=0. Division by μ excludes no points, and the original equation holds for every real x.

The factor is μ=eˣ, the potential is Φ=eˣy, and the function solutions are y=Ce^(−x), x∈ℝ. C is any real constant, including the zero solution.

Try it with paper and the lesson

For dx+xy dy=0 on ℝ², find an integrating factor depending only on y and a potential. Here we seek the potential, without assuming the entire level set is a single y(x).

Show hint

Since M=1, N=xy, the ratio (N_x−M_y)/M is y. Multiply by μ, then integrate Φ_x.

Show solution

The factor μ=e^(y²/2) is positive everywhere. Both (μM)_y and (μN)_x equal y e^(y²/2), so the form is exact. Taking Φ=x e^(y²/2) gives Φ_x=μ and Φ_y=xyμ. The level set is x e^(y²/2)=C; a local function branch can be chosen at an initial point with N=xy≠0. At N=0, no finite y′ can satisfy the original equation 1+xyy′=0.

Misconception and counterexample

Multiplying by any function that makes the equation exact preserves the original solutions.

The zero function μ=0 makes every equation vanish and allows every function as a solution. More concretely, dx=0 has no C¹ graph solution y(x), since it requires 1=0, whereas multiplying by μ=y gives y dx=0, admitting y(x)=0. A vanishing factor can create new solutions. A factor such as μ=1/y removes the zero solution y=0 of y′=0 from the domain.

Check your understanding

What denominator condition is required to use (M_y−N_x)/N in the μ(x) formula?

Show answer and reasoning

N≠0 throughout the region in question.

It is needed to define the ratio after division by N. To extend to zeros, check the original product-derivative equation separately.

If both one-variable ratios depend on x and y, can we conclude that no integrating factor exists?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

For M=N=e^(−xy), the one-variable formulas fail, but μ=e^(xy) makes the form dx+dy.

What ratio gives the x integrating factor for y′+py=q?

Show answer and reasoning

p(x).

Since M=py−q and N=1, M_y=p, N_x=0. Thus μ=exp(∫p dx) satisfies the same product-derivative condition.

Explore further

Prove sufficiency and preservation of solutions. On an open rectangle, let M,N∈C¹, N≠0, let P(x)=(M_y−N_x)/N be continuous, and set μ=exp(∫P). Then μ_y=0 and μ_x=Pμ, giving (μN)_x=μ_xN+μN_x=μ(PN+N_x)=μM_y=(μM)_y. The multiplied coefficients are C¹, so the rectangle exactness theorem provides a potential. For the y-only case, Q=(N_x−M_y)/M and μ_y=Qμ give (μM)_y=μ(QM+M_y)=μN_x=(μN)_x. With nonzero μ on the same domain, μ(M+Ny′)=0 ⇔ M+Ny′=0 at each point, so the set of C¹ graph solutions is exactly preserved. The same argument applies to parametrized curves through μ(Mx′+Ny′)=0. If the factor requires restricting the domain, equivalence holds only on that restricted region. A general two-variable factor must solve Nμ_x−Mμ_y=(M_y−N_x)μ, a partial-differential relation; this unit does not claim a theorem deciding all global existence cases.
