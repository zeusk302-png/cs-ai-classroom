Subtopic 1.1

Order and linearity of ODEs

If we know the rule governing a quantity’s rate of change, can we determine its future values?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Functions: y(t) assigns one state value y to each time t.
  • Derivative review: y′(t)=limₕ→₀[y(t+h)−y(t)]/h is an instantaneous rate. For y=t², the difference quotient 2t+h has limit 2t, so y′=2t; differentiating again gives y″=2.
  • Exponential review: (e^(kt))′=k e^(kt). If t has time units and k inverse-time units, kt is dimensionless.

Learning goals

  • Explain why an ODE’s unknown is a function.
  • Determine the highest derivative order separately from linearity.
  • State a model’s variables, units, and interval of application.

The answer is a function, not just a number

An ordinary differential equation (ODE) relates an unknown function y of one independent variable t to its derivatives. The equation y′=−2y gives a proportional rate rule: larger current states decrease faster. It does not immediately supply one value of y. A solution is a function differentiable to the required order on a specified interval and satisfying the equation at every t there.

For y=e^(−2t), y′=−2e^(−2t)=−2y, so it is a solution. So are y=3e^(−2t) and y=0. Partial differential equations (PDEs), with multiple independent variables such as space and time, belong to another topic. ODEs may also form systems with several unknown functions.

Check order and linearity separately

Order is the highest order of a derivative of the unknown function appearing in the equation. y″+3y′+y=0 is second order; (y′)²+y=0 is first order. Do not confuse a power of a derivative with derivative order. If a coefficient vanishes at a particular t, examine the normal-form properties at that point separately.

A linear ODE has the form aₙ(t)y^(n)+⋯+a₁(t)y′+a₀(t)y=g(t). The unknown function and its derivatives appear to the first power, are not multiplied together, and have coefficients depending on t. Thus t²y′+sin(t)y=1 is linear, whereas y′+y²=0, yy′=1, and sin(y)+y′=0 are nonlinear. “Linear” does not mean the solution’s time graph is a straight line.

Model assumptions and dimensions

If a state decreases at a rate proportional to its current amount, with constant proportionality, model it by y′=−ky, k≥0. If y is measured in mg and t in hours (h), y′ has units mg/h and k has units 1/h, so the dimensions agree. State which factors, such as inflow, external forcing, or saturation, have been omitted.

Linearity matters to solution structure. Linear combinations of solutions of a homogeneous linear equation g=0 are solutions again. Simply adding two solutions with the same nonzero forcing g instead produces right-hand side 2g. Also check that the leading coefficient aₙ is nonzero on the interval before dividing to obtain a normal form solved for the highest derivative.

A worked example, step by step

Classify order, linearity, and homogeneity for (a)y″+2ty′+y=t, (b)y′=y², and (c)t y′+y=0. For (c), also discuss the normal-form condition near t=0.

  1. In (a), the highest derivative is y″, so the equation is second order. All y terms are first-degree with coefficients depending only on t, making it linear. Its right-hand side t is not the zero function, so it is nonhomogeneous.
  2. In (b), the highest derivative is y′, so it is first order. The term y² makes it nonlinear; do not directly apply the homogeneous/nonhomogeneous classification of linear equations.
  3. Equation (c) is first-order, linear, and homogeneous, but its leading coefficient t vanishes at zero.
  4. On an interval where t≠0, normalize it to y′=−y/t. That normal form is undefined at t=0.

(a) Second-order linear nonhomogeneous. (b) First-order nonlinear. (c) First-order linear homogeneous, with care needed when normalizing at t=0.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Differentiate y(t)=4e^(−3t) to check y′=−3y. If y is in mg and t in h, state the units attached to 3.

Show hint

The chain rule produces the derivative factor −3.

Show solution

y′=−12e^(−3t)=−3y, so the function solves the equation for every real t. In a physical model, read the rate constant as 3 h⁻¹; the exponent −3t is dimensionless, and usually choose t≥0 as the application interval.

Misconception and counterexample

A linear ODE always has straight-line solution curves.

The ODE y′=−2y is linear, but y=e^(−2t) is a curved graph. Linearity concerns how the unknown function and its derivatives occur in the equation, rather than the shape of its graph against time.

Check your understanding

What is the order of (y′)²+y=0?

Show answer and reasoning

First order.

The square is a power of a derivative; the highest derivative is still y′.

Is t²y″+y=sin t linear?

Show answer and reasoning

Yes.

Its coefficients and right-hand side depend on t; the y terms appear to the first power. Normalizing at t=0 is a separate issue.

Is y′+y²=0 homogeneous linear because its right-hand side is zero?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

The y² term makes it nonlinear. First establish linearity before applying this classification.

Subtopic 1.2

General solutions, particular solutions, and initial values

Can quantities following the same decay rule trace different curves because they start at different amounts?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Antiderivative review: if y′=2t, then y=t²+C, since the derivative of C is zero.
  • Initial values: y(t₀)=y₀ means that the graph passes through (t₀,y₀).
  • Definite-integral review: for continuous f, ∫ₜ₀ᵗ f(s)ds accumulates change, and its derivative is f(t).

Learning goals

  • Distinguish a solution family from a solution selected by initial conditions.
  • Check the differential equation and initial conditions separately.
  • Explain why a regular nth-order linear equation needs n initial values, and state the limits of that rule.

Constants disappear under differentiation

On a connected interval, the solutions of y′=2t are y=t²+C. For any solution y, (y−t²)′=0, so the mean value theorem makes y−t² constant. This shows that the general solution contains every solution, rather than merely listing some examples.

A general solution is a family parameterized by constants; a particular solution fixes their values. Merely writing one constant does not guarantee that all exceptional solutions of a nonlinear equation are included. Check whether steps such as division have excluded a zero solution.

Two checks for an initial-value problem

An ODE together with y(t₀)=y₀ forms an initial-value problem. In y=t²+C, the condition y(1)=5 gives C=4. The proposed y=t²+4 must satisfy both y′=2t and the initial condition. Meeting only one is insufficient.

If y′=f(t) with continuous f, construct y(t)=y₀+∫ₜ₀ᵗf(s)ds. Differentiation verifies the ODE, while the integral is zero at t=t₀, verifying the initial value. This separates the initial amount from accumulated change and extends to integral equations for general ODEs.

Decay families and higher-order initial values

For y′=−ky, set z=e^(kt)y. Then z′=e^(kt)(y′+ky)=0, giving y=Ce^(−kt). This derivation never divides by y, so it includes y=0. If y(t₀)=y₀, then C=y₀e^(kt₀), and the solution is y₀e^(−k(t−t₀)).

For a regular nth-order linear equation with continuous coefficients and a nonzero leading coefficient, specifying y,y′,…,y^(n−1) at the same t₀ selects a unique solution. For y″=0, the family y=At+B retains A if only position is given; giving the slope fixes both constants. For general nonlinear equations, the number of conditions alone does not guarantee existence or uniqueness; the next section supplies conditions.

A worked example, step by step

Solve y′=−2y, y(0)=6, and express y(1) exactly. Assume time has been made dimensionless.

  1. (e^(2t)y)′=e^(2t)(y′+2y)=0, so e^(2t)y=C.
  2. In the general solution y=Ce^(−2t), setting t=0 gives y(0)=C=6.
  3. The selected function y=6e^(−2t) has derivative −12e^(−2t), equal to −2y.
  4. The initial value is 6e⁰=6, and at t=1, y(1)=6e⁻².

The initial-value solution is y=6e^(−2t), with y(1)=6e⁻². This is not a reported numerical run or an actual decay experiment.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Solve y″=0, y(0)=3, y′(0)=−2 on paper. Compare with giving only one initial condition.

Show hint

Integrate in succession: y′=A and y=At+B.

Show solution

B=3 and A=−2, so y=3−2t. It satisfies y″=0, y(0)=3, and y′(0)=−2. Giving only y(0)=3 leaves y=At+3 with arbitrary A, hence multiple solutions.

Misconception and counterexample

One initial value makes the solution of every differential equation unique.

y″=0, y(0)=0 admits infinitely many solutions y=At. Even a first-order nonlinear equation without a Lipschitz condition may have multiple solutions at the same initial value. Check order and regularity together.

Check your understanding

Which particular solution of y′=2t has y(2)=7?

Show answer and reasoning

y=t²+3.

4+C=7 gives C=3, and differentiation gives 2t.

In Ce^(−2t), which solution has y(0)=0?

Show answer and reasoning

y=0.

C=0 satisfies the ODE. A derivation dividing by y could miss this solution.

Is satisfying the ODE enough if the initial value is wrong?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Both the differential equation and the additional condition must hold.

Explore further

Go further: in the family A cos t+B sin t solving y″=−y, the conditions y(0)=a,y′(0)=b give A=a,B=b. Directly check the derivatives and initial values. Distinguish this check from showing that the family includes every solution, which additionally requires justification from linear existence and uniqueness.

Subtopic 1.3

Direction fields and solution curves

Without a solution formula, can slopes at individual points help us anticipate the shape of a curve?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Slope: positive Δy/Δt rises to the right; negative Δy/Δt falls.
  • Derivative: a solution curve’s tangent slope is y′(t).
  • Tables: calculate f(t,y) at each (t,y) position. The two coordinates t and y have different roles.

Learning goals

  • Represent y′=f(t,y) by a short slope segment at each point.
  • Explain equilibrium lines and increasing/decreasing directions algebraically.
  • Distinguish a direction-field sketch, numerical approximation, and exact-solution verification.

Draw the rule as small tangents

A direction field for y′=f(t,y) places a short segment of slope f(t,y) at each (t,y). The segment is not itself a solution; it indicates the tangent direction a solution through that point must have. Segment lengths may be normalized for readability: the slope ratio is what matters.

For f(t,y)=t−y, the slopes at (0,1),(1,0),(1,1) are −1,1,0 respectively. Calculate grid values in a table before drawing them. Different axis scales change the displayed angles, so label numeric slopes too.

Repeated rows and equilibria in autonomous equations

An equation such as y′=2−y, with no t on the right-hand side, is autonomous. At the same y height, slopes agree for every t. At y=2 the slope is zero, and the constant function y(t)=2 satisfies the ODE, making it an equilibrium solution. Slopes are positive below 2 and negative above 2.

Passing through a zero-slope point does not necessarily mean the solution is constant. In the nonautonomous equation y′=t, every slope at t=0 is horizontal, yet y=t²/2+C later changes slope. A constant function y=c is a solution only if f(t,c)=0 for every t on the interval.

Separate curves, approximations, and proofs

Sketching a smooth curve from an initial point along the slope segments suggests behavior. When uniqueness holds for a first-order normal form, distinct solutions cannot cross at the same (t,y) point. If uniqueness conditions fail, this visual intuition is not guaranteed.

One Euler step, yₙ₊₁=yₙ+h f(tₙ,yₙ), approximates the slope as constant over a small interval. A direction field alone proves neither exact numeric values nor existence for all time. Differentiate a proposed formula to check the ODE; compare approximations with exact solutions and report errors separately.

A worked example, step by step

For y′=2−y, y(0)=0, calculate directions on the grid t=0,1,2 and y=0,1,2,3. Check y=2−2e^(−t).

  1. In every t column, the slopes at heights y=0,1,2,3 are respectively 2,1,0,−1.
  2. From (0,0), anticipate a curve rising toward y=2.
  3. The proposed function has derivative 2e^(−t); the right-hand side is 2−(2−2e^(−t))=2e^(−t).
  4. y(0)=0. For t≥0, e^(−t)>0 gives y<2 and y′>0. As t→∞, e^(−t)→0 gives y→2.

The height-wise slope pattern (2,1,0,−1) repeats. The exact solution increases but does not reach equilibrium 2 at any finite t.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Starting from this initial value, calculate two Euler steps with h=1/2 by hand. Compare with the exact value 2−2/e at t=1 and determine which is larger.

Show hint

At each step, recalculate the slope 2−y from the current y.

Show solution

First, y₁=0+(1/2)×2=1 at t₁=1/2. Then y₂=1+(1/2)×(2−1)=3/2 at t₂=1. The exact value is 2−2/e. Since e<4, 2−2/e<3/2. The difference is 3/2−(2−2/e)=2/e−1/2>0. These are paper calculations, not a numerical-program execution.

Misconception and counterexample

Every zero-slope point is an equilibrium where the curve stops.

For y′=t, the slope at (0,y₀) is zero, but y=y₀+t²/2 increases for t>0. An equilibrium is a constant function satisfying the equation throughout an interval, not just one horizontal tangent.

Check your understanding

For y′=2−y, what is the slope at y=4?

Show answer and reasoning

−2.

Substitute the current state 4 into 2−y.

What is the equilibrium solution of the same equation?

Show answer and reasoning

y(t)=2.

For every t, its derivative satisfies 0=2−2.

Do two computed Euler points prove an exact solution?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Holding the tangent slope constant at each step is an approximation, unlike checking a function’s derivative throughout the equation.

Explore further

Go further: for y′=2−y with initial value a, the solution is 2+(a−2)e^(−t). Use signs to prove approach to 2 from below when a<2 and from above when a>2. Solutions with a≠b differ by (a−b)e^(−t), which is nonzero at every finite time, so they do not cross.

Subtopic 1.4

Conditions for existence and uniqueness

Can the same initial point and the same rate rule yield two different future curves?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Continuity review: small input changes do not cause sudden output jumps. Continuity alone does not always bound rates of change.
  • Intervals: local means a small interval around the initial time; global means the entire time range of interest.
  • Lipschitz review: |f(t,y₁)−f(t,y₂)|≤L|y₁−y₂| bounds differences in outputs by a fixed multiple of differences in states.

Learning goals

  • State precisely the local conclusion guaranteed by continuity and local Lipschitz conditions.
  • Explain the proof idea by converting the ODE to an integral equation.
  • Directly check counterexamples to uniqueness and to existence for all time.

The theorem and its conditions

Suppose f(t,y) is continuous on an open region containing (t₀,y₀) and locally Lipschitz in the y direction. Then y′=f(t,y), y(t₀)=y₀ has a unique solution on some interval around the initial time. This is the scalar Picard–Lindelöf theorem. Differentiability in time is not itself required. Here local Lipschitz in the state direction means a common constant L bounds state differences throughout a chosen small time–state rectangle; it is distinct from choosing separate constants at each time.

A useful sufficient condition is continuity of f and ∂f/∂y near the initial point. If ∂f/∂y is bounded on a small rectangle, the mean value theorem gives a Lipschitz constant L. Failure of this sufficient condition does not imply failure of uniqueness. Although f=|y| is not differentiable at y=0, ||y₁|−|y₂||≤|y₁−y₂| makes it Lipschitz.

Integral equations and the contraction argument

An ODE solution satisfies y(t)=y₀+∫ₜ₀ᵗf(s,y(s))ds. Conversely, if f is continuous and a continuous function satisfies this integral equation, the fundamental theorem of calculus makes it differentiable and verifies the ODE and initial value. This equivalence starts the existence-and-uniqueness proof.

Choose a closed rectangle centered at the initial point, |t−t₀|≤a, |y−y₀|≤b, lying entirely inside the domain, with |f|≤M and Lipschitz constant L. Choose 0<h≤a, Mh≤b, and Lh<1. Then T[y](t)=y₀+∫ₜ₀ᵗf(s,y(s))ds maps continuous functions staying in that strip back into it, and sup|T[u]−T[v]|≤Lh sup|u−v|. Iterating T reduces errors geometrically and converges to a fixed point, the solution. This is a proof outline using a complete space of continuous functions and the contraction mapping theorem; the full proof of that auxiliary theorem belongs to advanced analysis.

Continuity and global existence are different

Continuity of f can guarantee at least one local solution through the general local existence theorem for continuous right-hand sides, but uniqueness needs additional control. The equation y′=2√|y|, y(0)=0 has both y=0 and delayed-start solutions. Its right-hand side is continuous, but |f(0,ε)−f(0,0)|/ε=2/√ε becomes arbitrarily large for positive ε approaching zero, so it is not Lipschitz near zero.

Even smooth f and its y derivative do not guarantee a solution for all time. For y′=y², y(0)=1, the solution 1/(1−t) blows up as t→1⁻. The local theorem does not prevent blow-up. Also, assigning t₀=0 in y′=1/t fails to provide a domain for applying the theorem, since the right-hand side is undefined there.

A worked example, step by step

For y′=2√|y|, y(0)=0, check that for each a≥0, yₐ(t)=0 for t≤a and yₐ(t)=(t−a)² for t≥a is a solution. Determine whether the initial-value problem is unique.

  1. For t<a, yₐ=0, yₐ′=0, and the right-hand side is also zero.
  2. For t>a, yₐ′=2(t−a). Since t−a>0, the right-hand side 2√((t−a)²)=2(t−a) agrees.
  3. At t=a, both function values are zero and the derivative limits agree at zero; the derivative exists and is zero. The right-hand side is also zero.
  4. Since a≥0, every yₐ(0)=0. For a=0 and a=1, the values at t=1/2 are respectively 1/4 and 0.

Distinct solutions satisfy the same initial value, so uniqueness fails. Continuity alone cannot justify uniqueness.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Substitute y(t)=1/(1−t) into y′=y², y(0)=1. State the maximal interval containing the initial point and explain the problem at t=1.

Show hint

Find where the denominator vanishes, and compare the derivative 1/(1−t)² with y².

Show solution

y′=(1−t)⁻²=y², and y(0)=1. The maximal connected interval containing zero is (−∞,1). As t→1⁻, y→∞, so no real-valued continuous solution can extend through 1. The smooth right-hand side f=y² guarantees local uniqueness, but not a global solution.

Misconception and counterexample

If a theorem’s sufficient condition fails, there must be no solution or multiple solutions.

For f=|y|, ∂f/∂y fails to exist at zero, but the Lipschitz constant 1 guarantees the unique solution y=0 for initial value zero. Failure of one sufficient-condition test is logically different from negating the conclusion.

Check your understanding

What time range does the basic Picard–Lindelöf conclusion guarantee?

Show answer and reasoning

Some small interval around the initial time.

It is local existence and uniqueness; existence for all time needs additional conditions.

What is a y-direction Lipschitz constant for f(t,y)=3y?

Show answer and reasoning

L=3.

|3y₁−3y₂|=3|y₁−y₂|.

Since y′=y² is smooth, must its solution with y(0)=1 exist through t=2?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

1/(1−t) blows up at t=1 and the solution from the initial point cannot be extended past that time.

Explore further

Go further: start Picard iteration for y′=3y, y(0)=1 with y₀(t)=1. This gives y₁=1+3t, y₂=1+3t+(3t)²/2!, and y₃=1+3t+(3t)²/2!+(3t)³/3!. Check each by integration. These finite polynomials are approximations; their limit e^(3t) is the exact solution. Distinguish convergence on the short interval used in the contraction proof from verifying the solution formula for all time.
