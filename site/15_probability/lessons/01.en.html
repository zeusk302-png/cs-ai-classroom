Subtopic 1.1

Sample spaces and events

If two coin tosses are recorded as 0, 1, or 2 heads, does each of these three results have probability 1/3?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • A set collects elements. In {H, T}, H∈Ω means that H is an element of Ω. A subset selects some or all of the elements.
  • Quick review: the fraction 3/4 represents three of four equally weighted cells. If the cells have different weights, add their weights rather than count them.
  • A function is a rule assigning one output to each input. The head-count function X assigns 1 to both HT and TH. This unit requires no differentiation or integration.

Learning goals

  • Define the recording rule for an experiment before writing its sample space.
  • Express events as subsets and with unions, intersections, and complements.
  • Distinguish grouping outcomes from assuming equal probabilities.

Distinguish outcomes before assigning probabilities

A probability experiment models a situation whose outcome is not yet known. Before making a philosophical judgment about whether a physical object is random, decide what counts as one trial and what to record. When the first and second tosses are distinguished, HT and TH are different records. The sample space Ω is the set of all possible outcomes under this recording rule; each outcome ω is a sample point.

Here we exclude a coin landing on its edge and assume that every toss yields H or T, giving Ω={HH, HT, TH, TT}. If other outcomes occur meaningfully in the actual apparatus, revise the sample space and recording rule. Listing the sample space does not by itself make all outcomes equally probable.

An event collects the outcomes for which a condition is true

An event A collects the sample points that answer a question with yes. At least one head is A={HH, HT, TH}; a head on the first toss is B={HH, HT}; exactly one head is C={HT, TH}. A∩B contains outcomes satisfying both conditions, A∪B those satisfying at least one, and Aᶜ=Ω∖A those for which A is false. Unless stated otherwise, the word or in probability includes the case where both conditions hold.

For finite Ω, every subset can be used as an event. A general probability model is written (Ω,ℱ, P), where ℱ is the family of allowed events. A family containing Ω and closed under complements and countable unions is called a σ-algebra. This avoids the difficulties of unconditionally assigning probabilities to every subset in continuous models. In this unit’s finite problems, we only use the fact that ℱ consists of all subsets of Ω.

Compressing a record does not remove its original weights

Under the additional assumption of two independent fair coin tosses, each ordered pair has probability 1/4. Thus A has probability 1/4+1/4+1/4=3/4. If we instead record the head count X, the new space is Ω′={0,1,2}, but these outcomes group 1, 2, and 1 original points, so their probabilities are 1/4, 1/2, and 1/4.

In general, a new event D has probability P(X∈D)=P({ω:X(ω)∈D}). Read this as finding the corresponding event back in the original space. If the tosses are independent and each has head probability 3/4, HH, HT, TH, and TT have weights 9/16, 3/16, 3/16, and 1/16. The set descriptions of events remain the same while their probabilities change.

A worked example, step by step

Toss a fair coin twice in order, independently. Let A mean at least one head and B mean a head on the first toss. Find A∩B and Aᶜ and their probabilities.

  1. Write the four points of Ω as HH, HT, TH, TT, and label each with 1/4.
  2. A={HH, HT, TH} and B={HH, HT}, so A∩B={HH, HT}.
  3. Aᶜ={TT}, obtained by removing the three points of A from the four-point space.
  4. Add the weights: P(A∩B)=2/4=1/2, P(Aᶜ)=1/4, and P(A)=3/4.

The probability that both events hold is 1/2, and the probability of no heads is 1/4.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Write the four points on paper and color C=exactly one head and D=a head on the second toss. Find C∪D and C∩D and calculate their probabilities under fairness and independence.

Show hint

C={HT, TH} and D={TH, HH}. Check the second letter and do not count the same point twice.

Show solution

D={HH, TH}. C∪D={HH, HT, TH}, with probability 3/4; C∩D={TH}, with probability 1/4. Preserve the order of the letters to avoid confusing HT with TH.

Misconception and counterexample

If a sample space has three kinds of results, each has probability 1/3.

The head counts 0, 1, and 2 group the original four ordered pairs into groups of sizes 1, 2, and 1. Even under fairness and independence, their probabilities are 1/4, 1/2, and 1/4. The number of outcome names and the weights producing those outcomes are separate matters.

Check your understanding

What is the difference between an event and a sample point?

Show answer and reasoning

A sample point is one complete record; an event is a set of records satisfying a condition.

For example, HT is a point and {HT, TH} is an event. A set containing just one point is also an event.

For two independent tosses, each with head probability 3/4, what is the probability of at least one head?

Show answer and reasoning

15/16.

The complementary event TT has probability (1/4)²=1/16, so take the remainder.

Does knowing only P(HT)=P(TH) make all four points equally probable?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

The weights 9/16, 3/16, 3/16, 1/16 satisfy HT=TH but are not all equal.

Explore further

Check σ-algebra closure on the small space Ω={a, b, c}. The family ℱ={∅,{a},{b, c},Ω} is closed under complements and countable unions but does not include {b} as an observable event. It corresponds to a recording device that cannot distinguish b from c. A more precise sensor can change the event family.

Subtopic 1.2

Probability axioms and counting

Why can simply adding probabilities for a union produce a value greater than 1?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Quick review: A∩B contains common elements, A∪B elements in at least one set, and Aᶜ elements of the whole space outside A. Disjoint sets have no common elements.
  • The factorial is n!=n(n−1)…1, with 0!=1. When order matters, AB and BA differ; when selecting a two-person team, they count as the same selection.
  • Σ denotes adding several terms. The finite sums here require no differentiation or integration. Countable means that elements can be listed as 1, 2, 3, ….

Learning goals

  • Derive the complement rule, monotonicity, and the union formula from the probability axioms.
  • Explain the multiplication principle and the repeated counting behind permutations and combinations.
  • Use ratios of counts only under equal probabilities.

Minimal probability rules and their immediate consequences

Probability P assigns a number to each event and satisfies the following: P(A)≥0 for every event A; P(Ω)=1; and P(∪Aₖ)=ΣP(Aₖ) for disjoint events A₁, A₂, …. The last rule is countable additivity and also applies to finitely many events. It does not mean that overlapping events can simply be added. Even when probability expresses degrees of confidence about reality, these rules must hold consistently.

Adding the disjoint sets Ω and ∅ gives P(∅)=0. Since A and Aᶜ partition Ω, P(Aᶜ)=1−P(A), and hence P(A)≤1. If A⊆B, write B=A∪(B∖A) as disjoint pieces to obtain P(B)≥P(A). These derivations explain the empty event, complements, and why a smaller event cannot have greater probability than an event containing it.

Count the overlap only once

Split A∪B into the three disjoint pieces A∖B, B∖A, and A∩B. Since P(A)+P(B) counts the common piece twice, P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B). Replace P(A∩B) with P(A)P(B) only when independence is known. Disjointness and independence are different conditions.

If two disjoint events both have positive probability, their intersection has probability 0 but their product is positive, so they cannot be independent. For example, rolling 1 and rolling 2 on one die cannot occur together. A head on the first toss and a head on the second toss of a coin with head probability 1/2 can be independent, yet they share the outcome HH and are not disjoint.

Counting depends on which selections are considered the same

If each of m first-stage choices allows n second-stage choices, there are mn ordered pairs. Selecting k of n items without repetition and with order gives n(n−1)…(n−k+1)=n!/(n−k)! possibilities. Each unordered group of k items has k! orderings, so dividing yields C(n, k)=n!/[k!(n−k)!]. The crucial condition for this division is that every group was counted exactly k! times.

If every point in finite Ω has equal probability, normalization gives each point weight 1/|Ω|. Only then is P(A)=|A|/|Ω|. A procedure selecting two of three people while favoring a particular person still has C(3,2)=3 teams, but those teams are not equally probable. Check both the count and the sampling procedure.

A worked example, step by step

Roll a fair six-sided die once. Let A mean an even result and B mean a result of at least 4. Find P(A∪B).

  1. Ω={1,2,3,4,5,6}; each point has probability 1/6.
  2. A={2,4,6}, B={4,5,6}, and A∩B={4,6}.
  3. P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)=3/6+3/6−2/6=4/6.
  4. Count the union {2,4,5,6} directly to confirm the same value, 2/3.

P(A∪B)=2/3. Simply adding to obtain 1 counts the common outcomes 4 and 6 twice.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Select two of four distinct students a, b, c, d with equal probabilities. List the possible unordered teams and find the probability of including a. Compare this with selecting a president and vice-president.

Show hint

Teams ab and ba are the same team. When roles matter, exchanging the roles of the same two people gives two selections.

Show solution

The six teams are ab, ac, ad, bc, bd, cd. Three include a, so the probability is 1/2. There are 4×3=12 role assignments; six include a in either role, again giving 1/2. The counts differ, but this event’s probability is the same.

Misconception and counterexample

Independent events do not overlap.

For two independent fair coin tosses, A=a head on the first toss and B=a head on the second satisfy P(A∩B)=1/4=P(A)P(B), yet overlap at HH. Disjoint events with positive probabilities are instead not independent.

Check your understanding

If P(A)=0.7, P(B)=0.6, and P(A∩B)=0.4, what is the union probability?

Show answer and reasoning

0.9.

It is 0.7+0.6−0.4, which also satisfies the upper bound of 1.

How many unordered selections of three people from five are possible?

Show answer and reasoning

10.

Divide the 5×4×3 ordered selections by 3!=6.

If a die’s faces have different probabilities, is the probability of an even result always 3/6?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Add the actual weights of the three even outcomes. Fairness is a separate assumption.

Explore further

The union formula and P(A∪B)≤1 imply P(A∩B)≥P(A)+P(B)−1. Since the intersection is a subset of each event, P(A∩B)≤min(P(A), P(B)). For probabilities 0.7 and 0.6, the possible overlap ranges from 0.3 to 0.6. These bounds let you check for violations of the axioms before calculating.

Subtopic 1.3

Assumptions in sampling

Why do two draws from the same bag have different probabilities depending on whether the drawn item is returned?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • The fraction 2/3 is the weight when two of the three currently available objects have the desired property. First record whether the objects remaining after a draw change.
  • Quick review: if observation order matters, (r₁, r₂) and (r₂, r₁) differ. Even objects of the same color are counted with individual labels such as r₁ and r₂.
  • P(B|A) is the probability of B given the information that A occurred. For P(A)>0, it is defined as P(A∩B)/P(A). The next unit develops this systematically.

Learning goals

  • Distinguish replacement, equal probabilities, independence, and identical distributions.
  • List ordered pairs in a small population to check probabilities.
  • Distinguish random sampling from representativeness and selection bias.

The operating rules of the experiment determine the probability model

A population is the entire collection of targets for selection; a sample consists of the targets actually selected. Suppose the population contains red objects r₁, r₂ and one blue object b. To calculate color probabilities, specify that every currently available individual is equally likely at each draw. A procedure that first selects the two color categories with probability 1/2 each differs from choosing the three individuals equally.

Sampling with replacement returns the selected individual before the next draw. If we additionally assume adequate mixing and independent, equally likely draws, the probability of red is 2/3 each time and of two reds is 4/9. Returning the object alone does not automatically guarantee physical independence. Reusing random values or insufficient mixing can couple the outcomes.

Use conditional probabilities when the remaining objects change

Sampling without replacement cannot select the same individual again. After a red first draw, one of the two remaining objects is red, so the second-red probability is 1/2. After a blue first draw, both remaining objects are red, so it is 1. Dependence means that the first outcome provides information about the second.

The event of two reds is the intersection of first red and second red. Rearranging the conditional-probability definition gives P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B|A), here (2/3)(1/2)=1/3. The same value follows by counting the two pairs (r₁, r₂), (r₂, r₁) among 3×2=6 ordered pairs. The individual ordered pairs are equally probable, but a justification for treating the three color names RR, RB, BR as equally probable must be checked separately.

Independence, identical distributions, and representativeness differ

Identical distributions means that the individual probability distributions of the draws are the same; independence means that their joint probabilities factor into products of the individual probabilities. In the experiment without replacement above, the second-red probability is (2/3)(1/2)+(1/3)(1)=2/3, the same as for the first draw. Yet the two-red probability 1/3 differs from (2/3)², so the draws are not independent. The term i.i.d. requires both independence and identical distributions.

Even randomly selecting 1000 online survey respondents does not automatically represent all residents’ opinions. Nonresponding residents are absent from that sampling frame, and inclusion probabilities can differ. Interpreting real data requires recording the target population, sampling frame, inclusion probabilities, and nonresponse. Collecting many independent observations and repairing an incorrect sampling frame are different tasks.

A worked example, step by step

From r₁, r₂, b, select each currently available individual with equal probability. Compare the probability of two reds with and without replacement.

  1. With replacement and independence: among 3²=9 ordered pairs, 2²=4 have two reds, giving 4/9.
  2. Without replacement: among 3×2=6 ordered pairs, r₁r₂ and r₂r₁ are the two with two reds, giving 1/3.
  3. The difference is 4/9−1/3=1/9. Not returning the first red object reduces the next opportunity to draw red.
  4. Without replacement, each position has red probability 2/3, but the joint probability is 1/3; the draws are not independent.

With replacement and independence the probability is 4/9; without replacement it is 1/3. An answer omitting the sampling method does not fully specify the model.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Give individual labels to two red and two blue objects, and draw the 12 possible ordered pairs for two draws without replacement. Find the probabilities of matching and different colors.

Show hint

Each of the four first objects connects to three second objects. Count RR and BB separately; the remaining pairs have different colors.

Show solution

There are two RR pairs and two BB pairs, so matching colors have probability 4/12=1/3. Different colors have probability 8/12=2/3. With replacement and independence, matching colors instead have probability (1/2)²+(1/2)²=1/2.

Misconception and counterexample

If the red probability is the same on each draw, the two draws are independent.

For r₁, r₂, b without replacement, red has probability 2/3 at each position, but two reds have probability 1/3. Identical distributions compare weights one position at a time; independence is a condition on the positions jointly.

Check your understanding

Without replacement, what is the probability that the second draw is red after the first draw is blue?

Show answer and reasoning

1.

Only the two red objects remain. Do not reuse the original probability 2/3.

With three red objects and one blue object, what is the probability of two reds without replacement?

Show answer and reasoning

1/2.

It is (3/4)(2/3)=1/2, also confirmed by six red pairs among 12 ordered pairs.

Does increasing the number of respondents tenfold necessarily remove voluntary-response bias?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

A larger sample does not include groups absent from the sampling frame. Examine the inclusion structure.

Explore further

If K of N individuals are red and n are selected by simple random sampling without replacement, ignoring order, the probability of exactly k reds is C(K, k)C(N−K, n−k)/C(N, n). N, K, n, k must be integers satisfying N≥0, 0≤K≤N, 0≤n≤N, 0≤k≤K, and 0≤n−k≤N−K. The numerator multiplies the red selections by the selections of the others; the denominator counts all groups. For N=4, K=2, n=2, k=1, the probability is 4/6=2/3, matching the paper activity. This model connects to the later hypergeometric distribution.

Subtopic 1.4

Simulation and probability models

If 8 of 10 trials give heads, can we conclude with certainty that the coin’s true head probability is 0.8?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • The ratio k/n divides the observed number of successes k by the trial count n. Unlike the model’s P(A), this ratio can vary from experiment to experiment.
  • Quick review: an indicator variable I is 1 if an event occurs and 0 otherwise. The average (I₁+…+Iₙ)/n is the event’s relative frequency.
  • The square root √n is the positive number whose square is n. You can follow the error-scale explanation here using the definitions of variance and probability, without differentiation or integration.

Learning goals

  • Explain the difference between generating samples from a model and collecting real data.
  • Map a short list of random-number inputs to an event and calculate its relative frequency.
  • Explain the error scale under independent trials and the limits of seed-based reproducibility.

Specify the distribution, then experiment according to its rules

Simulation repeatedly implements a specified probability model and observes the results. If U is uniform on [0,1), declaring success when U<p creates a trial with success probability p. For p=1/4, the interval [0,1/4) has one quarter of the total length, which justifies the rule. An experiment to discover an unknown real-world p and an experiment to check a model into which p has already been supplied have different purposes.

Computer pseudorandom numbers are generated from an initial seed and an algorithm. The same algorithm, environment, call order, and seed make reproduction easier; repeating the same experiment with the same seed does not provide new independent evidence. The documented random() returns numbers greater than or equal to 0 and less than 1, but uses a finite-precision algorithm. Record the distinction between the mathematical idealization of uniformity and independence and the actual generator’s properties.

Relative frequency approximates probability and fluctuates

For indicators Iⱼ from n independent trials with the same success probability p, E(Iⱼ)=p and Var(Iⱼ)=p(1−p). E is an average weighted by the distribution; Var is the weighted average of squared distances from the mean. Since I²=I, Var(I)=E(I²)−E(I)²=p−p². The relative frequency p̂=(ΣIⱼ)/n satisfies E(p̂)=p. Independence makes the covariance terms zero, giving Var(p̂)=p(1−p)/n.

Standard deviation is √Var. For p=1/4, it is √(3/(16n)): about 0.0433 for n=100 and 0.0217 for n=400. Halving the typical error scale requires four times as many trials. This number is neither the maximum error in a particular run nor a confidence interval. Increasing n does not make error decrease monotonically at every step; for dependent trials this variance formula need not hold.

Check the implementation and the real world separately

In a small space, compare theoretical calculations with simulation. Two independent fair coin tosses have probability 3/4 of at least one head. If an implementation copies the first result to the second coin, only HH and TT occur and the probability becomes 1/2. More repetitions only approach this incorrectly implemented, different model more precisely. First inspect outcome mapping, replacement rules, and the use of independent random inputs.

If real data differ from theory, consider chance sampling variation, implementation errors, and unsuitable model assumptions separately. Even ten heads in ten trials have nonzero probability (1/2)¹⁰=1/1024 under the independent fair-coin model. One rare outcome is not a complete logical refutation. Specify the comparison procedure, sample size, and hypothesis in advance. This unit does not claim to have performed an actual statistical test.

A worked example, step by step

The following U list is for calculation practice, not an actual random-number run: 0.10, 0.70, 0.20, 0.90, 0.40, 0.24, 0.60, 0.80. Calculate the relative frequency using success rule U<0.25.

  1. 0.10, 0.20, and 0.24 are successes; the other values are failures.
  2. The indicators are 1,0,1,0,0,1,0,0, with sum 3.
  3. p̂=3/8=0.375, differing from model p=0.25 by 0.125.
  4. This list is practice input, not evidence of an independent uniform random experiment. We calculate only the proportion for these eight values.

The success proportion in this input list is 0.375. It does not establish the model probability as 0.375.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Apply U<0.25 to the practice list 0.00, 0.25, 0.249, 0.99. Compare the result after changing the rule to U≤0.25, and consider why the two rules have the same probability for ideal continuous U.

Show hint

The rules differ only at the boundary value 0.25. A single point has probability 0 in the continuous uniform model.

Show solution

The strict inequality succeeds for 0.00 and 0.249, giving 1/2. With ≤, 0.25 also succeeds, giving 3/4. The frequencies in this finite list differ, but the ideal continuous distribution has P(U=0.25)=0, so both probabilities are 1/4. A finite-precision generator can produce the boundary point, so specify the implementation conditions.

Misconception and counterexample

Increasing the trial count continually reduces error in every run.

For p=1/2, the first two results HT give frequency 1/2 and zero error. If the next result is H, the frequency becomes 2/3 and the error grows. Long-term concentration does not imply monotone convergence at each step.

Check your understanding

What is the frequency for 30 successes in 120 trials?

Show answer and reasoning

1/4.

It is 30÷120, an observed proportion.

Under independent trials, by what factor does the frequency’s standard deviation change when n increases from 100 to 900?

Show answer and reasoning

A factor of 1/3.

It is proportional to √(1/n), so √(100/900)=1/3.

Is the identical result obtained by rerunning the same algorithm with the same seed a separate independent experiment?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

It reproduces the same random-number path. Distinguish checking reproducibility from obtaining a new independent sample.

Explore further

For independent, identically distributed trials, with n a positive integer and ε>0, Chebyshev’s inequality gives P(|p̂−p|≥ε)≤p(1−p)/(nε²)≤1/(4nε²). The reason is that on the event where (p̂−p)² is at least ε², the average squared deviation is at least ε² times that event’s probability. For n=10000 and ε=0.05, the final bound is 0.01. It is conservative but is a finite-n upper bound with explicit conditions, connecting to the law of large numbers in the later unit on limit theorems.
