Subtopic 3.1

PMF and CDF

If different die outcomes are recorded as the same score, should their probabilities be added?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • A random variable X is a function assigning one real value X(ω) to each outcome ω. Distinguish that value from the event {ω:X(ω)=x}.
  • A discrete random variable takes values in some finite or countable set S with probability 1. Countable means that its elements can be listed using natural numbers; the values need not be integers.
  • The PMF is the probability mass function pX(x)=P(X=x); the CDF is the cumulative distribution function F(x)=P(X≤x). The CDF accepts every real number as an input.

Learning goals

  • Build a PMF by adding the probabilities of outcomes mapped to the same value.
  • Calculate CDF jumps and the difference between strict and non-strict inequalities.

Assign probabilities to events, not to values alone

Let the six equally likely outcomes be ω=1..6 and let X(ω)=0,0,1,1,1,3. Each ω has probability 1/6, but the values 0,1,3 have probabilities 2/6,3/6,1/6, respectively. When different outcomes are recorded as the same value, group their events and add their probabilities.

We have pX(x)≥0 and Σ(x∈S)pX(x)=1. The events {X=x} are disjoint and cover the full range of values with probability 1. A list of possible values does not justify declaring a uniform distribution, and the mass outside S is 0.

Include the cumulative boundary precisely

The CDF of this table is 0 for x<0, 1/3 for 0≤x<1, 5/6 for 1≤x<3, and 1 for x≥3. F(1)=P(X≤1)=5/6, whereas P(X<1)=1/3. In a discrete distribution with jumps, changing < to ≤ changes the included probability mass.

F is nondecreasing and right-continuous, with limits 0 and 1 at the two ends of the real line. Since F(a−)=lim(t↑a)F(t)=P(X<a), the jump F(a)−F(a−)=P(X=a). The values of a general discrete distribution may even be dense, so its CDF need not be a finite staircase.

Interval probabilities depend on endpoint conventions

P(a<X≤b)=F(b)−F(a), while P(a≤X≤b)=F(b)−F(a−). For a≤b, these formulas follow by taking differences of events. First mark whether the point mass at the left endpoint is included.

A PMF gives the probability at one point; a CDF gives cumulative probability. Do not add them together or replace them with the magnitude of the value. The probability 1/6 of score 3 does not change because 3 is a large number. This example uses a hypothetical score function specified beforehand, rather than records of actual die throws.

A worked example, step by step

Write the PMF and CDF of X above, and calculate P(0<X≤3), P(X<1), and P(X=1).

  1. The preimage of 0 is {1,2}, that of 1 is {3,4,5}, and that of 3 is {6}.
  2. The PMF is p(0)=1/3,p(1)=1/2,p(3)=1/6, whose sum is 1.
  3. The CDF has four intervals with values 0/1⁄3/5⁄6/1; it includes the new mass at each of 0,1,3.
  4. P(0<X≤3)=1−1/3=2/3, P(X<1)=1/3, and P(X=1)=5/6−1/3=1/2.

The masses are 1/3,1/2,1/6, and the three event probabilities are 2/3,1/3,1/2.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Y takes values −1, 1/2, 2 with probabilities 1/4,1/2,1/4, respectively. Calculate F_Y(1/2), P(Y<1/2), and P(−1≤Y<2).

Show hint

Discrete values need not be integers. Write down whether the mass at each endpoint is included.

Show solution

F_Y(1/2)=1/4+1/2=3/4. P(Y<1/2)=1/4. The last event includes −1 and 1/2 but excludes 2, so its probability is 3/4. Do not automatically assign 1/3 to each of the three distinct values.

Misconception and counterexample

For a CDF, P(X<a)=P(X≤a) always holds.

At a=1 in the example, the left probability is 1/3 and the right probability is 5/6. Their difference, 1/2, is the mass at point 1. The two event probabilities are equal only when that point has mass 0.

Check your understanding

Must a discrete random variable take only integer values?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Countably many real values are also possible, such as 1/2 for Y in the activity.

May the total PMF mass exceed 1?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

The disjoint value events partition a whole of probability 1.

What probability does F(1)−F(1−) represent?

Show answer and reasoning

P(X=1).

It is the mass at exactly the point newly included in the cumulative probability.

Explore further

Extension: Continuity of probability for increasing events follows from a countable sum of disjoint difference events. If A_n↑A, partition A into A₁ and A_n\A_{n−1} to obtain P(A_n)→P(A). Taking complements also gives continuity for decreasing events. If t_n↓a, then {X≤t_n}↓{X≤a}, so F(t_n)→F(a): this is right-continuity. If t_n↑a with t_n<a, then {X≤t_n}↑{X<a}, so the left limit is P(X<a). The difference of the two events, {X=a}, yields the jump formula. This reasoning also holds for general real-valued random variables; neither CDF continuity nor discreteness should be reduced to whether the values are integers.

Subtopic 3.2

Bernoulli and Binomial Distributions

When counting successes in four checks, why must we also multiply by the number of possible success positions?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • A Bernoulli variable I takes 0 for failure and 1 for success, with P(I=1)=p and P(I=0)=1−p. Here p∈[0,1]; success is an event label, regardless of whether it is good or bad.
  • For integers n≥0 and 0≤k≤n, the binomial coefficient C(n,k)=n!/[k!(n−k)!] counts ways to choose k of n positions. We use 0!=1.
  • For mutually independent trials, the probability of a complete success/failure pattern is the product of the individual trial probabilities. Having the same p is a separate condition from independence.

Learning goals

  • Check a fixed number of trials, independence, and a common success probability before using a binomial distribution.
  • Derive the PMF by counting patterns and interpret boundary values separately.

One trial and a fixed-count sum

Suppose I₁,…,I_n are mutually independent Bernoulli(p) variables and n is a fixed nonnegative integer. Then X=ΣI_i counts successes and has distribution Bin(n,p). If n=0, the empty sum is 0, so X=0 with probability 1. A number of trials that stops at the first success differs from a binomial variable with fixed n.

Equal marginal success probabilities alone do not establish the binomial model. If I₁=I₂=Z, where Z is a fair Bernoulli variable, both trials are simultaneously 0 or 1, so X takes only 0 and 2. This differs from the mass 1/2 at the middle value in Bin(2,1/2).

From one pattern to all patterns

For 0<p<1, a specified pattern with exactly k successes has probability pᵏ(1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ. There are C(n,k) disjoint patterns obtained by choosing the success positions, so P(X=k)=C(n,k)pᵏ(1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ. Here k=0..n; all other values have probability 0.

The binomial theorem verifies that the total is [p+(1−p)]ⁿ=1. If p=0, X=0 with probability 1; if p=1, X=n with probability 1. Defining these degenerate distributions directly makes their meaning clearer than turning 0⁰ at the boundary into a calculator issue.

The sampling procedure also affects success probabilities

A model of independent draws with replacement can assume a constant p. Sampling without replacement changes the composition of the remaining items and may violate independence or the common-p condition. The label success count alone does not determine a binomial PMF.

For four hypothetical independent checks with p=1/4, the probability of exactly two successes is 6·(1/4)²(3/4)²=27/128. This p is given in the problem, rather than an actual test accuracy or an estimate from observations.

A worked example, step by step

For the success count X in four independent Bernoulli(1/4) checks, find P(X=2), P(X=0), and P(X≥1).

  1. Fixed n=4, common p=1/4, and independence are assumed, so X~Bin(4,1/4).
  2. There are C(4,2)=6 pairs of success positions, and one pattern has probability 9/256. Hence P(X=2)=54/256=27/128.
  3. The probability of all failures is (3/4)⁴=81/256.
  4. P(X≥1)=1−81/256=175/256. At least one success and exactly two successes are different events.

P(X=2)=27/128, P(X=0)=81/256, and P(X≥1)=175/256.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Make a probability table for 0..3 successes in three fair, independent trials. Compare it with the model Z+Z, whose two trials always succeed or fail together.

Show hint

The coefficients in the first table are 1,3,3,1. For the second model, list the possible sums directly.

Show solution

The masses of Bin(3,1/2) are 1/8,3/8,3/8,1/8. For Z+Z, P(0)=1/2,P(1)=0,P(2)=1/2, so it is not Bin(2,1/2). Even with the same individual p, dependence changes the distribution of the sum.

Misconception and counterexample

Every success count has a binomial distribution.

Fixed n, independence, and a common p are required. Copying one fair Z twice gives a success count that is not binomial.

Check your understanding

Is Bin(n,p) undefined when n=0?

Show answer and reasoning

It is defined: X=0 with probability 1.

With no trials, the success count is the empty sum 0.

With p=1 in four trials, what is the probability that X=2?

Show answer and reasoning

0.

All four trials succeed, so X=4 with probability 1.

Does a common p automatically guarantee independence?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

In the counterexample I₁=I₂=Z, the marginal p values agree but the variables move together.

Explore further

Extension: Start at n=0 with the PMF p₀(0)=1. When adding the next independent trial I_{n+1}, divide the event of exactly k successes according to whether the final trial fails or succeeds. This gives p_{n+1}(k)=(1−p)p_n(k)+p p_n(k−1). For 0<p<1, substituting the binomial PMF combines the coefficients as C(n,k)+C(n,k−1)=C(n+1,k), proving the formula inductively. Set out-of-range coefficients and masses to 0; this includes k=0,n+1. Check p=0,1 directly using deterministic sums. The product probability of a complete pattern follows from mutual independence; the weaker condition of pairwise independence alone does not justify this product for arbitrary n.

Subtopic 3.3

Geometric and Poisson Distributions

Why do the number of trials until success and the number of arrivals in a fixed time have different distributions?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • The geometric variable T counts trials including the first success, taking values 1,2,… . The failure count K=T−1 takes values 0,1,… . Establish which of these two conventions you mean, even when both use the same distribution name.
  • exp(x)=eˣ, where e is the base of the natural logarithm. Review the exponential series Σ(k≥0)μᵏ/k!=e^μ and the geometric series Σ(k≥0)qᵏ=1/(1−q), |q|<1.
  • For Poisson(μ), μ is the mean count in the specified interval. With intensity λ per unit time and interval length τ, μ=λτ; distinguish this from λ itself.

Learning goals

  • Distinguish the trial-count and failure-count conventions for the geometric distribution and the boundaries p=0,1.
  • State assumptions for a Poisson count and for the time process that generates it.

Multiply the failures before success

Repeat independent Bernoulli(p) trials with 0<p<1. Then P(T=k)=(1−p)ᵏ⁻¹p for k≥1. For 0<p<1, the total mass is p/[1−(1−p)]=1, and the probability of failing forever is lim(1−p)ⁿ=0. If p=1, T=1 with probability 1.

If p=0, there is no success: record the waiting outcome as T=∞ with probability 1. This is an extended record that success never occurs, rather than a value of a finite real-valued random variable. The PMF over finite trial counts sums to 0 and is not a proper geometric distribution. For 0<p<1, the failure count K has PMF P(K=k)=(1−p)ᵏp; its exponent differs from that for T. Forcing an earlier stop produces a censored record, rather than a complete observation of the original T.

Poisson models counts in an interval

For μ>0, N~Poisson(μ) has P(N=k)=e⁻μ μᵏ/k! for k=0,1,… . The exponential series verifies total mass 1. Define μ=0 separately as the degenerate distribution with N=0 with probability 1. Counts cannot be negative and have no finite upper bound such as a fixed number of trials.

When using a time model, assume the conditions of a homogeneous Poisson process. Here the time intensity λ>0 and interval length τ>0. We have N(0)=0, independent increments over nonoverlapping intervals, and a Poisson(λτ) count for every interval of length τ. Because λ is constant, the distribution depends on the interval length, not its location. Equal overall mean counts alone do not verify these independent and stationary increment conditions.

Distinguish one time unit from half a unit

If p=1/2, the probability of success within the first three trials is 1−(1/2)³=7/8. In contrast, a process with intensity λ=2 per hour has μ=2 for one hour and μ=1 for half an hour. Do not mix physical time with a trial index.

If actual arrivals come in batches or the intensity changes with the time of day, a homogeneous Poisson model may be inappropriate. Assuming a Poisson distribution for one specified interval and assuming independent increments of an entire process are also different levels of assumption. The numbers in this unit are model conditions, rather than sensor observations.

A worked example, step by step

For the first-success trial count T with independent success probability p=1/2, find P(T=3) and P(T>3). Separately, in a homogeneous Poisson process of intensity 2 arrivals/hour, find P(N=0) and P(N=2) for the half-hour count N.

  1. T=3 means failure, failure, success, with probability (1/2)²·(1/2)=1/8.
  2. T>3 means all first three trials fail, with probability (1/2)³=1/8.
  3. The half-hour mean is μ=(2 arrivals/hour)(1/2 hour)=1 arrival. Thus N~Poisson(1).
  4. P(N=0)=e⁻¹ and P(N=2)=e⁻¹/2. Keep exact expressions instead of approximating the exponential with a calculator.

The two geometric probabilities are each 1/8; the two Poisson probabilities are e⁻¹,e⁻¹/2.

Try it with paper and the lesson

At p=1/4, compare the probabilities of first success on trial T=2 and failure count K=2. For a Poisson variable with μ=2, write P(N=0),P(N=1),P(N=2).

Show hint

K=2 means T=3. Use 0!=1 and 2!=2 for the Poisson probabilities.

Show solution

P(T=2)=(3/4)(1/4)=3/16, whereas P(K=2)=(3/4)²(1/4)=9/64. The Poisson probabilities are e⁻²,2e⁻²,2e⁻². Treating K and T as the same value changes the definition of the waiting count.

Misconception and counterexample

A constant mean arrival count confirms a Poisson process.

In a deterministic model with exactly two arrivals together at each whole-hour clock time, the count in every one-hour interval is the constant 2, so it does not have a Poisson(2) distribution. Its increments are constant random variables and can be mutually independent. However, equal-length short intervals have different counts depending on whether they include a scheduled arrival time, so stationary increments fail. A mean alone cannot establish a Poisson count distribution or all the assumptions of the process.

Check your understanding

Does substituting p=0 into the finite-trial-count geometric PMF give a proper distribution?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Every finite mass is 0, and the waiting time until success is infinite.

Are failure count K=2 and trial count T=2 the same event?

Show answer and reasoning

No. K=2 means T=3.

They differ in whether the successful trial is included.

If λ=2 arrivals/hour, is the half-hour mean μ also 2?

Show answer and reasoning

No. μ=1.

The Poisson parameter is λτ; multiply with matching time units.

Explore further

Extension: Let 0<p<1 and q=1−p. For integers m,n≥0, P(T>m+n|T>m)=q^(m+n)/q^m=q^n=P(T>n). This is the memoryless property of the trial-count geometric distribution, and the conditioning event has probability q^m>0. At p=1,m≥1, that event has probability 0, so the same conditional expression is undefined. The Poisson masses can also be obtained as a binomial limit. Fix μ>0 and a fixed integer k≥0. For n>max(μ,k), let X_n~Bin(n,μ/n). Then C(n,k)(μ/n)^k→μ^k/k!, (1−μ/n)^n→e⁻μ, and (1−μ/n)^−k→1. Their product shows that the PMF converges to e⁻μ μ^k/k!. This is a limit of the specified independent binomial models, rather than an experiment proving that actual arrival data are Poisson. It uses the standard limit (1−μ/n)^n→e⁻μ. Using ln(1+u)/u→1 gives n ln(1−μ/n)→−μ; continuity of exp then gives the result. We can also check why μ is called the mean. For a nonnegative discrete variable, E[N]=Σ(k≥0)kP(N=k); for μ>0, E[N]=μe⁻μΣ(j≥0)μʲ/j!=μ. The degenerate case μ=0 also has mean 0.

Subtopic 3.4

Choosing Distributions and Checking Models

If a calculated distribution seems plausible, have we also confirmed how the observed data were generated?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • A model specifies the random variable, generating assumptions, and parameters. A distribution derived mathematically from assumptions and its suitability for actual data are different claims. For a finite-valued variable, the mean is Σ_x xP(X=x): multiply each value by its probability and add.
  • An empirical frequency is an observed count divided by the total number of records. First check the recording unit, missing records, duplicates, and censoring to establish what the denominator means.
  • Mutual independence requires product probabilities for every finite combination. Means of records or the distribution of a single variable cannot establish independence.

Learning goals

  • Choose a candidate distribution that matches the contract for trial counts, waiting, or interval counts.
  • Prove that generating dependence can differ even when marginal distributions agree.

Start with the variable contract

Candidate distributions are binomial for successes in fixed n trials, geometric for trials until first success, and Poisson for arrivals in a specified time interval. State their assumptions separately: independent trials with common p; unlimited repetition and a stopping rule; or constant intensity and independent increments. The fact that each record is 0/1 does not make several records independent Bernoulli variables.

A hypothetical record 1,1,0,0 from four checks has two successes. That single row does not establish that the true p is 1/2 or that the checks are independent. Distinguish an estimated sample proportion from a fixed model parameter, and make clear the extent to which the same data are used for selection and validation.

Two sum distributions with different assumptions

Model A sums four independent Bernoulli(1/2) variables. Its masses at 0..4 are 1,4,6,4,1 divided by 16. Model B copies one fair Z four times to form 4Z. Each individual trial has a Bernoulli(1/2) marginal in both models, but the sum in B has mass 1/2 only at each of 0 and 4.

Both models have mean success count 2, yet their middle masses differ. Agreement with one observed marginal proportion or mean does not validate the joint generating conditions. Actual evaluation may require sufficiently many independent units, time or group information, and other evidence.

Distinguish a checking plan from its results

A validation plan can compare counts by time of day, dependence between neighboring intervals, changes in success probability across conditions, and missing records stopped after failures. Diagnostics based on small tables and plots can give reasons to question assumptions; they do not mathematically prove general probability laws from finite samples.

This unit only compares hypothetical data and mathematical models. It has not collected actual data, performed goodness-of-fit tests, or run simulations. If violations are suspected in practice, examine other distributions or conditional models; choosing a distribution name does not guarantee observational validity.

A worked example, step by step

For models A and B, calculate P(X=2), P(X=0), and the mean. Explain whether individual success proportions alone can distinguish the models.

  1. A is an independent binomial model with n=4,p=1/2. P_A(2)=6/16=3/8 and P_A(0)=1/16.
  2. For B, X=4Z, so P_B(2)=0 and P_B(0)=1/2; the remaining positive mass is at X=4.
  3. The mean of A is (0·1+1·4+2·6+3·4+4·1)/16=2. The mean of B is 0·1/2+4·1/2=2.
  4. The individual trial success probabilities and the means cannot distinguish them. The masses of their sums and their joint dependence differ.

The two probabilities are 3/8,1/16 for A and 0,1/2 for B. Both means are 2; marginal success probabilities alone do not determine independence.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Observe four independent Bernoulli(p) trials. If there has been no first success, stop recording at 4 and write “no success.” At p=1/2, make a probability table for recorded T=1..4 and no success. Explain what changes if you merge no success into T=4.

Show hint

Count a success after the first k−1 failures separately from four consecutive failures.

Show solution

The record masses for T=1,2,3,4 are 1/2,1/4,1/8,1/16, and no success also has mass 1/16; the total is 1. Merging no success into T=4 changes the final mass to 1/8, giving the distribution of the recorded value min(T,4). Calling this the PMF of the original first-success trial count T loses information about success and censoring.

Misconception and counterexample

Agreement of the mean and marginal success probabilities validates the independent model.

A and B share mean 2 and individual success probability 1/2, but P(X=2) is 3/8 versus 0. Separate internal consistency of model calculations from external checks of actual generating assumptions.

Check your understanding

If the observed success proportion is 1/2, must the true p be 1/2?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

A finite sample proportion can differ from the parameter, and the sampling mechanism must also be checked.

Can a censoring label be merged into a first-success value while preserving the original distribution?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

It becomes the distribution of a new recording function that merges events.

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Explore further

Extension: Show fully that individual marginal distributions do not determine the joint distribution. In A, assign probability 1/16 to each of the 16 patterns ω∈{0,1}⁴ and set I_i(ω)=ω_i. Eight patterns have a 1 in each coordinate, giving marginal probability 1/2, and the probability of a specified complete pattern is the product of four marginal probabilities. More generally, fixing values at r distinct coordinates leaves 2^{4−r} possibilities for the remaining 4−r coordinates, so the event probability is 2^{4−r}/16=(1/2)^r. Thus value events for every subset of coordinates satisfy the product of marginal probabilities, establishing mutual independence. In B, give each point in Ω={0,1} probability 1/2 and set every I_i(ω)=ω. The marginals agree, but P(I₁=1,I₂=0)=0≠1/4, so they are not independent. The two sum distributions therefore differ as in the example. It is logically impossible to infer independence from equal marginals or means. This is a counterexample proof in a finite model, rather than a calculation of the sample size or statistical power needed for actual validation.
