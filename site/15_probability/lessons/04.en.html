Subtopic 4.1

Density and the Cumulative Distribution Function

If a density graph has height 2, does that mean a probability of 200%?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • A random variable X is a function assigning a real value to each outcome. The event {a<X≤b} collects the outcomes whose values fall in that interval.
  • The CDF is F(x)=P(X≤x). It is defined for every real-valued random variable, is nondecreasing, and is right-continuous. Right-continuity means F(x+h)→F(x) as h↓0.
  • The integral ∫ₐᵇ f(t)dt can be understood as a limit of sums of height times width over small intervals. An integral with an infinite endpoint is a limit as finite endpoints tend to infinity. For a nonnegative function, its integral represents area; when an antiderivative is available, use the difference of its values.

Learning goals

  • Calculate probability by integrating over an interval rather than using a density value.
  • Distinguish continuity, absolute continuity, and pointwise differentiability of a CDF.

Height and area have different roles

We first study absolutely continuous distributions that have a density. A nonnegative integrable function f satisfies ∫ℝ f(x)dx=1 and gives P(X∈A)=∫_A f(x)dx for every Borel set A; here ∫_A means integration over the set A. Borel sets include ordinary real-valued events such as intervals and their countable unions. The height of f at one point is not the probability of that point.

If X is expressed in seconds, f has units of inverse seconds and f(x)dx is a dimensionless probability. A uniform model with density 2 per second on an interval of width 1/2 second is valid: its total area is 1. Integrating over one point gives 0, so P(X=x)=0. Adding an uncountable collection of point probabilities is not the countable-additivity rule for probability.

Read the distribution through cumulative probability

In a density model, F(x)=∫₋∞ˣ f(t)dt, and for a<b, P(a<X≤b)=F(b)−F(a). Since each point has probability 0, changing < to ≤ at an interval endpoint does not affect the answer. For a union of disjoint intervals, add the areas; for overlapping intervals, count the overlap only once.

A CDF obtained by integrating a density is absolutely continuous and therefore continuous. Absolute continuity means that for every ε>0 there is a δ>0 such that, for any finite collection of disjoint intervals with total length less than δ, the sum of the corresponding increases in F is less than ε. Continuity alone does not automatically ensure this condition or a density representation. Some singular distributions have continuous CDFs but no density, so this unit does not claim to handle every continuous CDF with a density formula.

Where can the CDF be differentiated?

If f is continuous at x, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives F′(x)=f(x). For a general integrable density, this relation holds almost everywhere, meaning outside an exceptional set of length zero. To calculate probabilities, use the original integral without first requiring a derivative at every point.

For the uniform distribution U(0,1), F is 0 for x≤0, x for 0<x<1, and 1 for x≥1. It is continuous but not differentiable at 0 and 1, where the one-sided slopes differ. Changing a density at finitely many points does not change its integrals or distribution. Do not confuse a convention for assigning density values at those points with the existence of a derivative of F.

A worked example, step by step

Normalize the assumed density f(x)=c x for 0<x<2 and 0 otherwise. Find its CDF and P(1<X≤3/2).

  1. Nonnegativity requires c≥0. The total integral is ∫₀² c x dx=2c=1, giving c=1/2.
  2. For x≤0, F=0; for 0<x<2, F=∫₀ˣ t/2 dt=x²/4; for x≥2, F=1. The pieces meet continuously at the endpoints.
  3. Compute F(3/2)=9/16 and F(1)=1/4=4/16. The interval probability is their difference.
  4. Thus P(1<X≤3/2)=5/16, whereas P(X=1)=0. The value f(1)=1/2 is a density height, distinct from a point probability.

c=1/2. The CDF is F(x)=0 for x≤0, x²/4 for 0<x<2, and 1 for x≥2. The interval probability is 5/16.

Try it with paper and the lesson

On paper, for f(x)=2 on 0<x<1/2 and 0 otherwise, find the total area, P(1/8<X<3/8), F(1/4), and P(X=1/4).

Show hint

Within the interval, calculate height times width. Accumulate the CDF from the left boundary 0.

Show solution

The total is 2·(1/2)=1. The interval probability is 2·(3/8−1/8)=1/2; F(1/4)=2·1/4=1/2; the point probability is 0. Although height 2 exceeds 1, all the probability values in these calculations lie in [0,1].

Misconception and counterexample

A continuous CDF is differentiable everywhere, and its derivative is a probability.

The continuous CDF of U(0,1) is not differentiable at 0 or 1. Its interior derivative 1 is a density, whereas P(X=1/2)=0. Continuity, differentiability, density, and point probability are different claims.

Check your understanding

Is f=3 on (0,1), and 0 elsewhere, a valid density?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Its total area is 3, violating normalization to 1.

In a density model, are P(a≤X≤b) and P(a<X<b) equal?

Show answer and reasoning

Yes, for a<b.

Each endpoint has probability 0, so removing them makes no difference.

When converting X in seconds to Y=1000X in milliseconds, do density heights stay unchanged?

Show answer and reasoning

No. fᵧ(y)=fₓ(y/1000)/1000.

Since dx=dy/1000, density units must also change to preserve probability areas.

Explore further

Direct proof of zero point probability and CDF continuity: In a density model, {x} has length zero, so ∫_{ {x} }f=0. For a general CDF, its left limit is P(X<x). Indeed, the events {X≤x−1/n} increase to {X<x}; continuity of probability from below gives that limit. Hence F(x)−F(x−)=P(X=x). In a density model this difference is 0; together with right-continuity, this makes F continuous. The same zero-integral argument explains why changing f at finitely many points has no effect. This proof does not establish the converse that a continuous F must have a density. The next topic calculates two specific density families.

Subtopic 4.2

Uniform and Exponential Distributions

When do equal-length time intervals have equal probabilities, and does having waited longer change the remaining wait?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Uniform means a constant density height. Before asserting equal probabilities for equal-length intervals, check that both lie inside the full support interval.
  • For positive λ, exp(−λt) decreases as t grows. The product λt is dimensionless, so if t is in minutes, λ has units of inverse minutes.
  • Conditional probability P(A|B)=P(A∩B)/P(B) requires P(B)>0. The mean E[X] is ∫x f(x)dx and is a finite real number when the integral of the absolute value is finite.

Learning goals

  • Normalize uniform and exponential distributions with a<b and λ>0.
  • Distinguish rate from scale, and memorylessness from validation of actual waiting-time data.

A uniform model on a finite interval

For real a<b, U(a,b) has density f=1/(b−a) on (a,b) and 0 outside. Endpoint density values do not affect integrals and may be set to 0. The CDF is 0 for x≤a, (x−a)/(b−a) for a<x<b, and 1 for x≥b. The probability of any interval is the length of its overlap with (a,b), divided by b−a.

The mean is ∫ₐᵇ x/(b−a)dx=(a+b)/2. Integrating the square of X−(a+b)/2 over the centered symmetric interval gives variance (b−a)²/12. The case a=b is not defined by this density. A constant random variable concentrated at a is a separate degenerate distribution; a zero width is not hidden inside the formula.

An exponential model for nonnegative time

For rate λ>0, Exp(λ) has f(t)=λe^(−λt) for t≥0 and 0 for t<0. Its total integral ∫₀∞ λe^(−λt)dt is 1. The CDF is 0 for t<0 and 1−e^(−λt) for t≥0. Use the tail P(T>t)=e^(−λt) for t≥0. Setting λ=0 does not produce a density with integral 1.

The same distribution can be parameterized by scale θ=1/λ>0, giving density (1/θ)e^(−t/θ). The scale θ has time units; the rate λ has inverse-time units; and the mean is E[T]=θ=1/λ. Guessing whether a parameter value 2 means rate or scale changes the mean to 1/2 or 2. State the notation and units together.

Conditional waiting and model assumptions

For s,t≥0, P(T>s)>0, so P(T>s+t|T>s)=e^(−λ(s+t))/e^(−λs)=e^(−λt). This is memorylessness: conditional on already having waited s, the remaining wait has the same tail as the initial wait. It does not say that elapsed time disappears.

Interpreting an exponential distribution as the wait for the first event of a constant-intensity Poisson process requires assumptions such as stationary independent increments and a constant intensity. Not every actual wait follows this model. For U(0,4), P(T>3|T>2)=1/2, whereas P(T>1)=3/4, so it is not memoryless. Whether a model fits data is a separate observational and review task.

A worked example, step by step

For a hypothetical departure wait U~U(2,8) in minutes and a separate hypothetical wait T~Exp(rate 1/4 min⁻¹), find P(3<U<5), E[U], P(T>8), and P(T>12|T>8). No joint distribution or independence of the two variables is assumed.

  1. The total width for U is 6 minutes, and (3,5) has width 2 minutes, so the probability is 2/6=1/3.
  2. The uniform mean is (2+8)/2=5 minutes. The rate 1/4 min⁻¹ for T is equivalent to a scale of 4 minutes.
  3. For T, λ·8=2, so P(T>8)=e⁻². Its mean is 4 minutes.
  4. Since {T>12}⊂{T>8}, the conditional probability is e⁻³/e⁻²=e⁻¹, equal to the tail for an additional 4 minutes.

1/3, mean 5 minutes, e⁻², and e⁻¹. Only the respective assumed marginal distributions were used.

Try it with paper and the lesson

On paper, find P(2<W<5) and the mean for W~U(−1,3). For H~Exp(scale 2 seconds), also find P(H>2), P(H>5|H>3), and the mean.

Show hint

Clip the uniform interval at 3 and count only its overlap. Convert scale 2 seconds into rate 1/2 s⁻¹.

Show solution

The overlap for W is (2,3), so its probability is 1/4 and its mean is 1. For H, the tail is e^(−t/2), so both probabilities are e⁻¹ and its mean is 2 seconds. The additional wait is 5−3=2 seconds, and the conditioning event has positive probability e^(−3/2).

Misconception and counterexample

The exponential parameter λ is the mean waiting time, and all waiting-time data are exponentially distributed.

In this manuscript λ is a rate, so the mean is 1/λ. The conditional U(0,4) counterexample above shows that a waiting model need not be exponential. Calculating within a specified model differs from validating it against actual data.

Check your understanding

Can U(4,4) be defined by the density formula above?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

The condition a<b fails and 1/(b−a) is undefined. A constant variable has a separate distribution.

What are the mean and scale for rate 3 s⁻¹?

Show answer and reasoning

Mean 1/3 second, scale 1/3 second.

The reciprocal of the rate is the scale, with time units.

For Exp(λ), may P(T>−1) be calculated as e^λ?

Show answer and reasoning

No. Its value is 1.

T≥0 almost surely, and using the exponential tail formula here violates its condition t≥0.

Explore further

Mean integration and the limits of memorylessness: For an exponential distribution with λ>0, integration by parts at a finite R gives ∫₀ᴿ tλe^(−λt)dt=−R e^(−λR)+(1−e^(−λR))/λ. As R→∞, exponential growth exceeds linear growth, so R e^(−λR)→0 and the mean is 1/λ. For example, e^(λR)≥(λR)²/2 gives R e^(−λR)≤2/(λ²R)→0. Memorylessness follows from the conditional-tail quotient above for s,t≥0, whose denominator is always positive. These calculations are theorems within a hypothetical exponential model; they do not newly establish independence of observed events.

Subtopic 4.3

Normal Distributions and Standardization

How can we read two bell-shaped distributions with different centers and spreads on the same scale?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • The mean μ describes the center of a distribution. Its variance is Var(X)=E[(X−μ)²], and its standard deviation σ is the positive square root of the variance. Variance has squared original units; σ has the original units.
  • Standardization Z=(X−μ)/σ subtracts the center and divides by the positive scale σ. When σ>0, inequality directions are preserved.
  • The symbol Φ names the standard normal CDF defined in this unit. An exact answer in terms of a function differs from a numerical approximation. A normal-distribution assumption is not automatically verified by data.

Learning goals

  • State the normal density and its parameter conditions.
  • Standardize probability events to obtain exact Φ expressions and explain why standardization does not create normality.

Define the normal model

For μ∈ℝ and σ>0, N(μ,σ²) has density f(x)=exp(−(x−μ)²/(2σ²))/(σ√(2π)) for every real x. Here the second parameter is variance. The density is positive and its total integral is 1. The density formula is unavailable for σ=0; a constant distribution concentrated at μ is treated separately.

The parameter μ shifts location, while σ changes horizontal spread. A smaller σ concentrates the same total area into a narrower range, so density height can increase. A visually bell-shaped pattern alone does not prove that data follow a normal distribution. A normal model also permits negative values; when applied to a quantity that must be positive, its tails and the real-world constraints need separate review.

Events preserved by standardization

For Z~N(0,1), define the density φ(z)=e^(−z²/2)/√(2π) and CDF Φ(z)=∫₋∞ᶻ φ(t)dt. If X=μ+σZ, then X~N(μ,σ²). Conversely, if X has that normal distribution, (X−μ)/σ is standard normal. The substitution dx=σ dz preserves both density normalization and probability areas.

Since σ>0, P(X≤x)=Φ((x−μ)/σ), and for a<b, P(a<X≤b)=Φ((b−μ)/σ)−Φ((a−μ)/σ). The upper tail is 1−Φ((x−μ)/σ). A standardized value z is dimensionless; μ, x, and σ must have the same units. Dividing by the variance σ² is not standardization.

Symmetry supports exact calculations

The density φ is even, so Φ(−z)=1−Φ(z) and Φ(0)=1/2. For c≥0, P(|Z|≤c)=2Φ(c)−1. This symmetry expresses left and right tails through the same function. Since φ>0 at every finite z, Φ is strictly increasing and 0<Φ(z)<1.

Standardization itself does not change a distribution’s shape to normal. For example, U~U(−1,1) has mean 0 and variance 1/3; √3U has mean 0 and variance 1 but remains uniform on a finite interval. The standard normal has positive density on the entire real line, so these distributions differ. Normal examples in this unit are calculations only within the specified models.

A worked example, step by step

For the assumed X~N(10,4) measured in cm, find exact expressions for P(8<X≤12), P(X>14), and P(X≤10). Do not use a normal table or numerical program.

  1. The second parameter 4 is variance in cm², so σ=2 cm and μ=10 cm.
  2. Standardize with Z=(X−10)/2; the two interval endpoints become −1 and 1. Thus P(8<X≤12)=Φ(1)−Φ(−1).
  3. By symmetry, the first answer becomes 2Φ(1)−1. The event X>14 is equivalent to Z>2, giving 1−Φ(2).
  4. The probability of being at or below the center is Φ(0)=1/2. The first two answers are exact function expressions; do not add unchecked decimal approximations or measured proportions.

2Φ(1)−1, 1−Φ(2), and 1/2. The standard deviation is 2 cm, not 4.

Try it with paper and the lesson

For Y~N(−2,9) measured in seconds, express P(Y≤1), P(−5<Y<1), and P(Y>−2) using Φ. Also state the density units and the units of standardized values.

Show hint

Use μ=−2 seconds and σ=3 seconds, converting the endpoints to −1 or 1.

Show solution

The three answers are Φ(1), Φ(1)−Φ(−1)=2Φ(1)−1, and 1/2. Density has units of inverse seconds, while z=(y+2)/3 is dimensionless. This hypothetical normal model permits negative times; it is not asserted to fit an actual waiting time that must be nonnegative.

Misconception and counterexample

Subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation makes all data normally distributed.

Multiplying U(−1,1) by √3 still gives a uniform density that is zero outside (−√3,√3). The two numbers mean 0 and variance 1 do not determine the full distribution.

Check your understanding

For N(5,9), what standardized value corresponds to X=8?

Show answer and reasoning

1.

Since σ=√9=3, (8−5)/3=1.

For a normal distribution, is P(X=μ)=1/2?

Show answer and reasoning

No. It is 0.

The value 1/2 is P(X≤μ). One point has zero area in the density integral.

How is Φ(−2) expressed using Φ(2) for a standard normal variable?

Show answer and reasoning

1−Φ(2).

Use the symmetry of equal left and right tails for an even density.

Explore further

Mathematical justification of the normalization constant: I=∫ℝ e^(−z²/2)dz is positive and finite. For |z|≥1, e^(−z²/2)≤e^(−|z|/2) verifies integrability. The integral-interchange theorem for nonnegative functions gives I²=∫ℝ² e^(−(x²+y²)/2)dxdy. With the polar-coordinate area element r dr dθ, I²=∫₀^{2π}∫₀∞ e^(−r²/2)r dr dθ=2π·1, so I=√(2π). Thus φ integrates to 1. This proof uses the nonnegative integral-interchange and polar-coordinate substitution theorems; it does not reprove those two multivariable integration theorems here. The substitution x=μ+σz, dx=σdz then shows that the general normal density also integrates to 1. This is an argument using real integrals, not output from a numerical integration program. The mean and variance can also be checked by integration. Since ∫ℝ |z|φ(z)dz=2φ(0)<∞ and zφ(z) is odd, E[Z]=0. Using φ′=−zφ, integration by parts with finite endpoints followed by limits gives ∫ℝ z²φ(z)dz=[−zφ(z)]₋∞∞+∫ℝ φ(z)dz=1. Exponential decay makes both endpoint terms 0, and the limit of the nonnegative integral is finite, so Var(Z)=1. Hence X=μ+σZ has mean μ and variance σ². This derivation requires the normal assumption; it does not prove normality of arbitrarily standardized data.

Subtopic 4.4

Quantiles and Tail Probabilities

Given an upper-tail probability, which threshold should we choose, and what happens when the CDF is flat?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • A number 0<p<1 is a lower cumulative probability level. A model’s 90th percentile means its p=9/10 quantile; this differs from conventions for calculating a sample’s 90th percentile.
  • The infimum inf S is the greatest lower bound of S. Whether it belongs to S is a separate issue, but right-continuity of a CDF ensures inclusion of the quantile boundary.
  • The tail P(X>x) is the event of exceeding a threshold. In a general distribution, distinguish it from P(X≥x)=1−F(x−). In a density model, the two agree because each point has probability 0.

Learning goals

  • Calculate quantiles using the infimum convention and handle flat intervals.
  • Prove the relation between quantile levels and exceedance probabilities, and distinguish exact normal-tail expressions from bounds.

A convention for a generalized inverse

For a CDF F, this unit defines the quantile Q(p)=inf{x∈ℝ:F(x)≥p} for 0<p<1. Since the two limiting CDF values are 0 and 1, this set is nonempty and bounded below, so Q(p) is a finite real number. Endpoint quantiles at 0 and 1 are outside this convention’s domain and may require infinite boundaries or other conventions.

If F is continuous and strictly increasing and takes every value between 0 and 1, then Q is its ordinary inverse and F(Q(p))=p. If F is flat, F(x)=p may have multiple solutions; the infimum convention selects the left boundary where cumulative probability first reaches p. State the convention when specifying a distribution’s particular quantile.

Flat intervals and exceedance probabilities

Put density f=1/2 on (0,1) and (2,3), and 0 elsewhere. Its total area is 1, and its CDF is flat at 1/2 on [1,2]. Every point of that interval solves F(x)=1/2, but Q(1/2)=1. Also, Q(3/4)=5/2 lies inside the second interval. A continuous CDF does not always have an ordinary inverse.

For a general CDF, F(Q(p))≥p, so P(X>Q(p))≤1−p. A jump can make this inequality strict. For a continuous CDF, F(Q(p))=p, so the exceedance probability is exactly 1−p. For example, the constant X=0 has Q(p)=0 but P(X>0)=0; do not assert exact exceedance probability 1−p for every distribution.

Distribution-specific thresholds and normal tails

For U(a,b), Q(p)=a+(b−a)p. For an exponential distribution with rate λ, Q(p)=−ln(1−p)/λ. The conditions λ>0 and 0<p<1 give a positive waiting threshold. For a normal distribution, Q(p)=μ+σΦ⁻¹(p). The function Φ is strictly increasing and has an inverse, and σ>0 preserves ordering.

The standard normal upper tail may be retained as the exact expression T(z)=1−Φ(z)=∫ᶻ∞ φ(t)dt. For z>0, it also satisfies [z/(1+z²)]φ(z)≤T(z)≤φ(z)/z. These are bounds, not an approximate equality or an actually measured probability. At z=0, do not use the zero denominator in the upper bound; use the exact symmetry value T(0)=1/2.

A worked example, step by step

For the two-interval density f=1/2 described above, find Q(1/2), Q(3/4), and the probabilities of exceeding each quantile.

  1. The CDF is 0 for x≤0; x/2 for 0<x<1; 1/2 for 1≤x≤2; 1/2+(x−2)/2=(x−1)/2 for 2<x<3; and 1 for x≥3.
  2. The set where F(x)≥1/2 is [1,∞), so its infimum is Q(1/2)=1. Do not arbitrarily choose the midpoint of the flat interval.
  3. The level 3/4 is reached on the second increasing interval. Solving (x−1)/2=3/4 gives x=5/2; every smaller x has cumulative probability below 3/4.
  4. The distribution has a density, so F is continuous. We obtain P(X>1)=1/2 and P(X>5/2)=1/4, each equal to 1−p.

Q(1/2)=1 and Q(3/4)=5/2. Their exceedance probabilities are 1/2 and 1/4.

Try it with paper and the lesson

For T~Exp(rate 1/2 min⁻¹), find Q(3/4) and its exceedance probability. For X~N(10,4) measured in cm, write Q(3/4) as an exact Φ⁻¹ expression. Express bounds for the standard normal tail T(2) using φ(2).

Show hint

Here 1−p=1/4; retain ln 4 exactly. Use σ=2 for the normal variable and z=2 for the tail threshold.

Show solution

The exponential quantile is −ln(1/4)/(1/2)=2 ln 4 minutes, with exceedance probability e^(−ln 4)=1/4. The normal quantile is 10+2Φ⁻¹(3/4) cm. The standard normal tail satisfies (2/5)φ(2)≤T(2)≤(1/2)φ(2), where φ(2)=e⁻²/√(2π). Do not attach decimal approximations that have not been computed.

Misconception and counterexample

A quantile is always the unique solution of F(x)=p, and the probability of exceeding it is always 1−p.

For p=1/2 in the two-interval density, all of [1,2] solves the equation, but the infimum convention selects 1. For constant X=0, every 0<p<1 has Q=0 and exceedance probability 0<1−p. Uniqueness and the tail equality each require additional conditions.

μ.

Does substituting p=1 into the exponential quantile formula give a finite value?

Show answer and reasoning

No. This unit’s convention requires p<1.

The term ln(1−p) becomes ln 0 and is not real-defined. As p↑1, the threshold tends to +∞.

Explore further

Proof of quantile properties and tail bounds: Let q be the infimum of A={x:F(x)≥p}. For every n, choose aₙ∈A with q≤aₙ<q+1/n. Monotonicity and right-continuity give F(q)≥p. Hence Q(p)≤x is equivalent to p≤F(x). If F is continuous, then F(y)<p for every y<q; letting y↑q also gives F(q)≤p and therefore equality. This makes the exceedance probability 1−p for a continuous model. Also, if p₁≤p₂, then {x:F(x)≥p₂}⊂{x:F(x)≥p₁}, so Q(p₁)≤Q(p₂). By the event equivalence, the inverse image under Q of each half-line is an interval within (0,1), making Q Borel measurable and allowing Q(U) to be treated as a random variable. For U~U(0,1), the same event equivalence gives P(Q(U)≤x)=P(U≤F(x))=F(x), proving inverse-transform construction of the distribution; no random samples were actually generated. For a normal tail with z>0, use φ′(t)=−tφ(t) and integration by parts to obtain T(z)=φ(z)/z−∫ᶻ∞ φ(t)/t² dt. The last integral is nonnegative, giving the upper bound. For t≥z, that integral is at most T(z)/z²; therefore T(z)(1+1/z²)≥φ(z)/z, giving the lower bound. The infinite-endpoint term φ(t)/t tends to 0, and exponential decay ensures convergence of the integral. These results are proofs for the specified real-valued models, not replacements for a numerical table or observational experiment.
