Subtopic 3.1

Operations on continuous functions

If we add or divide functions to make a new function, does continuity always come along?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • The domain of a function f is the set of allowed inputs. A zero denominator and a negative radicand in a real square root are not allowed.
  • The limit as x→a describes the value approached by outputs at inputs near a. The actual defined value f(a) is a separate item.
  • Continuity at a means that f(a) is defined, a finite limit exists, and that limit equals f(a). At an interval endpoint, check the one-sided approach from within the domain.

Learning goals

  • Assess the continuity of sums, products, quotients and compositions with their domains and conditions.
  • Avoid applying the quotient theorem mechanically at a zero denominator.
  • Match limits and the actual value separately at a piecewise joining point.

Operations that preserve continuity

If real-valued f and g are continuous at a, then f+g, f−g, fg and constant multiples are also continuous at a. The quotient f/g additionally requires g(a)≠0. First check whether the expression is defined both nearby and at a. These rules concern finitely many operations; they do not automatically guarantee the continuity of an arbitrary infinite sum.

Polynomials are continuous on all real numbers. A quotient of polynomials is continuous on its domain, where its denominator is nonzero. Cancelling factors to obtain a simpler expression does not automatically put an excluded point back into the original domain. To add a point, separately specify its value and check the limit.

A composition must also enter the outer function’s domain

If g is continuous at a and f is continuous at g(a), then f∘g, meaning f(g(x)), is continuous at a. The inner output must be an allowed input to f. The real function √(x+4) is defined for x≥−4 and continuous on that domain; at −4, use right continuity. Do not assume real values for x<−4.

Saying that a function is continuous somewhere does not make every composition continuous. Check the outer function at the actual value reached by the inner one. Forgetting the common domain can include forbidden inputs even when combining individually continuous expressions.

Match the value at the joining point

Consider the function 2x+3 for x<1 and kx+1 for x≥1. The left limit is 5; the right limit and f(1) are k+1. Continuity requires k=4, which makes all three values 5. The fact that each piece is a polynomial does not by itself establish continuity at the join.

Continuity is different from a smooth-looking graph. At 0, |x| has value and both one-sided limits equal to 0, so it is continuous, although the two slopes differ. Differentiability is a stronger condition studied later. Use definitions and conditions before relying on a small sketch or a table of a few points.

A worked example, step by step

Find the real domain of h(x)=(x²+1)/(x−2), and determine its continuity and value at x=3. Explain whether the same substitution rule applies at x=2.

  1. Exclude 2, where the denominator x−2 is zero. The domain is ℝ\{2}.
  2. The numerator and denominator are polynomials continuous at 3. The denominator value is 1, which is nonzero.
  3. By quotient continuity, lim(x→3)h(x)=h(3)=(9+1)/1=10.
  4. At x=2, the original function value is undefined and the numerator is 5. The zero denominator prevents applying the quotient theorem or extending the function by setting h(2)=10.

The function is continuous at every point of its domain ℝ\{2}, and h(3)=10. The point 2 is outside the original domain and requires separate treatment.

Try it with paper and the lesson

On paper, find the continuity condition for the function 2x+3 when x<1 and kx+1 when x≥1. Separately write the real domain of √(x+4) and the direction of approach at its left endpoint.

Show hint

Make separate columns for the left limit, right limit and actual value at the join.

Show solution

The left limit is 5; the right limit and actual value are k+1, so k=4. The square-root domain is [−4,∞). At −4, approach from the right and compare the limit 0 with the value 0. These are paper solutions; no numerical code or graphing tool was run.

Misconception and counterexample

The quotient of two continuous functions is continuous on all real numbers regardless of the denominator value.

Although its numerator and denominator are polynomials, 1/x is undefined at x=0. Preserve the quotient domain and the condition g(a)≠0.

Check your understanding

At which real numbers is the polynomial x²−7 continuous?

Show answer and reasoning

At every real number.

It consists of finitely many products and a difference involving x and constants, with no denominator or square-root restriction.

Is √(x+4) continuous as a real function at x=−5?

Show answer and reasoning

That input is outside its domain.

The radicand is −1 and no real value has been defined, so real continuity is not asserted there.

If the two one-sided limits at a join agree, may the actual value be anything?

Show answer and reasoning

No. The actual value must also equal that limit.

The definition of f(a) and its agreement with the limit are separate continuity conditions.

Explore further

Further study: directly verify ε–δ continuity of u(x)=1/(x−2) at a=3. Given ε>0, choose δ=min(1/2,ε/2). If |x−3|<δ, then |x−2|≥1−|x−3|>1/2, so |u(x)−1|=|x−3|/|x−2|<2δ≤ε. At x=3 itself the difference is 0 and the condition also holds. This argument shows why the denominator is kept away from zero; it does not prove that quotient assumptions may be omitted. At x=2, neither the same centre value nor the denominator lower bound is available.

Subtopic 3.2

The intermediate value theorem

If one endpoint value is negative and the other positive, must there be a zero between them?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • A closed bounded interval [a,b] contains all real numbers between finite endpoints a≤b, including both endpoints.
  • A solution of f(c)=0 is a root or zero. Asserting that a solution exists is different from computing its exact value.
  • Continuity is required throughout the interval, including one-sided continuity from within the interval at its endpoints.

Learning goals

  • Apply the intermediate value theorem after checking continuity and the target value’s position.
  • Distinguish existence, uniqueness and numerical approximation by their separate justifications.
  • Show that a root may occur without a sign change, and that a sign change alone is insufficient for a discontinuous function.

A theorem about not skipping values

If a real-valued f is continuous on [a,b], then for every real y between f(a) and f(b), some c∈[a,b] satisfies f(c)=y. If y is strictly between the endpoint values, c can be found in (a,b). If y equals an endpoint value, that endpoint is a solution. The endpoint values need not be in increasing order; interpret the range from the smaller value to the larger.

Continuity is not a condition checked only at two endpoints. A jump can skip the target. For example, a function equal to −1 for x<0 and 1 for x≥0 has opposite-sign endpoint values on [−1,1], but never equals 0. Do not substitute a sign check for the theorem’s continuity assumption.

Separate existence from uniqueness

If f(a)<0<f(b), the value 0 lies between the endpoint values, so there is at least one root. The theorem does not fix the number of roots at one or supply a closed-form solution. A continuous function can rise and fall through the same height several times.

The reverse conclusion, that same-sign endpoint values mean no root, is also invalid. On [1,2], (x−3/2)² equals 1/4 at both endpoints but equals 0 at x=3/2. Here the sign-change test does not guarantee a root; it does not prove the absence of one. Uniqueness needs an additional argument, such as strict increase.

A paper procedure for shrinking the interval

Keep continuity and opposite endpoint signs while checking the midpoint m. If its value is exactly 0, a solution has been obtained. Otherwise choose m and the endpoint of opposite sign as the new interval. In an exact real-arithmetic model the length halves, but finitely many steps do not always return the exact root.

After n halvings, the length is the initial length/2ⁿ. Taking the midpoint of a root-containing interval as an approximation gives an error at most half that length. This relies on the invariant that the interval continues to contain a root and on exact sign decisions. Floating-point rounding and failed function evaluations were not tested in this paper analysis.

A worked example, step by step

Show that f(x)=x³+x−3 has a root in [1,2], then check midpoints 3/2 and 5/4 with exact fractions to shrink the interval twice.

  1. As a polynomial, f is continuous on [1,2]. Since f(1)=−1 and f(2)=7, the intermediate value theorem gives a root c∈(1,2).
  2. At the first midpoint 3/2, f=27/8+12/8−24/8=15/8>0. Keep [1,3/2], between the negative value at 1 and the positive value at 3/2.
  3. At the next midpoint 5/4, f=125/64+80/64−192/64=13/64>0. The new interval is [1,5/4], of length 1/4.
  4. Using its midpoint 9/8 as an approximation gives distance from the contained root ≤1/8. This is not the exact solution value. All fractions were computed on paper.

A root exists in (1,5/4). After two reductions the interval length is 1/4, and the midpoint approximation 9/8 has error bound 1/8. Uniqueness is proved separately below.

Try it with paper and the lesson

For the same f, use the endpoint values on [1,2] to decide whether a solution of f(c)=1 exists. Separately compare the endpoint values and root of (x−3/2)² to explain a case without a sign change.

Show hint

First check whether the target value lies between the endpoint values. Do not confuse a test failing to guarantee existence with a proof of absence.

Show solution

Because −1<1<7 and f is continuous, a solution of f(c)=1 exists in (1,2). The square function has the same positive value 1/4 at both endpoints, but a zero at 3/2. The exact solution value for the target 1 has not been computed.

Misconception and counterexample

The intermediate value theorem found a root, so that root is unique and its numerical value is exact.

The theorem guarantees at least one solution. The example’s fractional interval gives an error bound, not the exact root value. Uniqueness needs an additional proof.

Check your understanding

If continuous f satisfies f(a)=0, is that endpoint a root too?

Show answer and reasoning

Yes.

The solution is not forcibly restricted to the open interval; the actual endpoint value equals the target 0.

Do endpoint values −1 and 1 alone guarantee a zero of a discontinuous step function?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Continuity throughout the interval is missing, and this step function actually skips 0.

If an interval of initial length 2 is halved exactly three times, what is its length?

Show answer and reasoning

1/4.

2/2³=1/4. The midpoint error bound is another half of that, 1/8.

Explore further

Further study: prove without differentiation that f(x)=x³+x−3 is strictly increasing on all real numbers. For x<y, f(y)−f(x)=(y−x)(y²+xy+x²+1)>0. The second factor equals (x+y/2)²+3y²/4+1≥1, and the first is positive. Two different inputs therefore cannot both yield 0, so the previously established root is unique. Induction on the opposite-sign interval reductions also preserves root containment and length 2⁻ⁿ. Keep the assumptions of exact signs, continuity and initial containment, and the limitation that no actual numerical program was run.

Subtopic 3.3

The extreme value theorem

Must every continuous function have a largest and a smallest value?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • A maximum M is actually attained at an allowed input c with f(c)=M, and satisfies f(x)≤M for every x. A minimum needs attainment and the reverse inequality.
  • An upper bound bounds every value from above but need not be attained. The least upper bound is the supremum; the greatest lower bound is the infimum.
  • For real numbers, a closed bounded interval [a,b] includes the endpoints and has finite length. A function value and an input where it is attained are different answers.

Learning goals

  • Check continuity, closedness and boundedness before applying the extreme value theorem.
  • Distinguish maximum and minimum values, their attaining inputs, and upper and lower bounds.
  • Prove simple global extrema by completing the square and compare an open-interval counterexample.

Conditions that guarantee existence

A real-valued function continuous on a nonempty closed bounded interval [a,b] attains both a maximum and a minimum in that interval. This is stronger than merely having bounded values. The theorem does not specify whether an attaining input is an endpoint or an interior point, how many such inputs exist, or the exact extremal values.

Without yet using derivatives, the example compares a completed square with the interval to find values directly. A proof of the general theorem requires analysis tools such as completeness of the real numbers and compactness. A single worked example is not claimed to prove the theorem itself. Finding values and attaining inputs remains a separate task after the existence guarantee.

Global values and where they are attained

For p(x)=5−(x−1)², the square is nonnegative, so p(x)≤5. If x=1 belongs to the domain, the value 5 is attained. On [−1,4], |x−1|≤3, so the square is at most 9; hence p(x)≥−4, attained at x=4.

Do not interchange the maximum value 5 and the attaining input 1. The minimum value is −4 and its attaining input is 4. For this function on this interval, equality conditions also establish unique attaining inputs. For a general continuous function, such as a constant function, every input may attain both the maximum and the minimum.

Read examples with missing assumptions

On the open interval (−1,4), the same p has maximum 5 at the interior input 1, but no minimum. Its values are strictly greater than −4 while approaching −4 as x approaches 4. The infimum −4 exists, but the input 4 is forbidden. The maximum 5 also refutes the claim that an open interval necessarily has no extrema at all.

A closed interval alone is insufficient too. On [0,1], define h(0)=2 and h(x)=x for 0<x≤1. The maximum is 2, but no minimum exists: positive values can be arbitrarily small, yet 0 is never attained. Discontinuity at 0 prevents applying the theorem. Its assumptions are sufficient conditions; failing an assumption does not itself prove the absence of extrema.

A worked example, step by step

On the closed interval [−1,4], find the maximum and minimum of p(x)=5−(x−1)² and their attaining inputs without differentiation.

  1. The polynomial p is continuous on this closed bounded interval, so both extrema exist.
  2. Because (x−1)²≥0, p(x)≤5. The input x=1 is in the interval and p(1)=5, attaining the maximum 5.
  3. In the interval, −2≤x−1≤3, so |x−1|≤3 and its square is at most 9. Therefore p(x)≥−4.
  4. Compare p(4)=−4 and p(−1)=1, and check that 4 is the only allowed equality input for a square of 9. The minimum −4 is attained at 4.

The maximum 5 is attained at x=1; the minimum −4 at x=4. Values and inputs are distinguished and verified by inequalities for every input in the interval.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Change the domain of the same p to the open interval (−1,4). On paper, decide whether its maximum and minimum are attained and find the infimum. How many inputs attain the extrema of the constant function 7 on [−1,4]?

Show hint

Do not include the forbidden input 4 in a function-value table. A constant function gives the same value at every allowed input.

Show solution

On the open interval, p attains maximum 5 at 1, but has no minimum. Its infimum −4 is approached as x→4− and is not attained. The constant function 7 has both maximum and minimum 7, attained at every point of its interval. No graphing or numerical tool was run.

Misconception and counterexample

Bounded values must attain a maximum and a minimum.

On (−1,4), p is bounded by −4<p≤5 but has no minimum −4. Distinguish an approached boundary value from a value attained within the domain.

Check your understanding

Does the theorem require a maximum to be attained at an interior input?

Show answer and reasoning

No. An endpoint is possible.

For example, f(x)=x is continuous on [0,1] and attains maximum 1 at endpoint 1. The general theorem does not restrict maximum or minimum locations to the interior.

Does the continuous function x have a maximum on all of ℝ?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

An input one greater than any given input produces a larger value, and the domain is unbounded.

If any one of continuity, closedness or boundedness is missing, must both extrema be absent?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

The open-interval p still has maximum 5. Being unable to apply a sufficient condition differs from proving absence of extrema.

Explore further

Further study: use |x−1|<3 to prove p(x)>−4 on the open interval. For positive integers n, take x_n=4−1/n; as n→∞, p(x_n)=5−(3−1/n)²→−4. Thus −4 is a lower bound, and no larger number can be a lower bound, so it is the infimum. Because −4 itself is not attained, there is no minimum. The extrema proof for the closed-interval example uses only bounds on a square. This single example does not prove the general extreme value theorem or the performance of an actual optimization program.

Subtopic 3.4

Types of discontinuity and counterexamples

Can changing the value at just one point make every discontinuous function continuous?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • The left and right limits at a use nearby inputs x<a and x>a respectively. Classification points in this unit are interior points with inputs on both sides.
  • Distinguish a finite real limit from divergence to ±∞. Infinity is not a real function value required for continuity here.
  • Changing the defined value at one point does not change surrounding outputs away from that point.

Learning goals

  • Classify removable, jump and infinite discontinuities using one-sided limits and the actual value.
  • Distinguish cases that can and cannot be repaired by redefining a single point.
  • Use an oscillating counterexample to show that these three categories do not exhaust all discontinuities.

A missing value can sometimes fill the hole

At an interior point a, a removable discontinuity has a finite two-sided limit L but a missing function value or a value different from L. Defining just f(a)=L gives a continuous extension. Computing the limit and stating the current domain and value are separate tasks.

The expression r(x)=(x²−9)/(x−3) is defined only for x≠3, where it equals x+3. The limit as x→3 is 6, but cancellation alone does not define r(3). Adding value 5 leaves a discontinuity; adding 6 makes it continuous.

Different one-sided values and infinite divergence

If the finite left and right limits differ, there is a jump discontinuity. For example, assigning −2 for x<0 and 3 for x≥0 gives left limit −2 and right limit 3. No single real redefinition of f(0) can match both surrounding limits.

The function 1/(x−3) diverges to −∞ from the left of 3 and +∞ from the right. This infinite discontinuity cannot be fixed by a finite value at one point either. State the original missing value at 3 separately from the divergence pattern nearby. A few large sampled values do not constitute a proof of infinite divergence.

Oscillation outside these categories and the limits of a metaphor

For x≠0, sin(1/x) keeps oscillating near 0. It has neither finite one-sided jump values, divergence in a fixed infinite direction, nor a finite removable limit. Thus the three common categories do not classify every discontinuity. Below, two approaching sequences with different outputs demonstrate the oscillation.

Drawing a graph without lifting a pencil can be an introductory metaphor, but a small display and finitely many samples cannot establish continuity. Check the domain, actual value, limits and failed conditions algebraically. Also, |x| is continuous at 0, so a pointed shape alone is not a discontinuity. No actual graph or sensor data was tested in this unit.

A worked example, step by step

Define F_k by (x²−9)/(x−3) when x≠3 and by k when x=3. Examine continuity at 3, comparing k=5 and k=6.

  1. For nearby x≠3, cancellation gives (x−3)(x+3)/(x−3)=x+3. This does not directly substitute at the original zero-denominator point.
  2. Both one-sided limits are therefore 6, and the finite two-sided limit is 6.
  3. The actual value F_k(3)=k makes the continuity condition k=6. With k=5 it differs from limit 6.
  4. For k=5 there is a removable discontinuity at 3. Changing just that value to 6 makes it continuous, for a reason different from jump or infinite divergence cases.

With k=6 the function is continuous; with k=5 it has a removable discontinuity. Read limit 6 from the surrounding expression and the actual value from the separately specified piece.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Find the continuity condition for a function equal to x+1 for x<2, 5−x for x>2, and k at x=2. If only the right-hand expression is changed to 6−x, can one value of k repair continuity?

Show hint

Compute the two surrounding limits before considering the actual value, and check whether those limits agree.

Show solution

The first function has left and right limits 3, so k=3 makes it continuous. The changed function has left limit 3 and right limit 4: a jump that no k can repair. These are paper calculations with no numerical execution.

Misconception and counterexample

Every discontinuity can be repaired by assigning a suitable value at one point.

This works for a removable discontinuity with a finite two-sided limit. A point change cannot alter surrounding behaviour with differing one-sided limits, infinite divergence or the oscillation of sin(1/x).

Check your understanding

If the two-sided limit and the actual value both equal 4, is the function discontinuous there?

Show answer and reasoning

It is continuous there.

The three conditions hold: a defined value, a finite limit and agreement between them.

Is a point with finite left limit 1 and right limit 2 removable?

Show answer and reasoning

No. It is a jump discontinuity.

One actual value cannot simultaneously equal two different one-sided limits.

Does assigning ±∞ as the actual function value give real continuity?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

This unit concerns real-valued continuity and finite limits; infinity is not an allowed real value.

Explore further

Further study: take x_n=1/(π/2+2πn) and y_n=1/(3π/2+2πn) for nonnegative integers n, then let n→∞. Both are positive and approach 0, but sin(1/x_n)=1 and sin(1/y_n)=−1, so there cannot be a common finite right limit. The values are bounded in [−1,1], so there is no infinite divergence either. Assigning any value at 0 leaves these surrounding sequence outputs unchanged. This is a paper argument using sequential limits, not evidence of executed trigonometric code, graphs or numerical samples.
