Subtopic 6.1

Derivatives of Exponential and Logarithmic Functions

How do we calculate the instantaneous change of a quantity that grows by a fixed factor when its input increases by 1?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • The derivative f′(a) is the finite real limit of [f(a+h)−f(a)]/h as h→0. We examine changes from both sides at an interior point of an open interval.
  • Composition inserts an inner function value into an outer function. If both are differentiable at the relevant points, the chain rule gives (f∘g)′=(f′∘g)g′.
  • The real exponential aˣ has positive values and is defined here for a>0. The natural logarithm ln is the inverse of eˣ, and its real domain is x>0. The number e is the base of the natural exponential.

Learning goals

  • Check the conditions on the base and logarithm argument first.
  • Differentiate exponential and logarithmic compositions and explain the formulas through inverse functions.

A function that turns addition into multiplication

E(x)=eˣ turns a sum of inputs into a product of values: E(x+h)=E(x)E(h). Its difference quotient is therefore E(x)[E(h)−1]/h. Using the basic natural-exponential limit limₕ→₀(eʰ−1)/h=1 gives E′(x)=E(x). We use this limit as a foundational theorem here; we do not reprove it starting from a construction of e.

For a>0, aˣ=exp(x ln a), so (aˣ)′=aˣ ln a. If 0<a<1, then ln a<0 and the function decreases. For a=1, the exponential is the constant 1 with derivative 0; however, a base-1 logarithm is undefined because there is no inverse function. A negative base does not define this exponential model for every real input.

The logarithmic rate from an inverse function

For x>0, set y=ln x, so eʸ=x. The exponential is differentiable and monotone with derivative eʸ>0, allowing the inverse-function theorem to apply. Differentiating gives eʸy′=1 and hence y′=1/x. A logarithm converts multiplicative comparisons between positive values into additive comparisons, but it has no value at x=0.

For a>0 and a≠1, define logₐ x=ln x/ln a for x>0. The base is a fixed constant, so the derivative is 1/(x ln a). On an open interval where the argument g is differentiable and g(x)>0, (ln g)′=g′/g and (logₐ g)′=g′/(g ln a).

Record the domain along with the value

The function exp(g(x)) is positive on the domain of a differentiable g, with derivative exp(g)g′. In contrast, ln(g(x)) requires the additional condition g>0. Do not simplify a derivative formula and then substitute a point excluded by the original function. A nonzero denominator alone does not ensure that a real logarithm exists.

The separate function ln|x| is defined for x≠0. It equals ln x for x>0 and ln(−x) for x<0, so its derivative is 1/x on both open intervals. This does not extend ln x itself to negative inputs. For example, ln(−2) has no real value, whereas ln|−2|=ln 2.

A worked example, step by step

Find the domain and derivative of f(x)=e^(x²)+ln(2x+1), and the tangent line at x=0.

  1. Solve 2x+1>0 to obtain the domain (−1/2,∞). The exponential term adds no restriction.
  2. The inner function x² has derivative 2x, so the first term has derivative 2x e^(x²).
  3. For the logarithmic term, divide the inner derivative 2 by the argument to obtain 2/(2x+1). Thus f′=2x e^(x²)+2/(2x+1).
  4. At x=0, f(0)=1+0=1 and f′(0)=0+2=2. The tangent is y=1+2x, and x=0 is an interior point of the domain.

For x>−1/2, f′(x)=2x e^(x²)+2/(2x+1). The tangent is y=1+2x.

Try it with paper and the lesson

On paper, find the domain and derivative of g=log₂(x²−1), and evaluate the derivative at x=2. Then explain whether replacing the base by 1 is possible.

Show hint

Solve x²−1>0 first, and retain ln 2 as a positive constant.

Show solution

The domain is x<−1 or x>1. The derivative is g′=2x/[(x²−1)ln 2], so g′(2)=4/(3 ln 2). For base 1, ln 1=0 and 1ˣ has no inverse; consequently log₁ cannot be defined.

Misconception and counterexample

If the denominator in a logarithmic derivative is nonzero, the original function exists.

The formula 1/x for ln x would give the number −1/2 at x=−2, but the original real logarithm ln(−2) does not exist. A formula does not remove its own conditions of application.

Check your understanding

What is (3^(2x))′?

Show answer and reasoning

2·3^(2x) ln 3.

The base 3 is fixed, and the derivative 2 of the exponent 2x is a factor.

What are the derivative and domain of ln(x²)?

Show answer and reasoning

2/x for x≠0.

The condition x²>0 is equivalent to x≠0, and 2x/x²=2/x. Do not fill in the excluded point 0.

What is the sign of the derivative of aˣ when a=1/2?

Show answer and reasoning

Negative for every real x.

The factor aˣ is positive and ln(1/2)<0, so their product is negative.

Explore further

Proof and limits of the logarithm-of-absolute-value formula: If g is differentiable and nonzero on an open interval, continuity at each point makes its sign constant in a neighborhood of that point. In the positive case ln|g|=ln g; in the negative case ln|g|=ln(−g). Both derivatives are g′/g. Thus (ln|g|)′=g′/g throughout the interval. A zero of g has no logarithm, so this proof cannot add that point. For example, g=x²−1 excludes x=±1. The inverse-function formula in the next topic likewise requires checking local conditions first.

Subtopic 6.2

Trigonometric and Inverse Trigonometric Functions

Do the derivative formulas stay the same when the same rotation is expressed in degrees rather than radians?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • On the unit circle, the point at angle θ is (cos θ,sin θ). A radian angle is arc length divided by radius; a full turn is 2π.
  • The function tan θ=sin θ/cos θ is defined only where cos θ≠0. Also, sec θ=1/cos θ.
  • An inverse function turns an output back into an input. Since sin is not one-to-one on its full domain, a branch must be restricted to obtain an inverse. The square root √u denotes the nonnegative value for u≥0.

Learning goals

  • Distinguish radian derivatives from factors introduced by composition.
  • Differentiate inverse trigonometric functions on the interiors of their domains using their principal branches.

The angle unit determines the formula

In this unit, the input x to sin x and cos x is in radians. Their derivatives are cos x and −sin x, respectively. Substituting sin(x+h)=sin x cos h+cos x sin h into the difference quotient gives sin x·(cos h−1)/h+cos x·sin h/h. The basic radian limits 0 and 1 yield cos x. These basic trigonometric limits are used as foundational theorems.

For a function receiving a degree-valued number d, S(d)=sin(πd/180) has derivative (π/180)cos(πd/180). The unit conversion remains as the derivative of the inner function. The quotient rule for tan gives (cos² x+sin² x)/cos² x=sec² x; this does not admit points with cos x=0.

Choose the branch that is inverted

The function arcsin is the inverse of sin restricted to [−π/2,π/2]. Its inputs lie in [−1,1] and outputs in [−π/2,π/2]. The restricted inverse arccos has inputs in [−1,1] and outputs in [0,π]. The restricted inverse arctan has inputs in ℝ and outputs in (−π/2,π/2). In this context, sin⁻¹ denotes arcsin, not 1/sin.

The inverse derivative is the reciprocal of the original derivative when that derivative is nonzero at the corresponding point. For interior inputs −1<x<1 of arcsin, cos(arcsin x)=√(1−x²)>0, giving derivative 1/√(1−x²). The derivatives of arccos and arctan are −1/√(1−x²) and 1/(1+x²). At the endpoints ±1, arcsin and arccos have values, but these finite derivative formulas do not apply.

Two conditions for an inverse composition

The composition arcsin(g(x)) has a value when |g(x)|≤1. Where |g(x)|<1 and g is differentiable, the ordinary chain rule gives g′/√(1−g²). When |g|=1, this division is unavailable, so examine the actual composition through its difference quotient instead. If g is the constant 1, the composition is also constant and has derivative 0, an exception worth noticing.

The identity arcsin(sin x)=x holds only on the chosen branch [−π/2,π/2]. For example, at x=π the left side is 0. An inverse trigonometric function does not recover every full turn; choosing another branch may also change signs or the output range.

A worked example, step by step

For the radian function f(x)=sin(2x)+arcsin(x/2), find the domain, its derivative in the interior, and the tangent at x=0.

  1. The condition |x/2|≤1 gives the domain [−2,2]. Use the general derivative formula on the interior (−2,2).
  2. The derivative of sin(2x) is 2cos(2x). The argument is in radians, and the inner factor 2 is included.
  3. The derivative of arcsin(x/2) is (1/2)/√(1−x²/4). The square root is positive in the interior.
  4. Since f(0)=0 and f′(0)=2+1/2=5/2, the tangent is y=(5/2)x.

For −2<x<2, f′=2cos(2x)+1/[2√(1−x²/4)]. The tangent is y=(5/2)x.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Find the domain, interior derivative and h′(0) of h(x)=arccos(3x). Also find k′(1) for k(x)=arctan(x²).

Show hint

Keep the negative sign for arccos and each inner derivative separate.

Show solution

The domain of h is [−1/3,1/3]; in its interior, h′=−3/√(1−9x²), so h′(0)=−3. The function k is differentiable for all real x, with k′=2x/(1+x⁴), so k′(1)=1.

Misconception and counterexample

An inverse trigonometric function is the reciprocal of the original trigonometric function.

Although arcsin(0)=0, 1/sin(0) does not exist. An inverse recovers an angle, whereas a reciprocal divides by a value. In addition, sin is not one-to-one on its full real domain, so it has no unrestricted inverse.

Check your understanding

For the degree-input function S(d)=sin(πd/180), what is S′(0)?

Show answer and reasoning

π/180.

Multiplying by the derivative of the inner conversion gives a value different from the radian sine derivative 1 at the origin.

Does arcsin x have a finite ordinary two-sided derivative at x=1?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

This is an endpoint of the original domain with no ordinary two-sided neighborhood. The interior derivative also grows without bound as x approaches 1.

What is the derivative of arctan(2x)?

Show answer and reasoning

2/(1+4x²).

Multiply by the inner derivative 2 and square the entire inner value in the denominator.

Explore further

Difference-quotient proof of the inverse derivative: Suppose f is continuous and strictly monotone on an open interval, differentiable at a, and f′(a)≠0. Its inverse g is continuous near f(a). For nearby y≠f(a), put h=g(y)−a. Then h≠0 and h→0. We have [g(y)−g(f(a))]/[y−f(a)]=h/[f(a+h)−f(a)]. The denominator divided by h tends to the nonzero number f′(a), so this quotient tends to 1/f′(a), giving g′(f(a)). On the interior of the arcsin branch, cos a>0; on the interior of the arccos branch, −sin a<0, which also determines the signs. This proof does not apply to endpoints where the denominator derivative is zero or to an unselected branch.

Subtopic 6.3

Implicit Differentiation

Can we find the tangent slope at a point without solving a curve for y explicitly?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • The relation F(x,y)=0 specifies allowed pairs of variable values. It does not say that every x has exactly one y.
  • The partial derivative Fₓ changes x while holding y fixed; Fᵧ changes y while holding x fixed. The condition C¹ means that these partial derivatives exist and are continuous.
  • Local means within a sufficiently small open neighborhood of a point. Along a branch y=g(x), y changes with x, so (y²)′=2yy′.

Learning goals

  • First check the solution point and a locally differentiable branch.
  • Find y′ under conditions allowing division, and distinguish failure of a theorem condition from failure of the function.

A relation differs from a function

The solution set of F=x²+y²−25 is a circle. At x=3 it contains y=4 and y=−4, so the full circle is not the graph of one function of x. However, the upper branch through (3,4), y=√(25−x²), is differentiable near x=3. Specifying the point and branch lets us interpret the relation as a local function.

The implicit-function theorem supplies a sufficient condition: If F is C¹ on an open neighborhood of (a,b), F(a,b)=0, and Fᵧ(a,b)≠0, then the solution set near (a,b) is the graph of a unique C¹ function y=g(x). Uniqueness concerns the solution branch inside a small rectangle; it does not eliminate other branches farther away.

Obtain the slope using the chain rule

Under these conditions, differentiating F(x,g(x))=0 gives Fₓ(x,g(x))+Fᵧ(x,g(x))g′(x)=0. Choose a small neighborhood where Fᵧ remains nonzero to obtain g′=−Fₓ/Fᵧ. This calculation requires either a prior guarantee of local function existence or an already given differentiable branch.

Differentiating the circle gives 2x+2yy′=0, hence y′=−x/y on a branch where y≠0. Do not write just 2y or delete the term as 0 by treating y as constant. For higher derivatives, also check that the branch has the required differentiability. The upper circle branch is smooth in its interior, so it can be differentiated twice.

Interpret a failed sufficient condition correctly

At (5,0) on the circle, Fᵧ=0 and the formula has a zero denominator. The nearby full circle cannot be expressed as a single y function on an open x interval, and its tangent is vertical. Since Fₓ=10≠0, x can instead be expressed locally as a function of y. The choice of dependent variable is part of the conditions.

The equality Fᵧ=0 means that this form of the theorem does not apply; it does not mean that a differentiable y function never exists. For F=(y−x)³, the solutions form the unique branch y=x, yet Fᵧ=0 at solution points. This known branch has derivative 1. Compare this example with the vertical circle tangent rather than converting a sufficient condition into a necessary one.

A worked example, step by step

On the upper branch of x²+y²=25, find y′ and y″ at (3,4), and the tangent line.

  1. Verify 3²+4²=25. Also, Fᵧ=2y=8≠0 and the polynomial F is C¹. The upper branch is smooth for |x|<5.
  2. From 2x+2yy′=0, obtain y′=−x/y, which is −3/4 at the given point.
  3. Differentiate again: 2+2(y′)²+2yy″=0. Thus y″=−[1+(y′)²]/y=−(25/16)/4=−25/64.
  4. The tangent is y−4=−(3/4)(x−3). Differentiating the explicit expression √(25−x²) gives −x/√(25−x²), confirming the same first derivative.

y′=−3/4, y″=−25/64, and the tangent is y−4=−(3/4)(x−3).

Try it with paper and the lesson

For F(x,y)=xy+y²−6 at the solution point (1,2), check the conditions for a local y function and find its slope and tangent line.

Show hint

Compute Fₓ=y and Fᵧ=x+2y. First check whether 1·2+2²−6 equals 0.

Show solution

The point is a solution and the polynomial F is C¹. Since Fᵧ(1,2)=5≠0, a local C¹ branch exists. Its derivative y′=−y/(x+2y) is −2/5 at this point, so the tangent is y−2=−(2/5)(x−1). This is not a claim that globally all solutions give one y for every x.

Misconception and counterexample

If Fᵧ is zero, y can never be a differentiable function of x.

For the C¹ function F=(y−x)³, Fᵧ=0 at solution points, but the solution set y=x is differentiable. In contrast, y²=x at the origin has two branches and a one-sided domain issue. Failure of the sufficient condition alone cannot distinguish these situations.

Check your understanding

What is y′ at (3,−4) on x²+y²=25?

Show answer and reasoning

3/4.

−x/y=−3/(−4), with the opposite slope sign from the upper branch.

Does F=x²+y²+1=0 have a real solution point for implicit differentiation?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

A sum of squares plus 1 is positive, so the assumption F(a,b)=0 fails.

Is checking Fᵧ(a,b)≠0 alone enough?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

The solution-point condition and C¹ regularity on an open neighborhood are also needed. Division in a derivative equation does not replace an existence theorem.

Explore further

Complete remainder derivation of the local derivative formula: For the differentiable branch g provided by the theorem, Δy=g(a+h)−g(a)=g′(a)h+o(h). Real differentiability of the C¹ function F gives F(a+h,b+Δy)−F(a,b)=Fₓ(a,b)h+Fᵧ(a,b)Δy+r, where r/√(h²+Δy²)→0. Since Δy=O(h), we have r/h→0. Both F values are 0; dividing by h≠0 and taking the limit gives 0=Fₓ+Fᵧg′. When Fᵧ≠0, g′=−Fₓ/Fᵧ. The notation o(h) means a remainder whose quotient by h tends to 0; O(h) means its absolute value is bounded by a constant multiple of |h|. This proves the derivative formula for a branch that already exists, not the full existence part of the implicit-function theorem. In the circle example, the explicit branch separately confirms existence.

Subtopic 6.4

Logarithmic Differentiation and Related Rates

How do we differentiate a function whose base and exponent both vary, and the radius of a sphere that changes over time?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • For positive u and real v, define uᵛ=exp(v ln u). With a variable exponent, changes in both the base and exponent contribute.
  • Over the reals, ln(uv)=ln u+ln v requires u>0 and v>0. For negative factors, check separate conditions such as those for ln|u|.
  • If time t is in seconds, radius r in cm, and volume V in cm³, then r′ has units cm/s and V′ has units cm³/s. An instantaneous value r(t₀)=2 does not mean that r is constant for all times.

Learning goals

  • Derive logarithmic differentiation for a positive base and differentiate x^x.
  • Relate rates and their units under the assumption that the relation holds on a time neighborhood.

Two changes in a variable exponent

If u and v are differentiable on an open interval and u>0, then y=uᵛ=exp(v ln u) is positive and differentiable. Differentiating ln y=v ln u gives y′/y=v′ln u+v u′/u. Hence y′=uᵛ[v′ln u+v u′/u]. The constant-exponent power rule and constant-base exponential rule appear as two special cases.

In particular, for x>0, x^x=exp(x ln x), so (x^x)′=x^x(ln x+1). Preserve this open positive domain. Even if some integer or rational exponent values exist separately for negative inputs, this does not automatically produce this function for all real exponents or a smooth neighborhood.

Check conditions before expanding logarithms

Expanding logarithms of products and quotients of positive functions turns a complicated product rule into a sum rule. After calculating y′/y, multiply by the original y again. For a nonzero function, ln|y| is available, but zeros and the domains of the individual factors must also be excluded. At a zero where the logarithm cannot be taken, calculate the derivative separately from the original expression.

For example, y=x·eˣ satisfies ln y=ln x+x for x>0, giving y′=y(1/x+1)=eˣ(1+x). For x<0, ln|y| gives the same expression. At x=0, logarithmic differentiation is unavailable, but the original product rule gives y′(0)=1. Distinguish failure of the method from failure of differentiability of the original function.

Differentiate in time before substituting instantaneous values

In a hypothetical perfect-sphere model, suppose differentiable r(t)>0 and V(t)=4πr(t)³/3 are given on an open neighborhood of time t₀. The chain rule gives V′=4πr²r′. Knowing V′ and r at the same time gives r′=V′/(4πr²). This is a calculation within a model where the geometric relation holds, not a validation of an actual balloon’s shape, leakage, or measurement accuracy.

Substituting r=2 into the original volume formula first turns it into a constant and erases the change. Differentiate first, then substitute the values at t₀. Division is unavailable at r=0; if r is differentiable and the relation holds, then V′=0 at that time. A positive nonzero volume inflow rate cannot be imposed there while keeping all the assumptions.

A worked example, step by step

First find f′(1) for f=x^x with x>0. Second, in the sphere model above, find r′(t₀) when r(t₀)=2 cm and V′(t₀)=12π cm³/s.

  1. Since f>0, ln f=x ln x. Differentiate to obtain f′/f=ln x+1.
  2. Thus f′=x^x(ln x+1). Since f(1)=1 and ln 1=0, f′(1)=1.
  3. Differentiate V=4πr³/3 on a time neighborhood to obtain V′=4πr²r′, then substitute the data for the stated instant.
  4. Since 12π=4π·2²·r′=16πr′, we obtain r′=3/4 cm/s. Dividing cm³/s by cm² also gives cm/s.

f′(1)=1, and the hypothetical sphere’s radius changes at 3/4 cm/s at that instant.

Try it with paper and the lesson

On paper, find g′ and g′(1) for g(x)=(x²+1)^x. Separately, in the same sphere model, calculate r′ when r=3 cm and V′=−18π cm³/s, and interpret its sign.

Show hint

The base x²+1 is positive for every real x. Differentiate ln g=x ln(x²+1), and use values at the same time in the sphere equation.

Show solution

We obtain g′=(x²+1)^x[ln(x²+1)+2x²/(x²+1)] and g′(1)=2(ln 2+1). For the sphere, r′=(−18π)/(4π·9)=−1/2 cm/s. The negative sign means that the radius decreases under these assumptions; it is not evidence of an observed physical contraction.

Misconception and counterexample

To differentiate x^x, treat the exponent x as constant and write x·x^(x−1).

That expression leaves only x^x and misses the ln x term. At x=e, the actual derivative is 2e^e, whereas the incorrect expression gives e^e. A changing base alone differs from a base and exponent that both change.

Check your understanding

If v is the constant c in uᵛ with u>0, what does the general formula become?

Show answer and reasoning

c u^(c−1)u′.

Here v′=0, leaving uᶜ·c u′/u.

For a sphere with r=2 cm and V′=0, what is r′?

Show answer and reasoning

0 cm/s.

Divide by the positive denominator 16π cm² to get 0. Only the given instantaneous zero volume rate is used.

Can differentiable r with r(t₀)=0 and V′(t₀)=1 cm³/s be assumed together with the sphere relation?

Show answer and reasoning

All those conditions cannot hold together.

The chain rule forces V′(t₀)=4π·0²·r′(t₀)=0, contradicting the nonzero data.

Explore further

Proof and existence limits for general related rates: Suppose F is C¹ around (u(t),v(t)) near time t₀, u and v are differentiable, and F(u(t),v(t))=0 throughout a time neighborhood. The chain rule for composition gives Fᵤu′+Fᵥv′=0. If Fᵥ≠0, then v′=−Fᵤu′/Fᵥ. However, if the relation happens to hold only at one instant, this derivative equation does not follow. As a counterexample, F(u,v)=v−u with u(t)=0 and v(t)=t satisfies F=0 at t=0, yet v′−u′=1. For a sphere, given positive r₀ and a positive volume V with continuous V′, r=(3V/(4π))^(1/3) constructs a locally differentiable radius. The example’s instantaneous data can therefore be consistently realized as actual mathematical functions on a small interval with positive volume. This is mathematical existence, not a physical experiment. Specifically, define V(t)=32π/3+12π(t−t₀) cm³ on a sufficiently small time neighborhood where it stays positive, and construct r using the formula above. This produces r(t₀)=2 cm and r′(t₀)=3/4 cm/s as an actual function model. The numerical time values are in seconds; no physical observations were conducted.
