Subtopic 4.1

Difference Quotients and Tangents

Can narrowing the interval between two points give the slope at one point?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • A slope is a change in output divided by a change in input. Two points with the same input give denominator 0, so this ratio is not calculated.
  • Assume a real-valued function f is defined on an open interval containing a. Both sufficiently small positive and negative h give allowed inputs a+h; form the difference quotient only for h≠0.
  • A limit differs from directly substituting h=0. Check whether approaching 0 from both sides gives the same finite value.

Learning goals

  • Calculate the difference quotient for valid h before finding its derivative limit.
  • Distinguish the derivative from the function value and construct the tangent equation.
  • Distinguish estimates from finite samples from a general limit proof, and recognize that a tangent can cross a curve.

From a two-point slope to a definition at one point

The difference quotient Q(h)=[f(a+h)−f(a)]/h is the slope of the secant through distinct points (a,f(a)) and (a+h,f(a+h)). It requires h≠0 and both inputs in the domain. Small h alone does not make Q(h) the exact derivative.

The derivative f′(a) is the finite two-sided limit of this quotient as h→0, when that limit exists. At h=0 the original expression is 0/0 and is not evaluated directly. Expand or cancel terms where h≠0, then investigate the limit of the simpler expression.

A tangent needs both a point and a slope

If the derivative m exists, the tangent is y=f(a)+m(x−a). A line with the correct slope but passing through a different point is not the tangent at this point. f(a) is an output value and f′(a) is a rate of change; do not assume they are the same number.

A tangent need not meet a curve only once or remain on one side of it. At 0, f(x)=x³ has quotient h²→0 and tangent y=0, but the curve crosses that line there. Use the limit and the point on the line as the definition, rather than a picture described as “just touching.”

Finite tables differ from arbitrarily close approaches

Our example f(x)=2x²−x+1 at a=2 gives Q(h)=7+2h. For h=1/10 it is 36/5=7.2; for h=−1/10 it is 34/5=6.8. Smaller h gives numbers nearer 7, but these two table entries alone do not prove the limit for all h.

The general expansion Q(h)=7+2h gives |Q(h)−7|=2|h|, handling every sufficiently small allowed h together. Separate a formula establishing the derivative from a numerical estimation method. Actual floating-point subtraction of close values and division involve rounding; smaller h does not always improve an estimate.

A worked example, step by step

Using only the definition by difference quotient, find the derivative and tangent of f(x)=2x²−x+1 at a=2.

  1. f(2)=8−2+1=7. Also f(2+h)=2(4+4h+h²)−2−h+1=7+7h+2h².
  2. For h≠0, Q(h)=[7h+2h²]/h=7+2h. This cancellation does not evaluate the original quotient at h=0.
  3. The two-sided limit as h→0 is 7, so f′(2)=7. The value and slope happen to equal 7 in this example, but their concepts differ.
  4. Using point (2,7) and slope 7 gives y=7+7(x−2)=7x−7. Check that the line passes through output 7 at x=2.

The derivative is 7 and the tangent is y=7x−7. The expansion and limit were checked on paper; no numerical program was run.

Try it with paper and the lesson

For the same f, find the difference quotient and tangent at a=1 on paper. Calculate the secant slope for h=1/10 and compare it with the derivative.

Show hint

f(1)=2. Expand f(1+h) first. The tangent needs point (1,2), as well as its slope.

Show solution

f(1+h)=2+3h+2h², so Q(h)=3+2h for h≠0 and f′(1)=3. The tangent is y=2+3(x−1)=3x−1. The secant slope at h=1/10 is 16/5=3.2, different from the exact derivative 3. These are paper calculations.

Misconception and counterexample

Substituting h=0 in a difference quotient calculates the derivative, and a tangent cannot cross the curve.

The original quotient is 0/0, so a limit is needed. At 0, x³ has Q(h)=h²→0 and tangent y=0, which crosses the curve. Both claims mechanically apply an analogy instead of the definition.

Check your understanding

Is h=0 allowed in Q(h)?

Show answer and reasoning

It is not allowed in the original difference quotient.

Its denominator is 0. A limit of the expression for h≠0 differs from direct substitution.

Is every line of slope 7 the tangent at x=2 in the example?

Show answer and reasoning

No. It must pass through (2,7).

Among lines with that slope, only y=7x−7 passes through that point; another intercept gives a different line.

Does f(1)=2 necessarily mean f′(1)=2?

Show answer and reasoning

No. Our example has f′(1)=3.

An output value and a rate of change have different definitions.

Explore further

Extension: For any ε>0 choose δ=ε/2. If 0<|h|<δ, then |Q(h)−7|=2|h|<ε, proving derivative 7 by definition. The actual output error from the tangent is f(2+h)−[7+7h]=2h²; for h≠0 its ratio to |h| is 2|h|→0. This is an exact identity for this polynomial, not a claim that every differentiable function has the same remainder 2h². One-sided derivatives at endpoints and infinite slopes must be defined separately from the open-interval, finite two-sided derivative adopted here.

Subtopic 4.2

Differentiability and Continuity

If a function has no break, must its slope be uniquely determined at every point?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Continuity at an interior point a means the actual value is defined and the finite two-sided limit equals that value.
  • Differentiability at a requires a finite two-sided limit of [f(a+h)−f(a)]/h for a function defined on an open interval containing a.
  • Even if the left and right difference-quotient limits each exist, unequal values mean there is no two-sided derivative. Distinguish one-sided limits of the function itself from those of its quotient.

Learning goals

  • Prove that differentiability implies continuity and give a counterexample to the converse.
  • Test matching values and matching slopes separately for a piecewise function.
  • Distinguish a diverging quotient from a finite derivative and distinguish one-sided endpoint approaches.

A defined slope requires the values to join

A function differentiable at an interior point is continuous there. For h≠0 write f(a+h)−f(a)=hQ(h). If Q(h) approaches finite L, this product approaches 0. The argument requires f(a) to be defined and nearby inputs to be allowed.

Continuity concerns the approach of outputs; it does not guarantee the approach of their small difference divided by h. g(x)=|x−2| is continuous at 2 because both the value and the two-sided limit are 0, but its left and right slopes differ. Do not reverse the implication into continuity⇒differentiability.

Separate the two tests at a junction

The function ax+2 for x<0 and bx+2 for x≥0 has both one-sided limits and actual value 2 at 0, so it is continuous for every real a,b. But its quotient is a for h<0 and b for h>0: differentiability requires a=b. Joining equal constant values does not also match slopes.

By contrast, the function x² for x≠0 and 1 for x=0 is discontinuous at 0: the limit 0 differs from its value 1, although the nearby formula is smooth. The actual quotient is (h²−1)/h=h−1/h, with no finite two-sided limit. Ignoring the current value and differentiating only the nearby polynomial does not differentiate the original function.

The scope of infinite slopes and one-sided derivatives

f(x)=√x is right-continuous at 0 on its domain [0,∞). Its quotient for h>0 is √h/h=1/√h, which diverges to +∞. Do not treat this as a real-valued derivative. Also, 0 is an endpoint, not a point to which the earlier open-interval two-sided definition directly applies.

Continuous nondifferentiable cases are not limited to corners. Unequal left and right quotients of |x| at interior point 0 differ from the diverging one-sided quotient of √x at its endpoint. These two cases do not completely classify every form of nondifferentiability, and we do not claim to judge it from sampled plots.

A worked example, step by step

Use the definitions to judge continuity and differentiability of g(x)=|x−2| at x=2 separately.

  1. g(2)=0 and |g(2+h)−g(2)|=|h|→0, so g is continuous at 2.
  2. For h>0, [g(2+h)−g(2)]/h=|h|/h=1. The right quotient limit is 1.
  3. For h<0, |h|=−h, so the quotient is −1 and the left quotient limit is −1.
  4. The two finite one-sided quotient limits differ, so no two-sided derivative exists. This does not contradict continuity, which says the function-value limits both equal 0.

It is continuous but not differentiable at 2, because the left and right slopes −1 and 1 differ. This is a paper limit argument; no graph was run.

Try it with paper and the lesson

For the function ax+2 when x<0 and bx+2 when x≥0, find the continuity and differentiability conditions at 0. Compare a=1,b=3 with a=b=3, and also write the right-hand difference quotient of √x at 0.

Show hint

First compare actual value 2 with the one-sided function limits; then write [the expression on the h side−2]/h. Infinite divergence is not a finite derivative.

Show solution

The function is continuous at 0 for all real a,b and differentiable only when a=b. For a=1,b=3 it is continuous but not differentiable, with left slope 1 and right slope 3. For a=b=3 the derivative is 3. √x is right-continuous at 0, but its h>0 quotient 1/√h→+∞ gives no finite right derivative, and 0 is not an interior point of an open interval for the two-sided definition. These judgments are on paper.

Misconception and counterexample

A connected graph means differentiability, and substituting into the derivative of the nearby formula makes a changed current value irrelevant.

|x−2| is continuous but its corner has unequal one-sided quotients. Changing x² at 0 to value 1 makes the function discontinuous there, and quotient h−1/h diverges. A derivative uses both current and nearby values.

Check your understanding

Does a finite derivative at an interior point imply continuity?

Show answer and reasoning

Yes.

The quotient approaches a finite value and the change equals h times that quotient, approaching 0.

Do equal one-sided function limits imply equal one-sided quotient limits?

Show answer and reasoning

Not necessarily.

For |x−2| the function limits both equal 0 but the quotients approach −1 and 1.

Should +∞ be written as a finite right derivative of √x at 0?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Our derivative is a finite real value, and the one-sided endpoint scope must be distinguished.

Explore further

Extension: If Q(h)→L∈ℝ, some δ₁>0 gives |Q(h)−L|<1 whenever 0<|h|<δ₁, hence |Q(h)|≤|L|+1. For ε>0 choose δ=min(δ₁,ε/(|L|+1)); then |f(a+h)−f(a)|=|hQ(h)|<ε. For h=0 the difference is 0, proving continuity. This quantitative argument for a small change requires a nearby open domain and a finite quotient limit. Differentiability at one point does not imply that the derivative exists everywhere nearby or is continuous there.

Subtopic 4.3

Velocity and Rates of Change

If you return to your starting position, must the distance traveled and average velocity both be zero?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Position s(t) on a straight line is a coordinate with a chosen reference point and positive direction. Negative position means the opposite side of the reference, not negative distance.
  • Average velocity is change in position, or displacement, divided by elapsed time. Distance traveled is path length, adding returning segments positively, so distinguish it from displacement.
  • Instantaneous velocity v(t) is s′(t) at differentiable times, with units m/s. Instantaneous speed is |v(t)|, and average speed is distance traveled divided by elapsed time.

Learning goals

  • Use definitions to obtain average velocity, instantaneous velocity and instantaneous speed for the same position model.
  • Distinguish displacement from distance traveled when motion changes direction.
  • Preserve output/input units and distinguish model calculations from actual device measurements.

Preserve the sign of a position change

Our model is s(t)=t²−6t+8 meters for t≥0 seconds. This formula takes the numerical value of t measured in seconds; a dimensioned expression assigns coefficients 1 m/s², −6 m/s and 8 m respectively. Negative s is a position on the opposite side of the chosen coordinate system, and negative v is instantaneous change in that direction.

Average velocity is [s(b)−s(a)]/(b−a) for b>a. Dividing displacement by time loses information about the return path. Even with identical starting and ending positions, average velocity alone cannot distinguish remaining still from traveling out and back.

Instantaneous velocity is a limit of short intervals

The model gives s(t+h)−s(t)=(2t−6)h+h², so for h≠0 the average rate is 2t−6+h. At interior times t>0 its finite two-sided limit gives v(t)=2t−6 m/s. At t=2, for example, velocity is −2 m/s and instantaneous speed is 2 m/s.

v=0 at t=3 does not imply constant position or no movement at neighboring times. The actual model s(t)=(t−3)²−1 changes direction around 3. Distinguish a one-sided derivative at endpoint t=0 from an open-interval interior calculation. This paper model does not test sensor noise or sampling intervals.

Use the same units rule for other rates

Even when the input is not time, a derivative has output units divided by input units. For a fictional A(r)=r², interpret r as a numerical length in cm and A as a numerical area in cm²; A′(r)=2r has units cm²/cm=cm. This is the local area-change ratio per 1 cm of input length, not the area itself.

Checking the derivative’s sign together with numerator and denominator units helps identify misinterpretations. Discrete experimental data do not directly measure the limiting process at a point; approximation and error models need separate examination. This exact polynomial is our own mathematical model, not a measurement of a real object or area.

A worked example, step by step

For the model s(t)=t²−6t+8 m with t≥0 seconds, find average velocity on [1,3], and instantaneous velocity and speed at t=2.

  1. s(1)=1−6+8=3 m and s(3)=9−18+8=−1 m. Coordinate −1 on the opposite side of the reference is a valid position.
  2. Displacement is −4 m and elapsed time 2 s, so average velocity is −2 m/s. Do not call −4 m the distance traveled.
  3. s(2+h)−s(2)=−2h+h², giving quotient −2+h→−2. Instantaneous velocity v(2)=−2 m/s.
  4. Instantaneous speed is |−2|=2 m/s. Average and instantaneous velocities happen to match numerically here; this is not a theorem for all motion.

Average velocity is −2 m/s; instantaneous velocity at t=2 is −2 m/s and instantaneous speed is 2 m/s. These are paper model calculations, not measurements of movement or sensors.

Try it with paper and the lesson

For the same model on [2,4], find average velocity, distance traveled and average speed. Use s(t)=(t−3)²−1 to judge monotonic direction on [2,3] and [3,4] separately.

Show hint

s(2)=0,s(3)=−1,s(4)=0. Add the absolute displacements of the first and second halves, after checking that neither segment reverses direction.

Show solution

On [2,3] the square term decreases from 1 to 0 and position decreases from 0 to −1. On [3,4] position increases from −1 to 0. Distance traveled is therefore 1+1=2 m. Total displacement is 0 m and time is 2 s, giving average velocity 0 m/s and average speed 1 m/s. Using absolute total displacement 0 as distance would be wrong. These are paper arguments.

Misconception and counterexample

Average velocity 0 means no distance was traveled, and velocity and speed are the same.

Our [2,4] model returns to the same position with average velocity 0, but travels 2 m. Velocity −2 and speed 2 at t=2 also differ in sign. Preserve path and direction in the definitions.

Check your understanding

Does s(3)=−1 m mean distance traveled is negative?

Show answer and reasoning

No. It is a coordinate on the opposite side of the reference.

Position coordinates and path length are different quantities.

Does v(3)=0 imply s is constant for all time?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

s(2)=0,s(3)=−1,s(4)=0 change; t=3 is one instant of reversal.

What units result from differentiating position in m with respect to time in s?

Show answer and reasoning

m/s.

A rate divides output-change units by input units.

Explore further

Extension: For this particular model and a<b, factor s(b)−s(a)=(b−a)(a+b−6), so average velocity is a+b−6. At midpoint time m=(a+b)/2, instantaneous velocity is 2m−6=a+b−6, the same value. This exact quadratic identity does not mean midpoint velocity always equals average velocity for general motion. The contrasting model s(t)=t³ on [0,2] has average velocity 4 but derivative 3 at midpoint t=1, because quotient 3+3h+h²→3. Attach units through each model’s coefficients. No motion device, timing measurement or experiment with a general mean value theorem was performed.

Subtopic 4.4

Linear Approximation and Differentials

When a tangent estimates a difficult function value, can we also state a bound for its error?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • The derivative f′(a) at an interior point a is a finite rate for small input changes. The tangent is L(x)=f(a)+f′(a)(x−a).
  • dx is the selected input change and dy=f′(a)dx is the tangent’s output change. Do not assume it equals actual Δy=f(a+dx)−f(a).
  • Distinguish approximation ≈ from equality =. Absolute error is the magnitude of the difference between actual and approximate values; a guarantee needs a separate remainder argument.

Learning goals

  • Find the required slope through a quotient and construct a linear approximation.
  • Compare an actual change with differential dy in an exact example.
  • Distinguish local approximation from an exact error bound and explain the remainder condition for differentiability.

Approximate a function value with its tangent

If f is differentiable at a, approximate nearby f(x) by L(x)=f(a)+f′(a)(x−a). L is a first-degree expression in x using both f(a) and slope. Generally f and L agree at a without agreeing everywhere else. Saying “nearby” alone does not automatically supply a numerical error for any fixed input.

For our g(x)=√x at a=16, g(16)=4. For h≠0 its quotient (√(16+h)−4)/h=1/(√(16+h)+4), approaching 1/8. Choose an open neighborhood such as |h|<1, where 16+h>0, to check both directions and the nonzero denominator.

Distinguish differential dy from actual change

The approximation for g is L(x)=4+(x−16)/8. If dx=0.16=4/25, then dy=0.02=1/50 and L(16.16)=4.02. Actual Δy is √16.16−4, however; do not substitute 0.02 as an equal value. Below, squares and a positive denominator give an exact error bound.

For our other function f(x)=x² at a=2, L(2+h)=4+4h while f(2+h)=4+4h+h², giving Δy=4h+h² and dy=4h. For dx=−0.04, dy=−0.16 and actual Δy=−0.1584, with difference 0.0016. Even an exactly defined derivative yields an output approximation with error.

Express locality as an explicit ratio condition

Differentiability can be rewritten as f(a+h)=f(a)+f′(a)h+r(h), with r(h)/h→0 for h≠0. Indeed, r(h)/h=Q(h)−f′(a). This says the error becomes small relative to the input change; it does not by itself imply r is always a constant times h².

Error direction can also vary by function. The exact quadratic x² is h² above its tangent, whereas the square-root example has the approximating line above the actual value. Use this example’s identities and positivity conditions rather than a plot as a numerical proof. No calculator or other approximation algorithm was run here.

A worked example, step by step

Use the linear approximation of g(x)=√x at a=16 to estimate √16.16. Without a calculator, prove the estimate is above the actual value and has error <0.00005.

  1. Use g(16)=4 and the earlier quotient limit g′(16)=1/8 to construct L(x)=4+(x−16)/8. Input 16.16 is in the domain.
  2. The input change gives 0.16/8=0.02, hence L(16.16)=4.02. This is an approximate output, not the exact value of √16.16.
  3. 4.02²=16.1604>16.16 and both values being compared are positive, so 4.02>√16.16. Their squares differ by 0.0004.
  4. Error E=4.02−√16.16=0.0004/(4.02+√16.16). Since √16.16>4, the denominator is >8, giving 0<E<0.0004/8=0.00005.

The estimate 4.02 is an overestimate with absolute error less than 0.00005. This is a paper proof using exact fractions and squares, not an observed output of a square-root program.

Try it with paper and the lesson

For f(x)=x² at a=2, choose dx=−0.04 and calculate L(1.96), actual f(1.96), dy, Δy and error on paper. What are dy and Δy when dx=0?

Show hint

1.96=2−0.04 and (2+h)²=4+4h+h². Record the sign of the decrease separately from positive error.

Show solution

Misconception and counterexample

Because dy=f′dx is exact, dy=Δy for every dx and linear approximation has no error.

For x² at a=2, Δy−dy=h², so they generally differ for h≠0. Linear approximation uses the first-order part of an input change; it is not an identity replacing the entire original function with a line.

Does r(h)/h→0 mean every differentiable function necessarily has error h²?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

It is convergence of an error/input ratio, not a guarantee of a particular remainder formula or quadratic bound.

Extension: Differentiability and r(h)/h→0 are equivalent by the identity above. For any ε>0, sufficiently small h gives |r(h)|≤ε|h|, an error-ratio bound. Conversely, that limit and r(0)=0 give quotient→f′(a). In the direction where the derivative is not already known, use a real number L and assume f(a+h)=f(a)+Lh+r(h); then the derivative is L. A quadratic remainder is not always required: F(x)=|x|^(3/2) at a=0 has quotient sign(h)√|h|→0, derivative 0 and r=|h|^(3/2). Although r/|h|→0, r/h²=1/√|h| diverges, so r is not O(h²). Here r=O(h²) means there exist C,δ>0 such that 0<|h|<δ implies |r(h)|≤C|h|². This argument uses a finite real two-sided limit, an interior point and the allowed domain; tool execution does not replace it.
