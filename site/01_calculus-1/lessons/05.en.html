Subtopic 5.1

Product and Quotient Rules

When both quantities change, how does their product change?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Differentiability at a point means that the difference quotient using the actual function value has a finite real limit; it implies continuity at that point.
  • For a positive integer n, (xⁿ)′=nxⁿ⁻¹; a constant has derivative 0, and the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives.
  • A product fg and a quotient f/g differ from a composition. A quotient excludes points where its original denominator is zero.

Learning goals

  • Apply the product and quotient rules with their conditions.
  • Check the result by expanding, and preserve the original domain when cancelling factors.

A product with two changing factors

If f and g are defined on a common open interval and differentiable at a, then (fg)′(a)=f′(a)g(a)+f(a)g′(a). Add the effect of one changing factor to the effect of the other. Mark where a function value and a derivative belong before multiplying terms.

Differentiability of both original factors is a sufficient condition. The reverse implication does not follow from differentiability of fg. If f=g=|x|, neither factor is differentiable at 0, but their product is x² and is differentiable there.

A quotient with a changing denominator

Under the same differentiability conditions, if g(a)≠0, then (f/g)′(a)=[f′(a)g(a)−f(a)g′(a)]/g(a)². Continuity of g supplies a small interval around a where the denominator remains nonzero. Reversing the order in the numerator changes the sign.

The expression f′/g′ is not a general quotient rule. In particular, if g is a nonzero constant, then g′=0, but f/g is differentiable. Its derivative is f′/g, which does not require division by a derivative equal to zero.

Rewriting does not fill a hole

P(x)=(x²+2)(x−3) is defined for every real x. H(x)=(x²+2)/(x−3), however, excludes x=3. Simplifying a formula and defining a new function value are different actions.

For example, (x²−1)/(x−1)=x+1 only when x≠1. The original function has no value at 1 and therefore no derivative there. A new function with the missing value separately defined as 2 equals x+1 everywhere; only then does its derivative at 1 equal 1.

A worked example, step by step

Find P′ and H′ for P(x)=(x²+2)(x−3) and H(x)=(x²+2)/(x−3). Evaluate both at x=1 and state their domains.

  1. Set f=x²+2, f′=2x, g=x−3, and g′=1. P has domain ℝ; H has domain {x∈ℝ:x≠3}.
  2. P′=2x(x−3)+(x²+2)=3x²−6x+2. Expanding P=x³−3x²+2x−6 and differentiating gives the same result.
  3. H′=[2x(x−3)−(x²+2)]/(x−3)²=(x²−6x−2)/(x−3)², for x≠3.
  4. At x=1, P′(1)=−1 and H′(1)=−7/4. This point belongs to both original domains.

P′(x)=3x²−6x+2 on ℝ; H′(x)=(x²−6x−2)/(x−3)² for x≠3. At 1 the values are −1 and −7/4.

Try it with paper and the lesson

At a=1, suppose u(1)=3, v(1)=−2, u′(1)=4, and v′(1)=5. Find the derivatives of uv and u/v at 1. Separately, find R′ for R(x)=x/(x+2), and evaluate R′(0).

Show hint

Write u′v and uv′ separately. The product uses their sum, whereas the quotient uses their difference and denominator v². Keep the domain of R.

Show solution

(uv)′(1)=4(−2)+3·5=7; (u/v)′(1)=[4(−2)−3·5]/(−2)²=−23/4. The data are consistent with u(x)=4x−1 and v(x)=5x−7. R′(x)=2/(x+2)² for x≠−2, so R′(0)=1/2. These are paper calculations.

Misconception and counterexample

Differentiate a product by multiplying the derivatives, and a quotient by dividing them.

For x·x, the derivative is 2x, not 1·1. For x/2, the derivative is 1/2, not 1/0. The product rule mixes values with derivatives; the quotient rule divides by the square of the original denominator.

Check your understanding

Is the quotient-rule denominator g′(a)²?

Show answer and reasoning

No. It is g(a)², and the original value g(a) must be nonzero.

The common denominator in the difference quotient uses g(a+h)g(a), which tends to g(a)².

Does cancelling (x−1) in (x²−1)/(x−1) make the original function differentiable at 1?

Show answer and reasoning

No. Only a separately defined extension can have a derivative there.

Cancellation is valid for x≠1. The original function still has no value at 1.

If fg is differentiable at 0, must both f and g be differentiable there?

Show answer and reasoning

No. Take f=g=|x|.

Each factor has unequal one-sided difference quotients at 0, whereas fg=x² is differentiable there.

Explore further

Let Δf=f(a+h)−f(a) and Δg=g(a+h)−g(a). For h≠0, the product difference quotient is (Δf/h)g(a+h)+f(a)(Δg/h). Differentiability and continuity give the limit f′(a)g(a)+f(a)g′(a). For the quotient, the difference quotient is [(Δf/h)g(a)−f(a)(Δg/h)]/[g(a+h)g(a)]. Continuity and g(a)≠0 ensure that the denominator stays nonzero near a and tends to g(a)². All small nonzero h used in these identities must keep both functions defined; the proof never substitutes h=0.

Subtopic 5.2

Composition and the Chain Rule

When a change passes through several layers, where do we evaluate the outer rate?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • A composition f∘g sends x to g(x) and then to f(g(x)); the inner output must lie in the domain of f.
  • f′(u) is the outer rate at its input u; g′(x) is the inner rate at x.
  • Differentiability at a point does not mean that the function value equals the derivative there.

Learning goals

  • Identify inner and outer functions and the input at which each derivative is evaluated.
  • Handle a zero inner derivative, and distinguish sufficient conditions from their converse.

The inner output is the outer input

If g is differentiable at a, f is differentiable at g(a), and f∘g is defined near a, then (f∘g)′(a)=f′(g(a))g′(a). Evaluate the outer derivative at g(a), not at g′(a).

For h(x)=(2x²−1)³, the inner function is g(x)=2x²−1 and the outer function is f(u)=u³. Their derivatives are 4x and 3u², respectively, giving h′(x)=3(2x²−1)²·4x.

Keep the layers and their values separate

For H(x)=((x+2)²+3)³, the layers are x→v=x+2→u=v²+3→u³. Multiply the rates 3u², 2v, and 1. Writing 3(2v)² instead would incorrectly insert a derivative into the outer input.

A product and a composition can occur together. For xh(x), first use the product rule: its derivative is h(x)+x·12x(2x²−1)². A product has two additive contributions, whereas nested layers require the chain rule.

A zero inner rate and the converse

If g′(a)=0 and the outer derivative exists at g(a), the composition has derivative 0. This does not say that g is constant near a: g(x)=x² has derivative 0 at 0 without being locally constant.

The converse fails. With f(u)=|u| and g(x)=x², the composition is x² and is differentiable at 0, but f is not differentiable at 0. We cannot invent an outer derivative at the corner and multiply it by zero.

A worked example, step by step

Differentiate h(x)=(2x²−1)³ and H(x)=((x+2)²+3)³, then evaluate h′(1) and H′(0).

  1. For h, set g(x)=2x²−1 with g′(x)=4x, and f(u)=u³ with f′(u)=3u². These polynomials are defined on ℝ. Both polynomials are differentiable at every real input.
  2. h′(x)=12x(2x²−1)². At x=1, g(1)=1, so h′(1)=12.
  3. For H, use x→v=x+2→u=v²+3→u³. Thus H′(x)=6(x+2)((x+2)²+3)².
  4. At x=0, v=2 and u=7, while their rates are 1 and 4. Hence H′(0)=3·49·4=588. Do not confuse the values 2 and 7 with the rates 1 and 4.

h′(x)=12x(2x²−1)² and h′(1)=12; H′(x)=6(x+2)((x+2)²+3)² and H′(0)=588.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Find G′ and G′(1) for G(x)=(3x−2)⁴. Then consider f(u)=u² and g(x)=x²: differentiate their composition at 0.

Show hint

The first inner derivative is 3. In the second question, the composition equals x⁴.

Show solution

G′(x)=12(3x−2)³ and G′(1)=12. The second composition is x⁴, whose derivative is 4x³=(2x²)(2x). At 0 this is 0, even though the inner function is not constant.

Misconception and counterexample

The notation dy/dx=(dy/du)(du/dx) always means ordinary numerical cancellation of du.

These symbols denote derivatives evaluated at the appropriate inputs. If an inner increment Δg equals 0, informal division by that increment is invalid. A remainder proof establishes the rule without that division.

Check your understanding

Should the outer derivative be evaluated at g′(a)?

Show answer and reasoning

No. Evaluate it at g(a).

The outer function receives the inner output g(a), not its rate of change.

When g′(a)=0, does the chain rule require division by zero?

Show answer and reasoning

No. Under its differentiability conditions, the derivative is the product f′(g(a))·0.

The rule multiplies rates. Its rigorous remainder proof does not divide by an inner increment.

Does differentiability of |x²| at 0 prove that |u| is differentiable at u=0?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

The composition equals x², but the outer function |u| has one-sided difference quotients −1 and 1 at 0.

Explore further

Set b=g(a) and Δg=g(a+h)−g(a). Differentiability of f at b gives f(b+k)−f(b)=f′(b)k+ε(k)k, where ε(k)→0 as k→0; define ε(0)=0. The composition difference quotient is [f′(b)+ε(Δg)]Δg/h. Continuity of g gives Δg→0, while Δg/h→g′(a) and is therefore bounded for small nonzero h. The error term tends to 0. This identity also holds when Δg=0, because it never divides by Δg. Thus the limit is f′(b)g′(a), provided both differentiability assumptions and the local domain condition hold. An undefined outer derivative cannot be replaced by a convenient value.

Subtopic 5.3

Higher Derivatives

Does differentiating again mean squaring the first derivative?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • The derivative f′ is itself a function on the points where the derivative exists.
  • f″(a) is the finite limit of the difference quotient of f′ at a, with f′ defined near a. It does not mean the square of f′(a).
  • In the fictional motion model, position is measured in metres and time in seconds; velocity has units m/s and acceleration m/s².

Learning goals

  • Distinguish repeated differentiation from powers, and check existence at each stage.
  • Interpret higher derivatives with the correct units.

Differentiate the derivative function

f″ is the derivative of f′, f‴ is the derivative of f″, and f⁽ⁿ⁾ means n successive differentiations, not fⁿ. The notation d²f/dx² is not an ordinary numerical fraction with squared quantities.

Before computing f″(a), check that f′ exists near a and is differentiable at a. Knowing the single number f′(a) is not enough, and a first derivative does not automatically guarantee a second.

Polynomials and a counterexample

For x⁴, successive derivatives are 4x³, 12x², 24x, 24, and 0; every later derivative is also 0. This process is valid for the polynomial, but cannot be applied to every differentiable function without checking existence.

For f(x)=x|x|, the difference quotient at 0 is |h|→0, and the whole first derivative is f′(x)=2|x|. At 0 the difference quotient of f′ has left limit −2 and right limit 2. Thus f′ is continuous, yet f″(0) does not exist.

Units and instantaneous motion

In the fictional model s(t)=t³−3t²+2t, the numerical time t is in seconds and the numerical position s is in metres. The coefficients carry units 1 m/s³, −3 m/s², and 2 m/s. These are assigned model values, not a fit to measured data.

Velocity is v(t)=3t²−6t+2 m/s and acceleration is a(t)=6t−6 m/s². At t=1, v=−1 and a=0. Zero acceleration at one instant does not imply zero velocity or constant velocity throughout an interval. Negative velocity specifies direction; speed is its absolute value.

A worked example, step by step

For f(x)=x⁴−2x³+x−5, find the first five derivatives. Evaluate the first three at 1 and compare f″(1) with [f′(1)]².

  1. Differentiate once: f′(x)=4x³−6x²+1. The constant −5 contributes 0.
  2. Differentiate again: f″(x)=12x²−12x and f‴(x)=24x−12.
  3. The fourth derivative is 24 and the fifth is 0. These are repeated derivatives, not powers of a derivative.
  4. At 1, f′(1)=−1, f″(1)=0, and f‴(1)=12. Meanwhile [f′(1)]²=1, which differs from f″(1).

The first five derivatives are 4x³−6x²+1, 12x²−12x, 24x−12, 24, and 0. The first three values at 1 are −1, 0, and 12.

Try it with paper and the lesson

For r(x)=x³−4x, find the first four derivatives and evaluate r′(0) and r″(0). In the fictional position model above, find velocity, speed, and acceleration at t=1.

Show hint

Differentiate the previous derivative at each step. Speed is the absolute value of velocity, not acceleration.

Show solution

The four derivatives of r are 3x²−4, 6x, 6, and 0. Thus r′(0)=−4 and r″(0)=0, whereas [r′(0)]²=16. In the motion model, velocity is −1 m/s, speed is 1 m/s, and acceleration is 0 m/s². These are model calculations, not physical measurements.

Misconception and counterexample

A function that can be differentiated once can always be differentiated indefinitely.

The function x|x| has the continuous first derivative 2|x|, but that derivative has a corner at 0. It is C¹ but not C².

Check your understanding

Does f⁽³⁾ mean f³?

Show answer and reasoning

No. It means the third derivative.

The superscript in parentheses counts successive differentiations; an ordinary third power multiplies function values.

Does acceleration 0 at an instant force velocity 0?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

In the assigned model at t=1, acceleration is 0 while velocity is −1 m/s.

What is the fifth derivative of a degree-four polynomial on ℝ?

Show answer and reasoning

It is identically 0.

After four differentiations the result is a constant; the next derivative and all later ones vanish.

Explore further

For an integer n≥1 and 0≤k≤n, the kth derivative of xⁿ is n!/(n−k)!·xⁿ⁻ᵏ. At k=0 this is the original function. If k<n, differentiating multiplies by n−k and lowers the power by one, giving the formula for k+1. At k=n the derivative is the constant n!, and the next derivative is 0. This finite polynomial argument does not automatically extend to noninteger powers without domain conditions, or guarantee higher derivatives for arbitrary functions. C¹ means that the first derivative exists and is continuous; C² requires derivatives through the second order to exist and be continuous. The example x|x| separates these two conditions.

Subtopic 5.4

Differentiating Piecewise Functions

If each branch is smooth, is their junction differentiable?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • First identify the actual value at the junction, including which branch contains the equality sign.
  • Continuity requires the limits to equal that actual value. Differentiability requires the two difference quotients to have the same finite limit.
  • A branch derivative is not automatically the derivative of the complete function at its junction.

Learning goals

  • Check continuity first, then compute both difference quotients using the actual junction value.
  • Test first and higher derivatives separately at a junction.

Start with the actual function value

Let F(x)=ax+b for x<2 and F(x)=x²+1 for x≥2. Its actual value is F(2)=5, so continuity requires 2a+b=5. Matching branch slopes without this value condition is insufficient.

Once continuity holds, the left difference quotient is a and the right difference quotient is [(2+h)²+1−5]/h=4+h. Equality of their limits requires a=4, and then b=−3. Checking only the right branch does not settle differentiability.

Equal branch slopes can hide a wrong value

Define J(x)=x for x≠0 and J(0)=1. Both nearby branch derivatives are 1, but the actual difference quotient at 0 is 1−1/h and has no finite two-sided limit. The mismatch in the function value matters first.

The function |x−1| is continuous at 1, but its left and right difference quotients are −1 and 1, so it is not differentiable there. Continuity is necessary but not sufficient. At an endpoint a one-sided question may be appropriate; at an interior junction the usual derivative requires both sides.

A first derivative need not yield a second

With a=4 and b=−3, F′ equals 4 to the left of 2, equals 4 at 2, and equals 2x to the right. Both sides approach 4, so the first derivative is continuous at the junction.

For its derivative at 2, the left difference quotient of F′ is 0 and the right one is [2(2+h)−4]/h=2. Thus F″(2) does not exist, even though both original branches can be differentiated indefinitely.

A worked example, step by step

Choose a and b so that F(x)=ax+b for x<2 and F(x)=x²+1 for x≥2 is differentiable at 2. Find F′(2), and determine whether F″(2) exists.

  1. The actual value is F(2)=5. Differentiability requires continuity, so 2a+b=5.
  2. Using that equality, the left difference quotient is a; the right one is 4+h and tends to 4.
  3. Equality gives a=4 and b=−3, and then F′(2)=4.
  4. For F′, use its actual junction value 4. Its difference quotients at 2 have left limit 0 and right limit 2. Hence F″(2) does not exist.

a=4 and b=−3; F′(2)=4. The second derivative at 2 does not exist.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Let G(x)=cx+d for x<1 and G(x)=x³ for x≥1. Choose c and d so that G is differentiable at 1. Find G′(1), then test the second derivative.

Show hint

The actual value is G(1)=1, so first require c+d=1. In the second-derivative test, subtract the actual value G′(1).

Show solution

The left first difference quotient is c, and the right one is 3+3h+h². Thus c=3 and d=−2, and G′(1)=3. The first derivative is 3 to the left and 3x² to the right. Its difference quotients at 1 are 0 on the left and 6+3h on the right, so G″(1) does not exist.

Misconception and counterexample

Matching branch derivatives always proves differentiability at the junction.

For J(x)=x when x≠0 and J(0)=1, nearby branch slopes match, but the actual difference quotient is 1−1/h. First check the value and continuity; then use the actual value in the difference quotient.

Check your understanding

If F(2)=5 but its left limit is 4, are matching branch slopes enough?

Show answer and reasoning

No. The function is not continuous at 2.

Differentiability implies continuity, so a value mismatch already prevents the derivative.

If F′(2)=4 and F′ is continuous there, must F″(2) exist?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

In this example the difference quotient of F′ has left limit 0 and right limit 2.

Does the equality sign in the right branch mean that only the right derivative matters?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

It determines the actual junction value. At an interior point the ordinary derivative still requires matching finite limits from both sides.

Explore further

At an interior junction a where both sides of the domain are accessible and F(a) is defined, suppose the left and right difference quotients have the same finite limit L. Given ε>0, choose δ₋ and δ₊ for the respective one-sided limits and put δ=min(δ₋,δ₊). Every h with 0<|h|<δ then makes the difference quotient differ from L by less than ε. Hence F′(a)=L. Conversely, a two-sided limit implies the same limit on each side. This is a necessary and sufficient test under the stated domain conditions. To test a second derivative, apply it to the actual function F′, including its actual junction value, rather than returning to F.
