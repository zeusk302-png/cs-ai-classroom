Subtopic 1.1

Domain, Range, and Graphs

Is a squaring calculator still a function if −2 and 2 give the same answer?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Arithmetic: use parentheses when squaring a negative number, as in (−2)²=4.
  • Coordinates: (x,y) represents a horizontal input x and a vertical output y.
  • Sets: [a,b] includes both endpoints, whereas (a,b) excludes them.

Learning goals

  • Check that a function assigns exactly one output to each input.
  • Distinguish domain, codomain, and range, and prove a claimed range in both directions.
  • Explain the limits of the information shown by a table or graph.

Exactly one output for each input

A function f:A→B is a rule that assigns exactly one element f(x) of B to every element x of A. A is the domain, and B is the codomain. Different inputs may have the same output. Sharing an output is different from one input having two outputs.

The range f(A)={f(x):x∈A} is the set of outputs actually reached. For f:ℝ→ℝ, f(x)=x², the codomain is ℝ but the range is [0,∞). A function is specified by its input and output sets as well as its formula.

Find the inputs allowed by a formula

The natural domain of a real-valued formula consists of all real inputs for which the formula gives a real value. A denominator must not be zero, and the quantity inside an even root must be nonnegative. If several conditions apply, they must hold simultaneously. If the input represents distance, negative inputs may be excluded from the model's domain even when they are algebraically allowed.

The natural domain of g(x)=√(x−1) is [1,∞). Its output is nonnegative, and for any y≥0, choosing x=y²+1 gives g(x)=y. This proves that the range is [0,∞). Finding upper and lower bounds on outputs through inequalities is only half of a range proof.

A graph is the set of all ordered pairs

The graph is {(x,f(x)):x∈A}. If one vertical line meets two distinct points, that x has two outputs, so the relation is not a function of x. The circle x²+y²=1 has y=1 and y=−1 at x=0, so it does not define y as a function of x.

If the domain is {0,1,2}, the graph consists of just three points. Joining them with lines introduces the additional assumption that intermediate inputs are also present. A finite table alone cannot determine the values of a function outside the table.

A worked example, step by step

Find the range of f:[−2,3]→ℝ, f(x)=(x−1)², and evaluate f(−2), f(1), and f(3).

  1. f(−2)=9, f(1)=0, and f(3)=4.
  2. If −2≤x≤3, then −3≤x−1≤2, so 0≤(x−1)²≤9.
  3. For every 0≤y≤9, choose x=1−√y. Then −2≤x≤1 and f(x)=y.

The domain is [−2,3], and the range is [0,9]. The maximum occurs at the left endpoint; the minimum occurs at the interior point x=1.

Try it with paper and the lesson

On paper, write the domain and range of h(x)=1/(x−2), and plot (0,h(0)), (1,h(1)), and (3,h(3)).

Show hint

Find the input that makes the denominator zero and the output for which the reverse construction x=2+1/y is unavailable.

Show solution

The inputs satisfy x≠2, with h(0)=−1/2, h(1)=−1, and h(3)=1. The output 0 is impossible. For any y≠0, x=2+1/y reaches y, so the range is ℝ∖{0}. These three points alone do not determine the whole graph.

Misconception and counterexample

For a function, different inputs must give different outputs.

For f(x)=x², f(−1)=f(1)=1, but each input still has exactly one output, so this is a function. Requiring different inputs to have different outputs is the additional property of being one-to-one.

Check your understanding

What are the codomain and range of f:ℝ→ℝ, f(x)=x²?

Show answer and reasoning

Codomain: ℝ. Range: [0,∞).

The codomain is the declared output set; the range consists of the square values actually reached.

What is the natural domain of √(5−x)?

Show answer and reasoning

(−∞,5].

The condition 5−x≥0 is necessary and sufficient.

Is the relation {(1,2),(1,3)} a function?

Show answer and reasoning

No.

Input 1 has two distinct outputs.

Explore further

Going further: redeclaring a function's range as its codomain makes the function onto. This declaration alone does not make it one-to-one. Compare the two properties using x²:ℝ→[0,∞).

Subtopic 1.2

Composite and Inverse Functions

What is needed to convert a temperature into a sensor reading and then recover the temperature?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Substitution in an expression: put the entire replacement expression in parentheses in place of x.
  • Linear equations: solve y=3x+2 as x=(y−2)/3.
  • Domain and range: check whether one function's output is an allowed input to the next function.

Learning goals

  • Calculate the order and domain of a composition.
  • Explain the existence of an inverse using one-to-one and onto conditions.
  • Check an inverse by composing in both directions.

Connect two rules

Applying g first and f next gives (f∘g)(x)=f(g(x)). The actual domain of the composition is {x∈Dom(g):g(x)∈Dom(f)}. If the entire codomain of g lies in the domain of f, composition is possible at every input of g. Otherwise, a composition can still be formed by restricting to the inputs for which it is possible.

The order generally cannot be reversed without changing the function. If f(x)=x² and g(x)=x+1, then f(g(x))=(x+1)², whereas g(f(x))=x²+1. At x=1, their outputs are 4 and 2, so these are different functions.

Recovery requires retaining the information

A function f is one-to-one if f(x₁)=f(x₂) implies x₁=x₂. An inverse must determine a unique original input from an output, so this condition is necessary. For f:A→B to have a two-sided inverse f⁻¹:B→A, it must also reach every element of B; it must therefore be onto as well.

If the inverse's domain is the range f(A) rather than the declared codomain, being one-to-one is sufficient. Squaring loses sign information on all of ℝ, but restricting its domain to [0,∞) allows recovery by taking the square root. The horizontal-line test—no horizontal line meets the graph at two distinct points—is a graphical expression of the one-to-one property.

Find and check an inverse formula

Solve y=f(x) for x and interchange the input and output roles, keeping track of the allowed sets. Check f⁻¹(f(x))=x for x∈A and f(f⁻¹(y))=y for y∈f(A). Agreement in just one direction at a particular point is neither a sufficient check nor a proof.

The notation f⁻¹ does not mean the reciprocal 1/f. An inverse function returns an input; a reciprocal is a multiplicative inverse of an output. For example, if f(x)=2x, then f⁻¹(y)=y/2 and 1/f(x)=1/(2x) are entirely different rules.

A worked example, step by step

A temperature T≥−5 is converted to a sensor reading s=g(T)=3T+15. A display shows h(s)=√s. Find the display function and the formula for recovering T.

  1. If T≥−5, then s≥0, so √s is defined. The display is w=(h∘g)(T)=√(3T+15), with range w≥0.
  2. Solving w²=3T+15 gives T=(w²−15)/3, retaining the condition w≥0.
  3. If T=7, then s=36 and w=6. Recovery gives (36−15)/3=7.
  4. For general T, recovery gives ((√(3T+15))²−15)/3=T. For w≥0, √(3((w²−15)/3)+15)=√w²=w.

The display is w=√(3T+15), for T≥−5. Its inverse is T=(w²−15)/3, for w≥0. Restricting the allowed values is essential to recovery.

Try it with paper and the lesson

For f(x)=√x and g(x)=x−4, find the formulas and domains of f∘g and g∘f, then compare them at x=9.

Show hint

First write the inner function's domain, then impose the outer function's input condition.

Show solution

f∘g=√(x−4), with domain [4,∞), gives √5 at x=9. g∘f=√x−4, with domain [0,∞), gives −1 at x=9. Both the formulas and the domains differ.

Misconception and counterexample

Solving y=x² gives x=±√y, so ±√y is the inverse function.

The ± expression can assign two outputs to one input, so it does not define a single inverse function on all nonnegative outputs. With the restriction x≥0, the inverse is √y; with x≤0, it is −√y. The two branches cannot be declared together as one inverse function.

Check your understanding

In f∘g, which function is applied first?

Show answer and reasoning

g.

In f(g(x)), calculate the inner expression g(x) first.

What is the inverse of f(x)=4x−7 with domain ℝ?

Show answer and reasoning

f⁻¹(y)=(y+7)/4.

Because 4 is nonzero, x can be solved uniquely for every output.

Does x²:[0,∞)→ℝ have a two-sided inverse on all of ℝ?

Show answer and reasoning

No. It has an inverse on its range [0,∞).

Negative outputs are not reached, so the function is not onto its declared codomain.

Explore further

Going further: if g:A→B and f:B→C are both bijections, then (f∘g)⁻¹=g⁻¹∘f⁻¹. The connecting set B must be declared the same for the composition and both inverses to work on their entire domains. Show that g⁻¹(f⁻¹(f(g(x))))=x, and explain why each intermediate value belongs to the required domain.

Subtopic 1.3

Polynomial, Rational, and Transcendental Functions

Does a quantity that increases by 2 each time belong to the same function family as one that doubles each time?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Powers: 2³=8, and x² means x multiplied by itself.
  • Fractions: division by a zero denominator is not defined.
  • Logarithm refresher: log₂8=3 means 2³=8.

Learning goals

  • Explain the relationships between polynomial, rational, algebraic, and transcendental functions.
  • Check the real-domain restrictions for each function family.
  • Explain why canceling polynomial factors does not restore an excluded input.

Polynomial and rational functions

A polynomial function is a finite sum aₙxⁿ+⋯+a₁x+a₀, with nonnegative integer exponents. If aₙ≠0, its degree is n. In this unit, the degree of the zero polynomial is left undefined. A polynomial can be evaluated at every real input. Neither x⁻¹ nor √x is a polynomial.

A rational function is a quotient of two polynomials, P(x)/Q(x), where Q must not be the zero polynomial. Its natural domain excludes the inputs where Q(x)=0. Every polynomial function can be written with denominator 1 and is therefore also rational. These classifications are not mutually exclusive boxes.

Algebraic and transcendental functions

An algebraic function satisfies a nonzero polynomial relation P(x,y)=0 between x and y. The function √x satisfies y²−x=0, but its real domain is x≥0 and it selects the branch y≥0. This is a broader classification than polynomial and rational functions.

A function not defined by such a polynomial relation is called transcendental; exponential, logarithmic, and trigonometric functions are basic examples. For a>0 and a≠1, aˣ is defined for every real x. The function logₐx is defined for x>0. The functions sin x and cos x are defined for all real x, whereas tan x is defined where cos x≠0. Trigonometric inputs in this course are in radians.

The rule and domain matter more than a name

The functions x² and 2ˣ both give 4 at x=2, but give 9 and 8 at x=3. A polynomial has fixed integer exponents; an exponential function has a fixed positive base and a variable exponent. Agreement at a few points does not make two functions the same.

The identity (x²−1)/(x−1)=x+1 holds only for x≠1. The original expression is undefined at x=1. Allowing that input in the canceled expression produces a different, extended function. In the unit on limits, we will examine why behavior near a missing point and the actual value at that point provide different information.

A worked example, step by step

Find the natural domain of r(x)=(x²−9)/(x−3), evaluate r(2) and r(4), and decide whether r is the same function as p(x)=x+3.

  1. Exclude x=3, where the denominator is zero, so Dom(r)=ℝ∖{3}.
  2. Since x²−9=(x−3)(x+3), r(x)=x+3 for x≠3.
  3. r(2)=(4−9)/(2−3)=5, and r(4)=(16−9)/(4−3)=7.
  4. p(3)=6 is defined, but r(3) is not. Equal functions must have the same domain as well as the same values, so these are different functions as a whole.

The domain is ℝ∖{3}, and the two values are 5 and 7. The graph is the line y=x+3 with the point (3,6) removed.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Classify x²+1, (x+1)/(x²−4), √(x+2), and log₂(x−1), and write their natural domains.

Show hint

For the first two expressions, check the polynomial and rational definitions. For the last two, check the square-root and logarithm input conditions separately.

Show solution

x²+1 is polynomial, rational, and algebraic, with domain ℝ. The quotient is rational and algebraic, with domain ℝ∖{−2,2}. √(x+2) is algebraic, with domain [−2,∞). log₂(x−1) is transcendental, with domain (1,∞).

Misconception and counterexample

A transcendental function always has irrational values.

The classification concerns the relation defining the function. The function 2ˣ is transcendental, but 2⁰=1 and 2³=8 are integers. The function √x is algebraic, but √2 is irrational. One value at one input does not determine a function's family.

Check your understanding

What are the degree and domain of x³−2x+1?

Show answer and reasoning

Degree 3; domain ℝ.

The highest exponent with a nonzero coefficient is 3, and there are no denominator or root restrictions.

What is the real domain of 1/(x²+1)?

Show answer and reasoning

ℝ.

Since x²+1≥1, the denominator is never zero.

Is ln(x²) defined for every real x?

Show answer and reasoning

It is defined only for x≠0.

A logarithm requires a positive input, and x²>0 is equivalent to x≠0.

Explore further

Going further: y=√x and y=−√x both satisfy y²=x. Show why a polynomial relation does not automatically specify a unique function, and explain why the branch and the domain must be chosen.

Subtopic 1.4

Units and Assumptions in Function Models

What units must be specified for a distance input of 5 and a fare output of 17 to have meaning?

Preparation and learning goals

Preparation

  • Proportionality: at 3 per kilometer, 2 kilometers contributes 6.
  • Unit conversion: 1 km=1000 m, and 1 hour=60 minutes.
  • A linear expression: in b+ax, b is the starting value and a is the change per unit of input.

Learning goals

  • Infer the units of coefficients and check the dimensions on both sides of an equation.
  • Restrict a model's domain using the assumptions of the situation.
  • Distinguish model predictions, measured facts, and extrapolation.

The meaning attached to a number

A physical quantity has a numerical value and a unit. If distance d is measured in km and fare C in thousand Korean won, then in C(d)=2+3d, the constant 2 has units of thousand won and the coefficient 3 has units of thousand won/km. Terms added together must have the same dimension.

A numerical formula depends on the choice of units. Let m denote the numerical distance measured in meters. Then d=m/1000, so C=2+0.003m. Although the coefficient changes, the formula predicts the same fare. Omitting units can make an incorrect calculation look numerically plausible.

The assumptions that make a model apply

Here we assume a constant base fare and a constant rate per unit distance, with no distance rounding, surcharges, fare cap, or time charge. We use the formula for a continuous real-valued distance d≥0, but a real device might increase its charge at every 100 m. That would require a step-function model.

A model is a rule chosen for a purpose. Being able to draw a line through two measured points does not prove that fares are linear at every distance. Using the model beyond the interval where data were observed is extrapolation and requires new validation.

Dimensional checks and validation have different roles

Adding distance d and time t as d+t is normally dimensionally inconsistent. Multiplying by a speed v makes vt a length, allowing d+vt. Inputs to functions such as exponentials and logarithms are usually made into dimensionless ratios. In a decay model e^(−t/τ), t and τ must use the same time unit.

Dimensional consistency does not establish that a model is correct in reality. The fare formulas 2+3d and 2+4d can both have consistent dimensions, but choosing between them requires comparison with fare data. Arithmetic checks, unit checks, and real-world validation answer different questions.

A worked example, step by step

Assume a fictional travel service uses C(d)=2+3d, in thousand Korean won, for distances from 0 to 10 km. Find the fare for 1500 m and the average speed if the trip takes 20 minutes.

  1. 1500 m=1.5 km, which lies within the model's interval from 0 to 10 km.
  2. C(1.5)=2+3×1.5=6.5 thousand won=6500 won.
  3. 20 minutes=1/3 hour, so average speed=1.5/(1/3)=4.5 km/hour.
  4. Using the meter-based formula gives 2+0.003×1500=6.5 thousand won, agreeing with the first calculation. Time is not an input to the stated fare model, so do not insert the speed into the fare formula.

The fare is 6500 Korean won, and the average speed is 4.5 km/hour. These are calculated results of a fictional model, not observations of an actual service's fares.

Try it with paper and the lesson

Write s(t)=5+2t on paper, where s is measured in m and t in seconds. State the units of both coefficients and find the position at t=4 seconds. Then rewrite the formula using time u measured in minutes.

Show hint

The numerical time in seconds is t=60u, and the starting position does not change.

Show solution

The units of 5 are m; the units of 2 are m/second. s(4)=13 m. Since t=60u, s=5+120u, and the units of 120 are m/minute. Substituting u=1/15 minute gives 5+8=13 m.

Misconception and counterexample

A model is completely proved if its units are consistent and it fits two measured points.

The models s(t)=5 m+(2 m/second)t and s(t)=5 m+(2 m/second)t+k t(t−1 second) both have consistent units when k has units of m/second², and they agree at t=0 seconds and t=1 second. Both terms in the subtraction t−1 second must also have the same unit. For nonzero k, their predictions differ at other times, so two points do not determine the model.

Check your understanding

In C(d)=2+3d, if C is measured in won and d in m, what are the units of 3?

Show answer and reasoning

Won/m.

Multiplying it by distance must produce fare units.

What is the average speed for 6 km traveled in 30 minutes?

Show answer and reasoning

12 km/hour.

30 minutes=0.5 hour, and 6/0.5=12.

Is a prediction at 100 km validated by a formula checked only over 0–10 km?

Show answer and reasoning

No. It is extrapolation.

The formula can be evaluated, but its assumptions outside the application interval have not been checked.

Explore further

Going further: suppose a fictional rule charges a base fare of 2 thousand won, adds nothing for the first 2 km, and then adds 3 thousand won per kilometer. Write it as C(d)=2 for 0≤d≤2 and C(d)=2+3(d−2) for d>2, with C in thousand won. Check that the two formulas give the same boundary value at d=2, where the fare is 2 thousand won. Discuss how this continuous model would need to change if the actual fare used distance rounding.
